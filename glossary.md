# Glossary

[README](README.md) · [Overview](summary/L0-overview.md) · [Parts](summary/L1-parts.md) · [Debates](debates.md)

Anscombe's technical terms, and a few commentators' terms that are standard in the literature (marked *commentators'*). Each entry gives the § where the term is introduced or chiefly developed, with a link to that section's L3 file, and a short definition. The definitions report Anscombe's usage. Where a definition is contested, the entry points to [debates.md](debates.md). Entries are alphabetical, ignoring quotation marks.

| Term | § | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **A–D order** | [§26](summary/L3-sections/§26.md) (set up [§23](summary/L3-sections/§23.md)) | The ordered series of descriptions of one intentional action, e.g. moving his arm (A) → operating the pump (B) → replenishing the water supply (C) → poisoning the inhabitants (D). Each term gives an intention with which the act described by the previous term is done. The last term is the intention of the act described by the first or any intermediate term. One action, many descriptions. |
| **at a distance** | [§41](summary/L3-sections/§41.md) | The mark of practical reasoning: the thing wanted lies beyond the particular action, so the action has to be reached by calculation. |
| **backward-looking motive** | [§13](summary/L3-sections/§13.md) | A motive that cites something in the past as the ground of the action, e.g. revenge ("he killed my brother"). It involves the ideas of good and harm ([§14](summary/L3-sections/§14.md)), which distinguishes it from a mental cause. Not an intention for the future. |
| ***bonum est multiplex*** | [§39](summary/L3-sections/§39.md) | "The good is manifold." Needing a desirability characterisation does not make any particular one compelling: there are many ways for something to be desirable. |
| **break (in the series)** | [§§23–26](summary/L2-sections/G08-s22-27-intention-with-which-and-the-a-d-order.md) | *Commentators' (Holton).* The point at which the descriptions of what an agent does stop being descriptions of intentional action: they are not known, the answer is "I didn't care", or success is not taken for granted. |
| **calculation** | [§§33–35](summary/L2-sections/G11-s33-35-practical-syllogism.md) | Working out means towards something wanted. The paradigm of practical reasoning as Anscombe treats it. |
| **cause of what it understands** | [§48](summary/L3-sections/§48.md) | The ancient and medieval formula Anscombe adopts for practical knowledge, as opposed to speculative knowledge, which is derived from its objects. Whether the causation is formal, efficient or both is disputed ([debates §2](debates.md#2-practical-knowledge-as-the-cause-of-what-it-understands-48-and-failure-45)). Commentators trace the formula to Aquinas (*ST* I q.14; I-II q.3 a.5 obj.1). |
| **contradiction of an intention** | [§31](summary/L3-sections/§31.md) | What contradicts "I am doing X" when it expresses intention is not a contrary fact but an opposing intention, as when someone replies "Oh no you aren't". This is part of the order analogy that §31 tries and finds inadequate. |
| **desirability characterisation** | [§37](summary/L3-sections/§37.md) | A description under which what is wanted is seen as desirable, e.g. pleasant, befitting, healthy. Once one is given, the question "What for?" comes to an end ([§38](summary/L3-sections/§38.md)). Without one, a claim to want something is unintelligible. |
| **difference of form** | [§33](summary/L3-sections/§33.md) (1963 addition) | Practical and theoretical syllogisms differ in form, not in subject matter: in how the conclusion relates to the premises. See [Sandis](sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md). |
| **direction of fit** | — | *Commentators'.* Not Anscombe's phrase. A later gloss (associated with Searle) on the §32 shopping list: the list must fit the world for belief, the world must fit the list for desire or intention. Whether this captures §32 is disputed ([debates §6](debates.md#6-cognitivism-is-intending-a-kind-of-believing)). |
| **evidence (sense of "Why?")** | [§9](summary/L3-sections/§09.md) | The sense of "Why?" that asks for grounds for believing something. Answering in this sense does not give a reason for acting. |
| **expression of intention (for the future)** | [§1](summary/L3-sections/§01.md), [§§2–3](summary/L2-sections/G02-s02-04-intention-for-the-future.md) | A statement about the future, typically "I am going to…". It is distinguished from an estimate of the future by the kind of justification, if any, given for it ([§3](summary/L3-sections/§03.md)). The first of the three heads, returned to in [§§50–52](summary/L2-sections/G17-s50-52-intention-for-the-future-revisited.md). Moran & Stone distinguish a special (future) use and a general use *(reported)*. |
| **extra feature** | [§19](summary/L3-sections/§19.md) | The rejected idea that being intentional is some additional item, such as an inner act or feeling, that accompanies a movement at the time it is done. |
| **form of description of events** | [§47](summary/L3-sections/§47.md) | What "intentional" relates to. It does not name a property of an event. It belongs to a form of describing events that gives the question "Why?" application. |
| **formally descriptions of executed intentions** | [§48](summary/L3-sections/§48.md) | Descriptions of what humans bring about that can apply only to intentional doings, e.g. paying, hiring, building, as opposed to descriptions under which something might also happen unintentionally. |
| **"How?"** | [§26](summary/L3-sections/§26.md) | *Commentators' gloss (Small).* The question that runs the A–D order in reverse, from end to means, where "Why?" runs from means to end. |
| **idle practical syllogism** | [§33](summary/L3-sections/§33.md) (1963 addition) | A practical syllogism set out only as an example, with no one actually acting on it. Contrasted with a practical syllogism "proper", whose conclusion is an action. |
| **"I do what happens"** | [§29](summary/L3-sections/§29.md) | Anscombe's formula for the problem of §29: the object of the agent's knowledge is the happening in the world, not an inner act. |
| **incorrigibly contemplative conception of knowledge** | [§32](summary/L3-sections/§32.md) | The modern assumption that all knowledge is knowledge of something that is already the case independently of the knowing, and is assessed by its accordance with it. Anscombe says this conception blocks understanding of practical knowledge. |
| **intention in acting / intention with which** | [§1](summary/L3-sections/§01.md), [§22](summary/L3-sections/§22.md) | The further intention with which something is done ("I'm doing P in order that Q"). The test (§22): the agent cannot accept "But if P, Q won't happen" while keeping that intention. The third head of §1. |
| **intentional action** | [§5](summary/L3-sections/§05.md), [§18](summary/L3-sections/§18.md) | An action to which the special sense of "Why?" applies. The class is fixed by the range of answers the question expects (§18). |
| **interior act of intention** | [§27](summary/L3-sections/§27.md) | A supposed inner act that would settle what is really being done. Anscombe denies that it could determine what kind of action is going on. |
| **interpretative motive / motive-in-general** | [§13](summary/L3-sections/§13.md) | A motive such as "out of friendship" or "from curiosity", which presents the action in a certain light rather than citing a past event or a future aim. |
| **involuntary** | [§7](summary/L3-sections/§07.md); [§8](summary/L3-sections/§08.md) | One ground for refusing "Why?". It cannot be used as a criterion without presupposing the account under construction (§7). §8 defines one class of involuntary movements without circularity: movements known without observation where there is no room for non-observational knowledge of their cause, such as the jerk on falling asleep. |
| **knowing one's way about** | [§48](summary/L3-sections/§48.md) | The practical knowledge that intentional action requires: a general competence, not only a specialised skill. Contrasted by commentators (Small) with mere true opinion about means ([§28](summary/L3-sections/§28.md)). |
| **knowledge "in intention"** | [§32](summary/L3-sections/§32.md) | *Commentators' phrase (Campbell, Small),* drawing on Anscombe. The agent knows what they are doing by intending and doing it, not by observing it. |
| **knowledge without observation** (non-observational knowledge) | [§8](summary/L3-sections/§08.md), [§28](summary/L3-sections/§28.md) | Knowledge not based on observation or on separately describable sensations: of the position of one's limbs, of the cause of a start, of what one is intentionally doing (§28). How central it is, and what its object is, are disputed ([debates §1](debates.md#1-knowledge-without-observation-what-is-known-and-how-central-is-it)). |
| **mad account** | [§29](summary/L3-sections/§29.md) | *As commentators label it.* The rejected view that whatever I think I am doing is what I am really doing. |
| **mental cause / mental causality** | [§10](summary/L3-sections/§10.md) | A cause the agent knows without observation, e.g. what made one jump or start. It is not a reason for acting, and it is distinct from motives, objects of feelings ([§10](summary/L3-sections/§10.md)) and intentions with which ([§11](summary/L3-sections/§11.md)). Anscombe says it is not important in itself. |
| **mistake in performance (not in judgement)** | [§32](summary/L3-sections/§32.md); anticipated [§2](summary/L3-sections/§02.md), recalled [§45](summary/L3-sections/§45.md) | When what is done fails to match an intention, the fault lies in the performance, not in the statement of intention. Anscombe links this to Theophrastus; where it comes from (*Magna Moralia*) is discussed in [§32](summary/L3-sections/§32.md). |
| **motive** | [§12](summary/L3-sections/§12.md) | A wider notion than intention with which. A motive is not a cause at all. |
| **"no particular reason", "For"** | [§17](summary/L3-sections/§17.md) | A nil answer that *accepts* the "Why?" question rather than refusing it, like answering "None" to "How much money have you got?". It cannot be the only kind of answer there is ([§20](summary/L3-sections/§20.md)). |
| **order (of practical reasoning)** | [§42](summary/L3-sections/§42.md) | What setting out a practical syllogism in full describes: not a psychological process but the same means–end order as the A–D series of §26. |
| **practical knowledge** | [§32](summary/L3-sections/§32.md) (raised), [§45](summary/L3-sections/§45.md) (model), [§48](summary/L3-sections/§48.md) (account) | Knowledge of what is done that is not derived from what is done: "the cause of what it understands". Modelled on the man directing a project he cannot see (§45). Its object and its factivity are disputed ([debates §2](debates.md#2-practical-knowledge-as-the-cause-of-what-it-understands-48-and-failure-45)). |
| **practical syllogism / practical reasoning** | [§33](summary/L3-sections/§33.md) | Aristotle's form of reasoning, whose starting point is something wanted ([§34](summary/L3-sections/§34.md)) and whose conclusion is an action. It is not a demonstration of what one ought to do, and modern philosophy has misunderstood it. |
| **prediction (wide sense)** | [§2](summary/L3-sections/§02.md) | Any description of the future, defined broadly enough to include estimates, orders/imperatives and expressions of intention. |
| **range of answers** | [§18](summary/L3-sections/§18.md) (summary [§16](summary/L3-sections/§16.md)) | The answers the special "Why?" expects: something future (intention with which), an interpretation (motive-in-general), something past (backward-looking motive), or "no particular reason". This range defines the class of intentional actions. |
| **reason for acting** | [§5](summary/L3-sections/§05.md) | What a positive answer to the special "Why?" gives. The concept cannot be used to define intentional action without circularity, which is why §§5–18 proceed through the question and its refusals. |
| **speculative knowledge** | [§48](summary/L3-sections/§48.md) | Knowledge derived from the objects known. Contrasted with practical knowledge. |
| **three heads** | [§1](summary/L3-sections/§01.md) | The three contexts in which "intention" is used: expression of intention for the future, intentional action, and intention in acting (intention with which). How they are unified is disputed ([debates §5](debates.md#5-intention-for-the-future-and-the-unity-of-the-three-heads)). |
| **under a description** | [§6](summary/L3-sections/§06.md) | One and the same action has many descriptions. It is intentional under some (those under which the agent knows it and "Why?" applies) and not under others. |
| **vital descriptions** | [§47](summary/L3-sections/§47.md) | *As Holton reports it.* Descriptions beyond physics that apply to the doings of living things whether or not they are intentional. Contrasted with essentially intentional verbs. Check the term against p. 85. |
| **voluntary** | [§49](summary/L3-sections/§49.md) (set aside at [§7](summary/L3-sections/§07.md)) | Accounted for only after intentional action has been explained. The class is wider than the intentional, under four headings ([§49](summary/L3-sections/§49.md), as quoted by Narboux): (1) mere physical movements done "fiddling", or not attended to, are voluntary rather than intentional; (2) known side-effects one could have prevented by giving up the action are voluntary but not intentional; (3) what is not one's own doing can be voluntary when one consents to it (the punt); (4) everything intentional is voluntary. From another point of view, regretted side-effects and reluctant intentional acts can be called involuntary. |
| **wanting** | [§§35–36](summary/L2-sections/G12-s36-40-wanting-and-desirability.md) | In the sense relevant to action, its primitive sign is *trying to get* (§36). What can be wanted is limited: not every describable object or state of affairs. "I want" does not belong among the premises of a practical syllogism (§35), though the first premise mentions something wanted. |
| **wanting : good :: judging : true** | [§40](summary/L3-sections/§40.md) | Good is to wanting as truth is to judgement: the formal object it aims at. Anscombe compares the two relations and also notes the problems with the comparison. |
| **"Why?" (the special sense)** | [§5](summary/L3-sections/§05.md) | The question whose application marks an action as intentional; a positive answer gives a reason for acting. It is refused application by "I didn't know I was doing that" ([§6](summary/L3-sections/§06.md)), by involuntariness ([§7](summary/L3-sections/§07.md)), and by knowledge only by observation ([§8](summary/L3-sections/§08.md)). |
