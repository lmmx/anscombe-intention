# On Anscombe on Practical Knowledge and Practical Truth — Lucy Campbell

## What this source is
A handbook chapter on how *practical knowledge* (*Intention*) relates to *practical truth*. Practical truth comes from Aristotle, *NE* VI.2, as Anscombe discusses it in "Thought and Action in Aristotle" (1965) and "Practical Truth" (1993/2005).

- The "Simple View" says practical knowledge is always knowledge of practical truth. It fails, because one has practical knowledge of *all* one's intentional actions (vicious ones included), while practical truth (truth "in agreement with right desire") is manifested only in acting well.
- Campbell's solution, the **Two-Level Simple View**, distinguishes:
  - a thin action-theoretical notion and a thick ethical notion of practical *truth*;
  - the same two levels of practical *knowledge*.

The first two sections give a compact, careful exposition of practical knowledge in *Intention*.

## Account of Intention
- **The knowledge condition.** Not knowing that one is doing something under a description is generally sufficient for not doing it intentionally under that description (I, 11, §6). By contraposition, doing something intentionally requires knowing that one is doing it. Campbell notes Davidson's carbon-copier as the classic objection, and Thompson 2011 and Wolfson 2012 as replies.
- **Two formal marks that distinguish practical from theoretical ("contemplative", I 57; "speculative", I 87) knowledge:**
  1. Practical knowledge is undermined by a mistake in *performance*, not in judgement (I, 57, §32). Campbell's example: Alexander eating the wrong person's chips. She adds that Anscombe needn't deny that a mistaken judgement also occurs (I, 88–89).
  2. It is "the cause of what it understands" (I, 87, §48). Campbell reads this as *formal* causation: the agent's knowledge "gives the descriptions under which what is going on is the execution of an intention" (I, 87). Without it, what happens "does not come under the description—execution of intentions" (I, 88). By contrast, theoretical knowledge is "derived from the objects known" and accidental to its object.
- **Knowledge "in intention" (I, 57).** One knows what one is doing simply *in or by* having and executing one's intentions. This explains both formal marks. Practical knowledge is then "an epistemic aspect of intentional action itself", not a separable stance.
- **Non-observation is not the defining feature.** Campbell treats non-observational and non-inferential character as *consequences* of knowledge in intention, not as definitive. Anscombe recognises non-observational knowledge that is not practical, such as limb position (I, 13, §8).
- **The "Why?" and expression.** Answering Anscombe's special "What?" and "Why?" questions just *is* expressing one's intentions (§2 et passim). Such answers invite further "Why?" questions, not requests for evidence.
- **Practical reasoning.** "Practical reasoning" names an abstract *order* characterising the structure of action, elicited by "Why?". It is not a prior mental process (I, 57–58; I, 79 on the "absurd appearance" of syllogisms set out in full, §42). Universally quantified "major premises" are generally "insane" (I, 61–62, §§33–34).
- **Avoiding ethics.** In *Intention* Anscombe deliberately avoids ethics (I, §§39, 41).

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- **Practical truth(A).** A person makes true descriptions of her action that are fixed by the content of the intentions she executes. All intentional action manifests it.
- **Practical truth(E).** Practical truth(A) where the ultimate description made true is "acting/living well".
- **Practical knowledge(A).** Knowing what one is doing and why.
- **Practical knowledge(E).** Also knowing that one is acting well, and so knowing what living well involves.
- **Anscombe's commitment to the thin notion.** Campbell argues Anscombe is committed to practical truth(A) despite the "Difficult Passage" at the end of "Practical Truth", which calls a thin notion "trivial and pointless". Her evidence: the contract-signing example; climbing on the table to "make it true" ("Practical Inference"); and an argument by analogy from "man" as a "special kind of cause".
- **Against "direction of fit" readings.** In the companion *Mind* paper (2024), Campbell warns against reading Anscombe as giving practical knowledge a "reverse direction of fit".

## Positions on key debates
- **Knowledge without observation:** Accepted as a feature of practical knowledge, but explained by knowledge "in intention" rather than used to define it.
- **§48:** Formal causation. Practical knowledge is internal and essential to its object.
- **Davidson / causalism:** Davidson's carbon-copier and "restaurant on daughter's birthday" cases are noted. The latter shows that doing what one intends is not the same as executing the intention. Campbell gives Anscombe's answer as practical knowledge (I, 88).
- **Naive action theory:** Thompson 2011 is cited as one way to resist Davidson. Otherwise not discussed.
- **Intention for the future:** Noted (n. 3): the same capacity yields knowledge of what one will do when one has decided. Set aside for reasons of space.
- **Cognitivism:** Not addressed here. Campbell 2018 ("An Epistemology for Practical Knowledge") calls theoretical knowledge knowledge "in belief" by contrast.
- **"Why?":** Answering it is expressing intention. The practical syllogism is the order elicited by "Why?".
