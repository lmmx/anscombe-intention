# Essays on Anscombe's Intention — Anton Ford, Jennifer Hornsby & Frederick Stoutland (eds.)

## What this source is

A collection of ten essays plus an editorial Introduction and a ten-page Summary of *Intention* by Frederick Stoutland, arising (per catalogue records) from a September 2008 Uppsala conference and an April 2009 Lipkind conference at the University of Chicago for the book's 50th anniversary. Clark (NDPR) calls it the mark of "a new stage in the reception of Anscombe's thought". Teichmann (Analysis, extract) notes that four of the ten essays deal directly with practical knowledge.

**Access caveat.** I have read no part of the volume. Everything below is reported from Clark's NDPR review [Clark], Piedrahita's *Ideas y Valores* review in Spanish [Piedrahita], and the first page of Teichmann's *Analysis* review [Teichmann-rev]. Page numbers in parentheses are the volume's pages as cited by those reviewers.

## Structure (chapter → §§ of Intention)

Volume order (from catalogue/reviews): Stoutland Introduction (1–22); Stoutland Summary (23–32); 1 Moran & Stone (33–75); 2 Ford; 3 Hornsby; 4 McDowell; 5 Haddock; 6 Setiya (170–197); 7 Thompson (198–210); 8 Rödl; 9 Müller; 10 Laurence. (Chapter numbers 2–4 from a catalogue listing; Thompson is ch. 7 per De Gruyter's chapter DOI suffix .c8, which counts the Introduction.)

**Stoutland's Summary** — Clark: "Against all odds, he manages to tell the story of Intention in a mere ten pages, so that each of the contributors picks up where some part of the summary leaves off." Piedrahita calls it quite useful for any reader. **Its internal division of Intention is not accessible to me.** The Introduction (per Piedrahita's quotation, p. 4) narrates the Truman degree protest — Anscombe heard Truman defended on the grounds that he merely signed a paper and that his only intention was to end the war — as the occasion of the lectures that became *Intention*.

The § mapping in the front matter is mine, inferred from the reviewers' descriptions; it is not the editors'.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation, attributed)

**Overall thesis of the volume (Clark).** For decades readers saw Anscombe's action theory "largely through a Davidsonian lens" — a selective, more accessible, less Wittgensteinian reconstruction that encouraged hopes of absorbing her insights within "a comfortable causalism about the mental". The volume shows those days are over. What was missed: perhaps how far Anscombe departs from the orthodox view of intention as a mental state that efficiently causes behaviour — on a process reading, intending to act is already being in the process of acting (a view Michael Thompson develops in *Life and Action* without attributing it to Anscombe there) [Clark].

**Moran & Stone (expression of intention).** Against "connectivist" strategies (linking a future-directed state of intending to action causally, or behaviouristically), Anscombe offers an "immediate elucidation" that "exhibits the divisions of intention as inflections of a single form" (37). "Expression of intention" has a special use (= for the future) and a general use covering the genus of answers to "Why?" ("I'm buying some shoes" expresses intention in action). The general use covers all three divisions of §1; what unifies them is that in each there is a "Why?" question answerable by an expression of intention: "An intention is whatever can be given to another in an expression suited to play this role" of fitting what is going on into a "whole-in-progress" (55) [Clark]. Piedrahita adds that they list features distinguishing linguistic expressions of intention from behavioural manifestations, explaining why Anscombe denies there is a natural, non-verbal expression of intention [Piedrahita].
- *Clark's objection:* pure intending that is never acted on involves nothing "going on" to explain; and Moran & Stone omit the end of the passage they quote on the equivalence of the three divisions ("asked when I go upstairs"), which may restrict the equivalence to cases where something is being done to carry the intention out [Clark].

**Ford (action and generality).** Reads §19's denial of an "extra feature" as rejecting the "standard approach" (genus + differentia, two-step explanation). Distinguishes accidental species (snub nose), categorial species (horse; red as species of colour) and essential species (pure gold). Action stands to intentional action as gold to pure gold (nothing extra must be added); event stands to action as colour to red (no independent differentia). So the standard approach is doubly mistaken [Clark]. Clark: Ford "really does succeed ... in explaining how there could fail to be an 'extra feature'", though the argument about horse is disputable (prime numbers counterexample) [Clark]. Piedrahita: the standard method makes the object of action theory an accidental category, contrary to Anscombe [Piedrahita].

**Hornsby (actions in their circumstances).** Three ways of relating action and causation: agent-causation (agent causes action); Davidsonian (causation only between events/states); and Anscombe's — an action *is* the agent's causing something, "agents have powers to affect things" (125). Davidson must treat a person's moving her body as just her body's moving (113), an ad hoc exception for transitive verbs. Differences extend to individuation of action; both favour Anscombe. The reason-vs-cause framing of the Anscombe/Davidson dispute misses this deeper disagreement [Clark][Piedrahita]. Piedrahita: Hornsby challenges the near-consensus that actions are caused by mental states and events; for Anscombe action is something one does by doing something else [Piedrahita].

**McDowell (bodily self-knowledge).** What does "known without observation" mean for limb position, given that ordinary perception is also non-inferential? Reconstruction: perceptual knowledge comes by impressions of secondary qualities; bodily self-knowledge involves no such experience. Bodily self-knowledge is neither practical (not "the cause of what it understands") nor receptive (knowledge of oneself as self) [Clark]. Piedrahita: McDowell denies Anscombe used limb-knowledge to explain knowledge of intentional action; "what is known is the self-conscious bodily being who is the knower" (142); Piedrahita finds little else in it illuminating for action theory [Piedrahita].

**Haddock (knowledge of intentional action).** What distinguishes practical knowledge within knowledge-without-observation? It is a "simple consequence of the actuality of its objects" (162): nothing beyond doing A intentionally is required to know one is doing it, unlike limb position, where a capacity to tell is needed. Helps with the commands analogy, the abrupt turn to practical reasoning, and the suggestion that practical knowledge would be the same even if the intended thing did not happen [Clark]. Piedrahita: clear and thorough, but neglects the opacity of intentional action (intentional under a description) [Piedrahita].

**Setiya (knowledge of intention).** Defends a cognitivist explanation of the "Anscombe Principle" — if A can act for reasons, A can know what he is doing without observation or inference (174) — via an Evans-style transparency/"redeployment" argument; non-cognitivists cannot explain why "Self-blindness" is impossible [Clark][Piedrahita]. Setiya, unlike Anscombe, denies that practical knowledge is necessary for intentional action (Davidson's carbon-copier) [Clark]. Clark and Piedrahita both doubt the argument's key premises.

**Thompson (practical knowledge).** Answers Davidson's carbon-copier by focusing on the progressive: in the ordinary case one checks and repeats, and "is all along ... making ten copies ... and he knows it" (210); Davidson's single-shot case is like buying a lottery ticket and does not illustrate Anscombe's topic [Clark]. The content of practical knowledge is progressive and imperfective; there is practical knowledge precisely when the thing is not yet done (209); Davidson's perfective, event-based descriptions miss this [Piedrahita]. Clark's counterexample: "Press hard, you are making ten copies" — intentional, no checking, not a lottery.

**Rödl (two forms of practical knowledge).** Reconciles Anscombe's practical knowledge (of what one is doing, something actual) with Kant's (formal; of what one ought to do): two aspects of one power — the will as a power to act through reasoning; Anscombe as "recovering Kant for modern philosophy" [Clark]. Piedrahita objects that the will, as a substantive power, is not an Anscombean notion in *Intention* [Piedrahita].

**Müller (backward-looking rationality).** Anscombe distinguishes forward-looking reasons (further intentions) from backward-looking ones (revenge, gratitude; §§13–14) but refuses to count acting on the latter as practical reasoning, modelling reasoning on Aristotle's syllogism from something wanted; Müller thinks this a mistake (245n5, 249) and argues backward-looking responders also "calculate" (255) [Clark][Piedrahita]. Piedrahita replies that *Intention* never aimed to cover all practical rationality via the syllogism: backward-looking reasons are reasons for acting (they delimit intentional action) without being intentions [Piedrahita].

**Laurence (collective action).** A distinct collective sense of "Why?" — "We're pushing it to the gas station" — asks for an action of a group of which the agent's act is a part or phase; agents act as a group when this question applies [Clark]. Piedrahita: a non-individualist, non-psychologistic teleological account; open question whether collective action is a process [Piedrahita].

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

1. Anscombe is **not** a proto-Davidsonian whose quarrel was merely "reasons are not causes" (Hornsby; editors' framing via Clark).
2. **Intention as process / whole-in-progress** rather than a state (Moran & Stone; Thompson).
3. **No "extra feature"** reconstructed with a logic of non-accidental species (Ford).
4. **Practical knowledge** is the centre of gravity (four essays: Haddock, Setiya, Thompson, Rödl [Teichmann-rev]), with McDowell on the adjacent non-observational bodily case.
5. Internal disagreement: Setiya (knowledge not necessary; cognitivism) vs Thompson and Haddock (knowledge internal to intentional action).

## Section pointers (§N — what the source says)

- §1 — three divisions; Moran & Stone unify them via the general sense of "expression of intention" [Clark].
- §§2–4, §§50–52 — expression of intention for the future; the never-acted-on intention is Clark's problem case for Moran & Stone.
- §8 — limb position known without observation (McDowell) [Clark][Piedrahita].
- §§13–14 — backward-looking motives (Müller) [Clark][Piedrahita].
- §19 — no "extra feature" (Ford) [Clark][Piedrahita].
- §§22–26 — nested means–end descriptions (Moran & Stone "nested chain"; Laurence's collective "Why?") [Clark].
- §§23–26 / §§29–30 — individuation and bodily movement vs action (Hornsby) [Clark].
- §§28–32, §§45–48 — practical knowledge (Haddock, Setiya, Thompson, Rödl) [Clark][Piedrahita][Teichmann-rev].
- §§33–41 — practical syllogism from something wanted (Müller; Rödl) [Clark].
- Clark quotes Anscombe on the three divisions being "to a certain extent ... simply equivalent" where answers like "I am going to fetch my camera" / "I am fetching my camera" / "in order to fetch the camera" are interchangeable as answers to "Why?" "asked when I go upstairs" — Clark's parenthetical "(57)" is ambiguous between the volume's page and Intention's page; I have not located the § of *Intention* this comes from.

## Reviews used (what each contributes)

- **Clark (NDPR 2013)** — the fullest English account of every essay, with page-cited quotations; offers his own counterexamples (shoes intention never acted on; "press hard" carbon copies; prime numbers vs Ford). Most reliable source here.
- **Piedrahita (*Ideas y Valores* 2017, Spanish)** — essay-by-essay summary; translates quotations into Spanish (English originals sometimes given in brackets); critical of McDowell's relevance, Setiya's premises, Rödl's "will", Müller's reading. Reliable as summary; its evaluations are a graduate student's.
- **Teichmann (*Analysis* 2012, extract)** — positive: the volume shows Anscombe's ideas "opening up new avenues and taking on a life of their own"; notes the four practical-knowledge essays and McDowell; quotes Cora Diamond's jacket remark that the significance of *Intention* "has been taken for granted rather than genuinely understood".
