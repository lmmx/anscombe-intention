# Action, Practical Knowledge, and the Good — Jennifer A. Frey (2016 draft)

## What this source is
A draft paper arguing that two Anscombean theses are inseparable: the **knowledge requirement** (intentional action is known by its agent immediately and non-evidentially) and the **goodness requirement** (action is pursued "under the guise of the good"). Frey notes that Setiya, Thompson and Velleman accept the first but not the second, while Davidson accepts the second but not the first.

Her central interpretive claim: an intentional action is typically "a material event in the world that is both brought about and metaphysically constituted through the agent's own practical self-knowledge of it". The practical syllogism formally represents this means–end order and so displays the good the action serves.

**Caveat:** this is an unpolished draft, and the published Ergo paper (2019) was not accessed. Page references to *Intention* are given by page number. The § mappings below are made against the analytic table of contents.

## Account of Intention
- **Intentional descriptions (pp. 7–9, §§4–5).** Intentional descriptions can be picked out from the third person. The special "Why?" ties them to the agent's knowledge and reasons.
  - Refusing the question ("I didn't know he was in here") shows that the description is not intentional (§6).
  - Intentional descriptions are typically vague. Frey's Giant Eagle example: no particular bottle of milk is intended.
- **Mental causes (p. 16, §§9–10).** Knocking a cup off the table because one saw a face at the window is known without observation, yet it is not intentional. Frey uses this as a decisive argument that "non-observational" and "non-inferential" do not define practical knowledge. She targets Hursthouse ("Intention", 2000), Setiya, Pickard, Gibbons and Velleman.
- **Six further marks of practical knowledge in Anscombe:**
  1. failure is a mistake of performance, not judgement (p. 82, §45; also §32);
  2. it is contradicted by an opposing intention, not a contrary fact (p. 55, §31);
  3. it is understood only via practical reasoning (p. 57, §33);
  4. it implies know-how (pp. 87–88, §48);
  5. it is "the cause of what it understands" (§48);
  6. its objects are formally subject to "Why?" and to a rational order (p. 88, §48).

  The "incorrigibly contemplative conception of knowledge" passage (p. 57, §32) is the key text.
- **"I do what happens" (p. 52, §29).** The object of practical knowledge is the observable material event itself. Anscombe calls the "epistemically modest" alternative a "mad account".
- **The "argument from practical fallibility".** We often fail to do what we intend, so we cannot know what we do just by intending. Frey says Sarah Paul presses this argument. Frey replies that it presupposes a gap between two independently specifiable realities, a mental state and an event, linked causally. For Anscombe, in the good case, intention and action are "one and the same reality". Failure is a "failure of self-constitution", not of correspondence.
- **Contrast with Davidson.** Davidson takes the specification of action descriptions for granted and asks only about rationalisation. For Anscombe, specification and rationalisation are one question: knowing what one does is grasping the action's practical form (§47's "form of description of events" is Frey's intended reference, marked "{need citation}").
- **Part–whole unity.** The pumping example (§§23–26) shows the part–whole, means–end order. "Why?" (A→D) mirrors "How?" (D→A), and both presuppose knowledge of "what" one is up to. Unity comes from the end and is supplied by practical reason, not by nature. Frey compares Thompson's mitosis example from *Life and Action*.
- **The practical syllogism (§§33–42).**
  - It is not a psychological process (p. 80, §42) and not a moral proof (the Nazi example, p. 72, §38).
  - It is not "ordinary reasoning" to an "ought" conclusion (the beggar syllogism, p. 58, §33; the "mince pie syllogism").
  - Its conclusion is an action whose point the premises show "on active service" (p. 60, §33).
  - It "describes an order which is there whenever actions are done with intentions" (p. 80, §42).
  - The first premise mentions something wanted (§§35–37). Wanting relates to good as judgement relates to truth (p. 76, §40). The light "should" (p. 64, §35).
- **Later "Practical Inference".** Frey draws on it: goodness of the end plays the role in practical reasoning that truth of premises plays in theoretical reasoning.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Knowing what one is doing *is* knowing the supposed good of doing it. These are "one and the same practical self-knowledge".
- Action is **self-constitution** (borrowing Korsgaard's term). The agent is the self-conscious "principle and source" of a material process, a kind of cause "not understood solely in terms of efficient causality".
- The order of intention (A–D) and the order of execution (D–A): "what is first in the order of intention is last in the order of execution".
- Practical truth comes in degrees relative to level of description. Full practical truth belongs only to the practically wise.
- Intending requires know-how and possible attainment (citing Small 2012): one cannot intend to write in Spanish without the skill.

## Positions on key debates
- **Knowledge without observation:** Real but not definitive. The distinctive feature is its *practical* form, not the absence of observation (mental-cause knowledge is also non-observational).
- **§48 "cause of what it understands":** Strongly endorsed. Action comes to be only in the agent's knowledge that it is coming to be.
- **Davidson / causalism:** Explicitly opposed. The "standard causal view leaves the action itself un-theorized". Davidson is listed among early critics of the knowledge requirement ("Intending").
- **Naive action theory:** Adopts Thompson's "naive rationalizations" as the structure of action.
- **Intention for the future:** Little discussed. Prospective intentions presuppose attainability ("trying to get").
- **Cognitivism:** Not framed in these terms. Rejects belief-based "correspondence" models of practical knowledge.
- **The "Why?" question:** Foundational. It elicits the A–D order, which the practical syllogism formalises.
