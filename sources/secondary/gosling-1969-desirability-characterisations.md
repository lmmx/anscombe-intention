# Desirability Characterisations — J. C. Gosling (1969)

## What this source is
An early critical article, about 7,900 words, devoted entirely to the desirability-characterisation thesis of *Intention* §§33–43. Gosling was an Oxford colleague who wrote while the second edition (1963) was current. Its page references fit the 1963 pagination. It is the most detailed open-access discussion of §§35–40 found for this repo. It is critique rather than commentary: after a short exposition (pp. 3–6) the rest develops counter-examples and distinctions.

## Account of Intention
Gosling's exposition, pp. 3–6:
- **Scope.** Anscombe is concerned only with wanting that leads to or explains action. She excludes "fruitless yearnings" and cases of doing something "because I just wanted to" with no particular reason. Gosling's illustrations here (sticking out one's tongue at a hostess, rapping a gong) appear to be his own. The relevant cases are those whose reasoning can be spelled out in calculative form that the agent would recognise as an elaboration of his reason.
- **Core thesis.** The starting point of the reasoning, which is the last term in the series of answers to "What do you want x for?", must give something wanted, characterised as desirable. Until then "And what do you want that for?" / "What is the good of that?" remain in order. After it, they are out of place.
  - Illustration: a farmer going to market "to buy a Jersey cow", because "any farm like mine could do with a Jersey cow". It is then absurd to ask what he wants what he could do with for. Gosling presents this within his exposition; he does not say whether it is Anscombe's own example.
  - Further illustration: catching a bus to go to a concert because one enjoys concerts.
- **Four points Gosling extracts:**
  1. A desirability characterisation ('P) is a **necessary condition** of reasoned action: the agent must consider the starting point to be 'P.
  2. **Stopping property.** Sometimes stated weakly: no further question is *required* (p. 71 para 2). Usually stated strongly: no further question is *in order* (p. 66 para 1; p. 69; p. 75 para 1).
  3. Desirability characterisations are **necessary but not sufficient** for wanting for a reason. Gosling's lobster case: thought both tasty and indigestible, and eaten because tasty.
  4. **No single desirability characterisation is necessary** (p. 75 para 1). "X is a good thing to have" gives the general *form* of such characterisations, not one of them (= *bonum est multiplex*, §39).
- **"Good" as formal object.** On Gosling's reading, "good" is in Anscombe's use the formal object of wanting *for a reason*, not of wanting as such. "I just wanted to" is another way of saying "for no particular reason".

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- **Tension between points ii and iv.** Gosling's sybarite who wants health food because he thinks healthy food is pleasant has an answer to "What do you want what is healthy for?". So "because it is healthy" need not stop the series, and the strong stopping claim fails if characterisations are independent. If we retreat to a thesis about the agent's actual starting point, we lose the easy test for identifying desirability characterisations, namely that further "What for?" is absurd.
- **Breakfast.** Looking for a restaurant because one wants breakfast is practical reasoning. But breakfast is merely habitual and is not wanted under any desirability characterisation. Either breakfast becomes a desirability characterisation ("and then the rot has set in"), or the case is a "degenerate" piece of practical reasoning.
- **Three interests behind "What for?",** illustrated with chess:
  1. the agent's actual reasoning or purpose;
  2. whether the action has been thought out in a wider context, e.g. strategy;
  3. the form of the action's *attraction* (the "romantic" who loves the knight's move).

  Answers such as "it's fun" or "thrilling" have special stopping status only for interest 3.
- **Thought-experiment.** A population whose ultimate goals are just preserving life and furthering knowledge, without any language of attraction, still reasons practically. So it is not evident that a rational agent's ends must be wanted under an attraction-giving characterisation.
- **Conclusions** (Spanish summary, pp. 23–25):
  - no clear desirability-characterisation thesis is tenable for practical reasoning;
  - something like it holds for questions about forms of attraction;
  - making attraction central to reasoned desire would be at most "a programme".

## Section pointers
- §§33–43 — named as the target (p. 3).
- §35 (p. 66 para 1) — strong stopping claim, as Gosling cites it.
- §36 (p. 69) — cited for the strong stopping claim. The TOC places p. 69 in §36, before §37 starts on p. 70.
- §37 (p. 71 para 2) — weaker version: no further "What for?" *required*.
- §39 (p. 75 para 1) — no single characterisation is necessary; *bonum est multiplex*; also cited for strong stopping.
- §17 (probably) — Anscombe's tiptoe/spot-on-the-wall case, done "for no particular reason" (cited without page).
- **Caution:** the Jersey-cow and concert illustrations sit inside Gosling's exposition, but he does not say they are Anscombe's. Check against the text before attributing them.
