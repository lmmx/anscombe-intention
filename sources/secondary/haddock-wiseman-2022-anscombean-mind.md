# The Anscombean Mind — Adrian Haddock & Rachael Wiseman (eds.)

## What this source is

A 23-chapter Routledge "Philosophical Minds" handbook, arranged in three parts mirroring Anscombe's three *Collected Papers* volumes (From Parmenides to Wittgenstein; Metaphysics and the Philosophy of Mind; Ethics, Religion and Politics), opening with Anscombe's previously unpublished 1948 report to the Mary Somerville Fellowship Committee. About ten chapters bear directly on *Intention*. I read the editors' Introduction in full from the publisher preview; chapter content below is the **editors' summaries**, not the chapters.

## Structure (chapter → §§ of Intention)

The volume is not a commentary and imposes no division on *Intention*. The Introduction's ordering of the Intention material is nonetheless informative: (i) knowledge without observation / practical knowledge (Part 2: McDowell, Özaltun), (ii) rationality and animals (Marcus), (iii) practical inference (Small, then Newton), (iv) intention-dependent descriptions and the ethics boundary (Bierson & Schwenkler), (v) human/voluntary action and §49 (Aucouturier, Narboux), (vi) perception and "Why?" (Mac Cumhaill). § alignments in the front matter are mine.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation, attributed to the editors' Introduction)

- **Genesis.** In 1956 Anscombe opposed Oxford's honorary degree for Truman and was surprised her arguments were not understood; she diagnosed a false picture of action and intention. In the 1957 broadcast "Does Oxford Moral Philosophy Corrupt the Youth?" she spoke of a failure to distinguish intended from foreseen consequences, adding that she lectured on such problems "in the philosophy of psychology" — the lectures that became *Intention*. Read with the first thesis of "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958).
- **But not only ethics.** The 1948 Somerville report shows she had been thinking about intentionality for a decade before the Truman protest, purely in philosophy of mind, epistemology and metaphysics, trying to unify what she learnt in Wittgenstein's classes with Aristotelian and Thomist theories of the self. The Part 2 chapters show the interest of her philosophy of mind and action "in abstraction from the moral philosophy that it seeks to make possible".
- **Self-knowledge.** The concern with "the subject" surfaces in *Intention* through "the knowledge that a man has of his intentional actions" (Intention pp. 50–51), which is first-personal, described as "knowledge without observation" and later as "practical knowledge", and is in some sense internal to intentional action.
- **Recent literature.** "Particularly notable" is the recent literature on *Intention*, "much of it inspired by the writings of Michael Thompson"; but that work "only scratches the surface" of the corpus.

Editors' chapter summaries (Intention-relevant):
- **McDowell** — difficulty: practical knowledge is knowledge of a public happening of a kind knowable observationally, yet "without observation". Using a case shaped like Davidson's carbon-copier, McDowell (who does *not* take Anscombe to hold that whoever does A intentionally knows he is doing A) argues practical knowledge must be *aided by receptive*, e.g. perceptual, knowledge to know that the relevant things are happening.
- **Özaltun** — *does* attribute that thesis to Anscombe and defends it: Velleman was right that agent's knowledge stems from its practical, causal nature, wrong to make it true belief about outcomes; we need a sui generis agential attitude rather than belief-like representation.
- **Marcus** — puzzle: actions are intentional under descriptions the agent can give, which seems to restrict intentional action to language users, yet Anscombe says animals *have* (but cannot express) intentions (§47). Against "transformative" readings, animals act intentionally and for reasons with practical knowledge; rationality changes what practical knowledge is (a power to determine which ends are worth pursuing), and makes possible distinctively human practical irrationality.
- **Small** — §33's "difference of form" between theoretical and practical reasoning vs the later paper "Practical Inference", where practicality lies in the use of premises and whether it is a distinct form is "a matter of indifference": Anscombe is right about use, wrong about indifference — the teleology of practical inference fixes more formal features than she recognised.
- **Newton** — Anscombe's practical syllogisms can be valid without being sound, do not show the action logically compelled, and are indifferent to motive; Kantian practical syllogisms are self-conscious knowledge of what one ought to do. Anscombe is a sceptic about the latter — a "pre-critical", not post-Kantian, thinker.
- **Bierson & Schwenkler** — the correctness of some descriptions of physical facts depends on what the agent thinks (stalking requires perceiving and wanting) and on customs and institutions; some descriptions (marrying, promising, stealing, murdering) depend on knowledge of the nature of one's act; Anscombe's philosophy of psychology leaves open which of these are morally good or bad — a separate task for ethics.
- **Aucouturier** — following Aquinas, "human action" (under the command of reason) is wider than intentional action and equivalent to (a sense of) voluntary action; explains how carelessness, negligence and omission can be bad actions though unintentional.
- **Narboux** — new extended commentary on **§49**; voluntary action depends on intentional action, and "the topic of the voluntary has been the subject of Anscombe's inquiry all along"; but *Intention* does not complete the account, which needs the relation of voluntariness to knowledge and an ethical setting for intention vs foresight (arsonist vs surgeon cases).
- **Mac Cumhaill** — "Why?" is a live question in a dialogue between perceiver and agent in shared circumstances; descriptions in perceptual reports coincide with those an agent would give; series of "Why?" answers parallel widening descriptions of what is seen; what one can see "depends on who one is".
- **Wiseman** — draws on the parallel between "What do I really do?" and "What do I really see?" and between answers to "Why?" and the "description under which" in sensation; the Somerville report shows this escape from phenomenalism contains the seeds of "The First Person".

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

1. *Intention* is both prolegomenon to ethics and independently a work in philosophy of mind with a pre-1956 history (Somerville report).
2. **The voluntary as the hidden topic** (Narboux) — a strong reading that elevates §49 from appendix to key.
3. **Live disagreement on the knowledge thesis**: McDowell (practical knowledge needs receptive aid; Anscombe not committed to knowledge-of-all-intentional-action) vs Özaltun (she is committed and is right).
4. **Practical inference**: Small vs Anscombe's later "Practical Inference"; Newton's anti-Kantian Anscombe vs the Rödl-style Kantian reconciliation (cf. ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md).
