# Anscombe lectures I–IV (Cambridge Part II, 2025) — Richard Holton

## What this source is

Four lecture handouts for Richard Holton's Michaelmas 2025 Cambridge Part II course on Anscombe within the "Wittgenstein & His Successors" paper. The course page lists the readings per lecture: L1 §§1–4 (with "Modern Moral Philosophy" and Wiseman's Routledge GuideBook); L2 §§5–21 (with Moran & Stone, "Anscombe on the Expression of Intention"); L3 §§22–45 (with the Horizon documentary on Ian Waterman, *The Man Who Lost His Body*); L4 §46 ff. (with "Causality and Determinism"). Holton recommends Wiseman's and Schwenkler's guides as introductions. The handouts are outline notes plus long quotations; the commentary is compressed but contains several original interpretive proposals, notably on §§31, 45 and 48. A separate Holton course ("Intention and the Will"/"Mental Faculties", Lent 2025) has a first handout that uses Anscombe's three uses of "intention" but is otherwise about Bratman, Davidson, Velleman; it is not summarised here.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

### Lecture I — Background, methodology and the three heads
- **Moral background.** "Mr Truman's Degree" (protest May 1956, pamphlet 1957) raises the question what Truman's intentions were. "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) argues ethics needs an adequate "philosophy of psychology" — an account of what a human action is and how its description is affected by motive and intention — e.g. to underpin the doctrine of double effect. *Intention* (1957) came from lectures given in Hilary (Lent) term 1957, so was published very quickly.
- **Method.** "Depth grammar": going beyond the verbal to the form of life in which a concept sits. Is she describing mental processes? Holton cites §42 (Aristotle's practical syllogisms describe an *order* present whenever actions are done with intentions, not actual mental processes — the same order found in the pumping discussion). He quotes Wiseman that Anscombe is not talking about individual minds, and pushes back: she is surely talking about individual actors, and what they think, in a social context. Wiseman's "three voices" (Descriptive, Intuitive, Philosophical) are reported, with the point that accommodating first intuitions is not the aim; Holton asks whether Wiseman's contrast with Austin and McDowell is fair.
- **§1** quoted at length as the example: three kinds of statement; "intention always concerns the future" is false of intentional action; the word is not equivocal; talk of "different senses" signals we are in the dark about the concept; she begins piecemeal with expressions of intention.
- **Wittgenstein's question** (PI §621: what is left over if I subtract my arm's going up from my raising it?). Anscombe rejects its presupposition: raising my arm entails my arm going up but is not *constructed* from it plus some further element. Analogy: red entails coloured but is not coloured + extra factor; Williamson on knowledge ≠ true belief + X. Holton credits Anscombe as among the first to see this.
- **Prescient ideas:** deviant causal chains (§20, the "Tremble" example — trembling because of a terrible voice is not obeying); what it would take to be genuinely indifferent to an outcome (§25).
- **Things she is *not* much interested in:** (i) whether reasons exclude causes — she says little about causation in *Intention*; "mental causation" is a special usage and she seems too unsure what causation is to draw big conclusions; (ii) the progressive aspect ("I was intending to…", §23), which later writers (Thompson, Lavin) made central but which is not explicit in Anscombe much.

### Lecture II — Intentional action
- **Geography of the three heads.** Expression of intention: §§2–4 and §§50–52. Intentional action: explicit focus §§5–20, recurring after. Intention with which: Wiseman starts it at §22 (citing §22's "We must now turn to the closer examination of this"); Holton thinks it is implicit long before (the "Why?" raised in §5; intention relevant to it at §12; back at §16; in focus from §20). Its end is unclear: Wiseman says §40; Holton prefers §50, where Anscombe says she has completed the inquiry into intentional action and intention with which.
- **How the heads fit.** Standard strategies make one basic: intentional action (Ryle?), intention with which (Davidson 1963), or intention (Bratman). Anscombe instead says (§23) that "to a certain extent" the three are equivalent — "I am going to fetch my camera", "I am fetching my camera", "in order to fetch my camera" are interchangeable answers to "Why?" when going upstairs. This is the *conclusion* of her inquiry, and only "to a certain extent"; she shows why by looking at the point of the concept in social life, sometimes by imagining concepts that apply under one head only (§20). The heads can come apart in cases, not in general; her focus is the core of the practice, not outlandish counterexamples.
- **Expression of intention (§§2–4).** Why expressions rather than states? Not behaviourism; perhaps Wittgensteinian practice. Are expressions of intention so different from expressions of belief ("It is red"/"I'm going to the shops")? English "I am intending" takes the progressive, unlike belief, but Holton thinks this local to English (Wittgenstein, *RPP* I §598, quoted in German). The intuitive intention/prediction contrast leads to "reason to act", then an odd Wittgenstein passage about leaves blown about, then postponement of reasons to the discussion of intentional action. §4 turns to how we can tell intentions — look at actions; Holton juxtaposes §4's end ("what a man actually does" comes first) with its admission that an intention can be complete yet "purely interior".
- **"A feast of distinctions" (§5 ff.).** Contents §5 quoted. To avoid circularity Anscombe starts negatively, and the relata are actions *under descriptions* (§6 sawing example). Holton's taxonomy:
  - (A) "Why?" fails to get application (only causal explanations available): (i) ignorance, §6; (ii) knowledge only by observation, e.g. peristaltic movement of the gut, §8; (iii) known without observation but cause not so known, e.g. the jerk when going off to sleep, contrasted with jumping back at the crocodile's leap and bark, where the cause is known without observation (§8; and §9's remark that the causation itself is known without observation).
  - Critique of §8's definition of "without observation" (limb-position passage quoted): many class proprioception as a mode of perception; the "separately describable sensations" criterion is shaky (blindsight, peripheral vision); and proprioception may involve sensation.
  - (B) the question "gets application": (i) **mental cause** — action and cause both known without observation, but the answer is a mere (mental) cause: the child who fears the red stuff on the stairs because of a misheard remark; cases not sensitive to whether the agent thought of good/harm (p. 22); "I saw such-and-such and it made me jump" (p. 16); reason and cause not "everywhere sharply distinct" (p. 24). (ii) **intentional acts** — Anscombe's §16 summary quoted in full, then §17 ("For no particular reason" is not a rejection of the question, any more than "None" refuses the question how much money is in my pocket).

### Lecture III — The intention with which
- **The A–B–C–D account.** §23's pumping scenario quoted. Idea: keep asking "Why?" through descriptions, each instrumental to the next, until a "break"; the last term gives the intention with which, which "swallows up" the preceding intentions (§26 quoted).
- **Three natural break points:** (i) the man did not know the consequences; (ii) he knows/notices but doesn't care — the curious clicking of the pump; "I didn't care about that, I wanted my pay and just did my usual job" (Holton cites §24; the analytic contents put the "didn't care" discussion in §25). Does testing this push things back into the man's mind? No — there is a "control of the truthfulness of the answer". (iii) consequences uncertain: affects how we describe the action before completion — the not-quite-sharp break between "He is Y-ing" and "He is going to Y"; the less normal it is to take success as a matter of course, the more the objective is expressed only by "in order to" (§23; going to London to make my uncle change his will, not "I am making my uncle change his will").
- **Can what is done be known without observation?** §8's criterion again, with the same doubts; the case of Ian Waterman (loss of proprioception); Shoemaker on "self-blindness" regarding the will — would such a person be an agent? Perhaps knowledge of one's intentions is not proprioceptive but **practical knowledge** (§32 ff.).
- **"I do what happens" (§29).** Quoted: writing with eyes shut, observation as a mere aid like the eyes in writing; "If there are two ways of knowing there must be two different things known."
- **The proposed solution (§31).** The "This England" court-martial story: told to clench his teeth, the soldier hands over his false teeth. "The water is running out of a hole in the pipe" stands to "I'm replenishing the house water-supply" as "My teeth are false" to the order "Clench your teeth"; the contradictory of a description of intentional action is not "You aren't, since there's a hole in the pipe" but "Oh no you aren't", said by someone setting out to make a hole with a pick-axe. **Holton's gloss:** presupposition. The order presupposes the man has his own teeth; when false, the order misfires but is not contradicted. Likewise "I am pumping water" presupposes working apparatus; denying a presupposition is not contradicting. So the agent knows what he is doing provided the presuppositions are met, *without* knowing that they are met (which could only be known by observation) — a form of Nozick's "open knowledge", recently revived (Sharon & Spectre; Yablo 2017, knowledge closed within a subject matter: the pumper is talking about what he is doing, not about the pipes). Holton notes no one has attributed this to Anscombe, because readers assume she denies that (practical) knowledge is factive. (Note: the analytic contents describe §31's order-comparison as an "attempted solution" whose "inadequacy" Anscombe then registers; Holton presents it as "The solution" and does not mention this.)
- **§45 and Theophrastus.** The blackboard passage ("I am a fool", eyes shut; my knowledge "would have been the same" had the chalk failed; "a funny sort of knowledge"; Theophrastus: "the mistake is in the performance, not in the judgment"). Holton traces the Theophrastus remark (from the *Magna Moralia*, 1189b, which Anscombe attributes to Theophrastus — on not deliberating how to spell "Archicles") back to §2, where Anscombe says that if I don't do what I said, what I said *was* false, but the falsehood does not impugn what I said — the facts are impugned for not according with the words. So in §45 the lesson is not that we have knowledge of what is false but that the *lack* of knowledge is not the judgment's fault. Where there is no bad luck — where (per §48) the event really is the execution of an intention — there is categorical practical knowledge, not conditional knowledge.
- **"Would have been the same."** Holton's parody: "my wife would have been the same if I had not married her" — the same *person* but not my wife; likewise the same mental state (same content) would have been mere false belief. This is a modal claim about one state, not a common-factor comparison between two states. Why more plausible for practical than theoretical knowledge: for theoretical knowledge, state identity is bound to aetiology (externalism), but in practical knowledge the world-state that makes it true is causally *subsequent* to the knowledge, so the very same state could have been false belief.

### Lecture IV — Some remaining topics
- **Intention-dependent concepts (§47).** Anscombe's two lists: descriptions usable only of intentional action (telephoning, calling, groping, crouching, greeting, signing/signalling, paying/selling/buying, hiring/dismissing, sending for, marrying/contracting) and ones that sometimes are and sometimes are not (intruding, offending, coming to possess, kicking, abandoning, dropping/holding/picking up, switching on/off, placing/arranging) — the latter "vital descriptions" that go beyond physics, existing because we make e.g. switches. Purpose: to stop us treating "intentional" as an *extra property* of a natural class of events. Compare "Causality and Determinism" (1971): the general word "cause" presupposes many special causal concepts (scrape, push, wet, carry…); perhaps "intentional" likewise presupposes mastery of intention-laden verbs.
- **Do we always know what we are doing?** §48 quoted: failure to execute intentions is "necessarily the rare exception", though failure to attain the *desirability characterisation* (pleasure, health, virtue) is common; what is rare is for one's performance under its more *immediate* descriptions not to be what one supposes; and the agent's knowledge gives the descriptions under which what goes on is the execution of an intention.
- **§52 cases.** Certainty that one will do something combined with intending not to: the man hanging from a precipice ("his fingers let go, not he"); the man sure he will break under torture yet determined not to; St Peter, who might have reasoned "since he says it, it is true" and yet said "I will not" — possible because of ignorance of *how* the prophecy would be fulfilled, so he could do intentionally what he intended not to without changing his mind. Holton's question: what if Peter had known how? Aside: Geach (*Mental Acts*, 1954) also uses Peter and the cock-crow.
- **Back to Truman.** Anscombe says little in response to a double-effect defence of Truman; Holton quotes the pamphlet: with Hiroshima and Nagasaki it was "certainly decided to kill the innocent as a means to an end".

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

- The intention-with-which discussion runs implicitly from §5/§12 and explicitly to §50 (against Wiseman's §22–§40).
- The equivalence of the three heads (§23) is the *conclusion* and holds only "to a certain extent"; Anscombe studies the core of the practice, not edge cases.
- Anscombe as anti-additive about action (raising ≠ rising + X), anticipating knowledge-first-style anti-decomposition.
- Persistent scepticism about §8's criterion of "without observation" (proprioception as perception; Waterman; blindsight).
- **Main original proposal:** §31 read via presupposition failure; practical knowledge as factive but "open" (not closed under known entailment to background conditions), against the common non-factive reading of §45.
- §45 read through §2: Theophrastus locates *blame* for falsehood, not a non-factive knowledge; "would have been the same" is a modal claim about one state. Practical knowledge differs from theoretical because its truthmaker is causally downstream.
- §47's lists read as blocking the "extra property" view, paralleled with Anscombe's later treatment of "cause".
