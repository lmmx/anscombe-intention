# Anscombe on Intentions and Commands — Graham Hubbs

## What this source is
An essay arguing that Anscombe's repeated comparisons between intentions and commands (§2, §20, §§31–32, §45) are central to her argument. Hubbs claims Stoutland's "Summary" (2011) neglects them. On his reading, they frame the move from practical knowledge to practical reasoning (§§33–44) and back (§§45–50). The last part uses the result to reconstruct why "Modern Moral Philosophy" calls Kant's "legislating for oneself" absurd.

## Account of Intention
- **The "two-knowledges/one-object" puzzle.** Hubbs frames the middle of the book around §29: "How can one speak of two different knowledges of exactly the same thing?" The obstacle is modern philosophy's "incorrigibly contemplative conception of knowledge" (§32). The resolution is the Thomistic formula of §48. Anscombe has already argued (§§5–20) that non-observational knowledge is constitutive of action.
- **The pumper and the observer.** If there is a hole in the pipe, an observer may seem to know something that contradicts the pumper. That suggests the pumper knows only that he is *trying*. Hubbs denies this is Anscombe's view and cites Thompson: practical knowledge is "self-knowledge in respect of what might in principle be observed". In the good case, observer and agent know one and the same fact.
- **Commands in §2.** A command is "a description of a future action… cast in a form whose point… is to make the person do what is described". Execution-conditions correspond to truth-conditions. Commands and expressions of intention are alike in being justified by what it would be good to make happen, not by evidence. §2 also contains the first use of Theophrastus ("mistake… of performance, not of judgment").
- **§20.** Commandability is considered as a way of marking off intentional actions, but the distinction "seems to be an idle one" (n. 7).
- **§31.** The court-martialled soldier with false teeth. The contradictory of an order is an order ("Do not clench your teeth"), not a report that it cannot be executed. Likewise the contradictory of "I am replenishing the water-supply" is "Oh no, you aren't" said by someone who sets out to hole the pipe with a pick-axe.
  - Hubbs quotes Haddock: "contradictories must be of the same logical order".
  - So observation-reports about the pipe do not directly *contradict* the agent's non-observational self-description.
  - But the parallel "fails at the centre", because "an order is not a piece of knowledge" (§31).
- **§32.** Failing to execute an accepted order is "precisely analogous" to failing to carry out an intention. Both are mistakes of performance. Disobedience is different: it is refusing to form the intention.
- **§§33–45.** Practical reasoning concludes in action, or in an intention when the circumstances are not yet right. Because it is reasoning, it also yields knowledge, whose object is "what happens".
- **§45.** The builder knows what is done *practically* because he has set execution-conditions. "Orders… can be disobeyed, and intentions fail to get executed."

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- **The apparent contradiction.** §32 calls failure to execute an order "analogous" to failure of intention, and contrasts both with disobedience. §45 seems to pair disobedience with intention-failure. Hubbs resolves this by perspective: §32 takes the recipient's point of view, §45 the commander's. For the commander, both disobedience and failed execution are performance-failures.
- **No analogue of disobedience for intentions.** One cannot both set an end and reject it, so "to set is to form". This closes the gap between intending and failing to act.
- **Second interpretive step (following Thompson's imperfective reading and Haddock).** Practical knowledge is "in the first place, knowledge of what is happening".
  - If an H-bomb destroys the pumper before the water arrives, it is still true that he *was* replenishing the supply.
  - Picking up new chalk is part of still writing "I am a fool".
  - Hubbs's resulting answer to the puzzle: the agent knows that execution-conditions have been set and that the process of meeting them has begun. In the good case, this is the very fact an observer can know. Hubbs notes that Schwenkler 2015 dissents.
- **Kant.** Self-legislation would require one to stand to oneself as both superior legislator and possibly disobedient subject. That is incoherent for an individual, because to set an end is to form an intention. Hubbs suggests a non-absurd *collective* version (Rousseau's general will).

## Positions on key debates
- **Knowledge without observation:** Real and constitutive of action. Its object is, in the good case, the same as the observer's.
- **§48 "cause of what it understands":** Taken as the vindicated Thomistic doctrine, reached via practical reasoning. Not analysed in causal-metaphysical detail.
- **Davidson / causalism:** Not discussed.
- **Naive action theory:** Endorses Thompson's progressive, imperfective framing as Anscombe's "ultimate answer".
- **Intention for the future / §§1–4:** §2's commands–intentions parallel is foundational. Both are "predictions" in Anscombe's wide sense, with execution-based correctness. Formed intentions for later are commitments to act when circumstances are appropriate.
- **Cognitivism:** Not directly. Intentions are not self-commands, because the agent's description is "a piece of knowledge" and an order is not.
- **"Why?":** Background only.
