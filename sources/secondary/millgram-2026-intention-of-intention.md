# The Intention of Intention — Elijah Millgram

## What this source is
A whole-book interpretation that tries to make the *structure* of *Intention* visible to readers who find it an "unreadable classic". Millgram's thesis has two parts:

1. The book is itself an intentional action, deliberately built to fit its own theory.
2. Its organising template is the nested A–D structure of the pumping example (§23), which Millgram calls the **"pumping model"**.

Each apparently awkward stretch of argument is read as an outer "matryoshka-doll" layer around that core. The essay ends by proposing that the book's own "desirability characterisation", its point, is a conception of freedom located in action.

The text is a manuscript dated June 2026, so it is recent and possibly unpublished. Citations are by section number.

## Account of Intention
- **The pumping model (§23).** A core activity, such as moving an arm, is redescribed in ever wider contexts: operating the pump, replenishing the water-supply, poisoning the inhabitants. The actions nest "like matryoshka dolls".
  - The layers are connected by the agent's own grasp of what is a way of bringing about what, as elicited by the "Why?" series.
  - No fancy causal theory is needed. §22's bumpkin who thinks an authority can cancel an eclipse shows that the agent's "working sense" of means is enough.
  - The model has limits. "Oh, no reason" answers (§§17–18, 20–21) and the logical "break" past which further hopes, such as the poisoner's utopia, are not part of the action (§§23, 34).
  - "The why is the what": inside an action, asking why and asking what come to the same.
- **Comparison with Vogler and Thompson.** Millgram credits Vogler (*Reasonably Vicious*, 2002) and Thompson (*Life and Action* Part Two) with treating special cases of this nesting. Thompson's actions are recursively composed and, on Thompson's view, never bottom out.
- **"Nothing added" (§19).** The "intention as extra add-on" arguments (§19's second paragraph, and the §2 point that intentions are future-directed) look weak on their own, and Bratman would be unmoved. Read as a frame around the pumping model, they make a different point: intention is the *formal cause* (the control structure) of an action, internal to it, not something "sitting around nearby". Millgram cites Vogler 2019 ("Nothing Added: Intention §§19 & 20") and Ford 2011.
- **Clearing the ground (§§4–16).** The "Twenty Questions" peeling-off of mental causes, startled jumps, limb position and motives (§4; §§6, 8; §§12–14) clears away rival kinds of non-observational knowledge ahead of time. Backward-looking motives (§13) are later classed under desirability characterisations.
- **Desirability characterisations (§§37–40).** These stop the "what for?" regress in one way (§37), but still allow further questions of another kind ("thrilling how?"). Examples include "what is the good of its being bad?" (§39) and "what is the pleasure of it?" (§40).
  - Millgram's main claim: *knowing what you are doing* is the desirability characterisation of intentional action as such.
  - He distinguishes this from Velleman, who makes self-understanding an *objective* of action.
- **Practical syllogism (§§33–44).** The discussion shows that there are many incompatible ways of building activity into action toward the same end (§44 "options"; §45 "a right order"). So practical reasons do not compel the way facts compel belief. Millgram calls this the **logical freedom** of action. §32's shopping list (fit to what one set oneself to do, not to the facts) is the second of the "two best-known passages".
- **Contradicting an intention (§31).** The contradictory of an intention is another intention ("Oh no, you're not!"), not a counter-prediction. Following Small 2012, a failed precondition only makes one *means* "fall to the ground".
- **Moore's-paradox analogy (§52).** A first-person confident counter-prediction sounds self-contradictory, but in the third person it is not. Intentions and predictions are therefore not "head-on" contradictories. Millgram notes the textual caveat that at §22 a counter-prediction "is, in some way, to contradict the intention".
- **Determinism.** Read speculatively, the opening and closing discussion of prediction (§§2, 50–52) shows that predictability does not contradict intention. Determinism and logical freedom "pass each other by". Millgram links this to Anscombe's indeterminism in her later papers.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- The book has a deliberate "matryoshka" architecture. Section markers ("I therefore turn…" §4; "Having raised enough difficulties…" §31; "now I will return…" §50) show it executing its own A–D order.
- Intention is the formal cause, or control structure, of action. Whether mental states *implement* it is a question for cognitive science, not philosophy.
- Anscombe is resolutely **anti-psychologistic** (citing the end of §18).
- Anscombe "and Davidson as nemeses": §§20–21 (not everything can be "no particular reason") parallels Davidson's anti-skeptical point that one cannot be wrong about most things.
- Ethics in the background: the pumping example evokes an assassination of Nazi leaders, and Millgram suspects that double effect is in view (§§5, 12, 38–39 disclaim moralising).
- Critique: Millgram is "not an Anscombean". He suggests that not knowing what one is doing (learning, art, "dialectical activities") can be valuable.

## Positions on key debates
- **Knowledge without observation:** Explained by the agent's imposing the logical (A–D) form on activity. Knowing what one is doing *is* managing one's activity to have that form. Setiya 2016 is cited for knowledge of the formal cause.
- **§48 "cause of what it understands":** Not treated directly. The "formal cause" theme is applied to intention itself rather than to knowledge.
- **Davidson / causalism:** Bratman's add-on, mental-state view of intention is the "standard view" that Anscombe's arguments, read piecemeal, fail to dislodge. Read structurally, they make the question of implementing mental states philosophically idle. A Davidsonian parallel is noted (§§20–21).
- **Naive action theory:** Thompson's recursion is treated as a *special case* of the more general nesting.
- **Intention for the future / §§1–4, 50–52:** The opening and closing frame of the book. Expressions of intention vs. predictions; the Moore's-paradox analogy (§52); "I just want to" (§51) still takes a desirability characterisation. Millgram reads the opening linguistic-analysis accent as a concession to the 1950s audience. He reads §1's "three heads" as an order of inquiry, not a blacklist of "I intend to…".
- **Cognitivism:** Rejects Velleman's prediction-based view as adopting a view Anscombe "considers and dismisses" (§2).
- **The "Why?" question:** Elicits the nested structure, and the why = the what.
