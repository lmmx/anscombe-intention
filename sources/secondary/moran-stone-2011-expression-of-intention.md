# Anscombe on Expression of Intention: An Exegesis — Richard Moran & Martin J. Stone (2009/2011)

## What this source is
An exegetical essay on why *Intention* begins with "expression of intention" as one of its three divisions (§1), and on Anscombe's puzzling claim (p. 5) that intention, unlike emotion, has no natural expression. It is the lead essay of the Ford–Hornsby–Stoutland volume (see [the volume file](ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md)). Its sections are: 1 The Problem; 2 Background: The Transformation of Intention; 3 Why "Expression": Three Clues; 4 Expressions of Intention: The General and the Special Use; 5 "In Terms of Language"; 6 What Natural Indications Can't Do: Contradiction, Commitment, Impugning the Facts; 7 Homage Revisited.

## Account of Intention
- **The puzzle (§1, p. 5).** "Expression of intention" looks like an outlier among the three divisions, since one would not head a book on emotion with "expressions of emotion". Anscombe's first sentence nonetheless foregrounds it, and on p. 5 she says brutes have intentions but lack "any distinct expression" of them: a cat's movements in stalking a bird are hardly an expression of intention, any more than a car's stalling expresses its being about to stop. Expression of intention is "purely conventional" or linguistic, and Wittgenstein "seems … to have gone wrong" in speaking of its natural expression (*PI* §647). The authors take the point to apply to humans as well as animals.
- **"Transformed Anscombe" (TA).** Davidson and Bratman recast the first division as "intending to act", a possibly "pure" mental state, and then try to connect it to intentional action. The authors say this is not Anscombe's set of divisions. She speaks of "intention in a proposed action". She grants that an intention can be complete though "a purely interior thing" (p. 9) yet says what a man actually does is "the first" thing to consider (p. 9). At p. 90 she says only that what was said about intention in acting "applies also" to intention in a proposed action.
- **Clues for "expression" (pp. 5–6, 3, 92).** Ignoring expression leads to "wrong notions" (drives and sets, intention as a kind of desire, an irreducible intuition of "I intend"). An expression of intention, like a prediction, gives information about what will happen and is true or false according to whether it happens (p. 92, in a note: "I'm just going to get up").
- **The spectrum.** Uses of "intention" run from pure intending (nothing yet done toward the end), through acting in progress, to the perfected "He ϕ-ed". States are static; performances unfold and have purposive sub-parts. Intending takes a performance (to ϕ), not a proposition, as its object. It can be asked "Why?" about and can be commanded, like action. Pure intentions are found within actions already under way (Davidson's "writing the word 'action'").
- **The general and the special use (pp. 34–35, 40).** Answers such as "Doing Yoga" are expressions of intention (pp. 34–35). "Expression of intention for the future" is "one species of a common genus" of positive answers to "Why?". The three divisions are "to a certain extent … simply equivalent" as answers to "Why?" (p. 40), and there is not "a quite sharp break" between "She is making tea" and "She is going to make tea" (p. 40).
- **Animals (pp. 86–87).** Anscombe "certainly" ascribes intention to animals, because we describe what further they are doing in doing something, although "the cat can utter no thoughts" (pp. 86–87). The authors also cite her bird-lime case from "Under a Description".
- **What natural indications cannot do (p. 55, pp. 4–5).** An expression of intention is contradicted by opposing the act ("Oh, no, you aren't", p. 55), not by doubting the agent's state. It makes a claim on the world, can impugn the performance ("the mistake here is one of performance, not of judgment", pp. 4–5), and commits the agent. Natural behaviour cannot do these things.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- **"Immediate elucidation" vs "connective strategies."** Their term (not Anscombe's): an immediate elucidation "exhibits the divisions of intention as inflections of a single form". Connective strategies (TA) first analyse a state of intending and then connect it to action. The "Why?" question is Anscombe's "device" (p. 80) for a "non-connective" explanation.
- **Intending has the structure of a performance.** Intending is "structurally of a piece with the simple progressive", only further to the left on the spectrum. With Ferrero, they hold that "to intend to ϕ is to be (already) engaged in ϕ-ing".
- **Not behaviourism.** Anscombe denies only that the notion of a "state" from belief and desire applies here, not that intending exists.
- **Against direction of fit.** The phrase does not occur in Anscombe. Its attribution comes from Platts (1979). Her contrast is between a mistake in what was said and a mistake in performance.
- **"What?" and "Why?".** A positive answer to "Why?" also says what is happening. This "systematic unity" of action's "What?" and "Why?" may be her central theme.

## Positions on key debates
- **Unity of the three heads:** unified as species of one genus (answers to "Why?"), with intention in action as the most perspicuous case. They do not derive the other heads from a mental state.
- **Is intention a mental state?** Not in the static sense of belief or desire. Intending inherits the teleological structure of action through its object.
- **Direction of fit / §32:** rejects the Searle–Platts reading.
- **Animals:** animals have intentions and act intentionally under descriptions, but do not *express* intentions. Anscombe's point concerns expression, not possession.
