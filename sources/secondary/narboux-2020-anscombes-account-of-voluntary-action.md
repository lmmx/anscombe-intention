# Anscombe's Account of Voluntary Action in *Intention* — Jean-Philippe Narboux (2020)

## What this source is
A section-by-section commentary on §49, the only open-access commentary on it found. It opposes Hyman's charge (2015) that *Intention* made voluntariness seem a minor concept ("exactly two pages"). Its sections are: 1 Introduction; 2 The place of §49 in *Intention*; 3–6 the four headings of §49 in turn; 7 the unity and limitations of §49.

## Account of Intention
- **Why the voluntary is deferred (§§5–8).** §5 shows that involuntariness cannot mark off mental causes from reasons, since walking about to military music is mentally caused yet voluntary and intentional (§5, p. 11). §7 calls the voluntary/involuntary pair "altogether very confusing" and gives four examples of the involuntary (gut, sleep-jerk, involuntary recoil, involuntary benefit). The account must wait until intentional action and practical knowledge are clear.
- **§49 (1), voluntary rather than intentional movements.** Narboux quotes it (p. 89). Mere physical movements to which "Why?" applies are called voluntary rather than intentional when the answer is like "I was fiddling" or "I don't know why", or when the movements are not considered by the agent though he could say what they are. Narboux ties (a) to §17 and (b) to the end of §30, quoting p. 54: one does not deliberate about an acquired skill, and the description of what one is doing is "at a distance" from the details of one's movements.
- **§49 (2), known concomitant results.** Quoted (p. 89): such a result is voluntary though not intentional, because one could have prevented it by forgoing the action, yet one "rejects the question 'Why?' in its connexion". It "harks back" to §25, where the pumper replies "I couldn't care tuppence" (p. 42), and was anticipated at §17 (p. 26). Narboux also quotes the rider: "from another point of view" such things can be called involuntary, if much regretted (p. 89).
- **§49 (3), voluntary passivity.** Quoted (pp. 89–90): things that are not one's own doing at all can be voluntary when they happen to one's delight and one consents. Examples: being pushed out in a punt; sliding down a bank into a party of people — "You didn't mind … did you?". Narboux traces the puzzle to Aquinas (*ST* I-II q. 6 a. 5) and to Anscombe's later "Sin" lectures.
- **§49 (4).** Quoted (p. 90): every intentional action is also voluntary, though "from another point of view" it can be called involuntary when one regrets "having" to do it. "Reluctant" would be the more common word.
- **§§25 and 27.** Narboux reads §25 as expounding and attacking the "direction of intention" by a little speech to oneself (citing §25a with "War and Murder" and AIDE). The "only you can know" temptation (§27a, p. 48; §25a) "will have to await §27 for a solution".

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- **The four headings mark a distinction between two "forms of description"** (cf. §46). They do not sort unintentional actions into kinds. This is against Hyman: (1) and (2) are neither intentional nor unintentional. §49 gives sufficient, not necessary, conditions for the voluntary, and it does not circumscribe a class of voluntary events.
- **Idle actions and (1)(a).** Teichmann holds that idle actions are voluntary but not intentional. Schwenkler (2019, p. 201) treats (1) as low-stakes terminology. Narboux rejects both. He argues that (1)(a) concerns only "mere physical movements", voluntary in a merely physiological sense. This reconciles it with Anscombe's later denial that idle beard-stroking is a "human act" (AIDE p. 208; Aquinas).
- **The voluntary is wider than the intentional.** (1)–(2) show that not everything voluntary is intentional, and (4) that everything intentional is voluntary. (3) widens the voluntary beyond actions. So "the topic of the voluntary has been the subject of Anscombe's inquiry all along".
- **Unity of (1)–(3).** All three are known without observation but not produced by practical calculation, and "Why?" is not simply irrelevant to them.
- **Deliberate limits.** §49 says little on the involuntary, omissions or voluntary ignorance. It says nothing on the relation of voluntariness to knowledge (negligence, bigamy without inquiry) or on the double-effect criterion. These are left out because they require ethics.
- **Against Wiseman (2016a: 130).** The indifferent poisoner of §25 *is* a murderer if the people die (AIDE p. 219, the arsonist).

## Positions on key debates
- **Role of §49:** central, not a coda (contrast Schwenkler 2019, Ch. 7.1).
- **Intention vs foresight / double effect:** the intent/foresight distinction is the intentional/merely-voluntary distinction. Its moral import is not built into it.
- **Does "Why?" applicability suffice for intentionality?** Yes, by Anscombe's own statements (§23d, p. 38; §25, p. 43), against Teichmann.
