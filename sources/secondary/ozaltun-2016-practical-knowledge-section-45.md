# Practical Knowledge of What Happens: A Reading of §45 — Eylem Özaltun

## What this source is
A close reading of a single section, §45, the man directing a building project by orders alone, together with its links to §2 and §§28–32. Özaltun argues that §45 does **not** restrict the object of practical knowledge to something short of what actually happens. She defends Anscombe's thesis "in its original unapologetic form": the knowledge in question is knowledge *of what happens*.

Method: Özaltun distinguishes Anscombe's own voice from a Wittgensteinian "interlocutor", a reasonable but misled voice. Phrases such as "someone might say", "it may be objected" and "it is natural to feel an objection" mark the interlocutor's moves.

## Account of Intention
- **Background: the "modest" readings.** After Davidson's "Intending", many have defended weaker versions of the knowledge thesis:
  - knowledge is non-factive (Roessler);
  - true belief suffices (Velleman);
  - the object is one's intention (Grice; Paul 2009);
  - the object is one's trying (O'Shaughnessy);
  - the object is only on-going, non-completed activity (Thompson 2011, which Özaltun counts as "still… a more modest version").
- **Moran's version.** Moran's (2004) view is "modest in a subtle way". The agent knows non-observationally only the description under which the act is intentional. Moran never discusses the §45 builder.
- **The puzzle (§29).** If there are two ways of knowing (by observation and "in intention"), must there be two objects? Anscombe rejects the way out of making the agent's object something inner. Özaltun reads "I do what happens" and "non-observational knowledge of what happens when it is my intentional doing" as "two faces of the same coin". §§33–44 (practical reasoning) are needed before §45 can answer the §29 puzzle.
- **§45, paragraph 1 (the builder).** The example works as a *definition* of practical knowledge.
  - The builder lacks perception and reports, and his "superhuman" imagination does what perception normally does. This is a thought-experiment, as §48 confirms ("a very improbable one").
  - What makes him the *agent* is settling everything "in a right order", i.e. giving the in-order-to structure. His body is irrelevant to what makes him the agent.
- **Paragraphs 2–3.** "First and foremost, he can say what the house is like." The object of practical knowledge is the publicly observable house, known immediately. The objection that he knows only a conditional ("if my orders have been obeyed") would turn the agent into an "ineffectual bystander" like someone who merely imagines.
  - Writing "I am a fool" with eyes shut: "this is what I am writing" is not elliptical for a conditional.
- **Paragraph 4, the interpretive crux.** Özaltun reconstructs it as a dialogue:
  - Interlocutor premise 1: orders can be disobeyed.
  - Interlocutor premise 2: "my knowledge would have been the same" if the chalk failed.
  - Interlocutor's challenge: so it is not knowledge of what happens.
  - Anscombe's reply: "a funny sort of knowledge…!" is *ridicule*. Premise 2 is the unwarranted step.
  - When performance fails there is no knowledge. The failure is in *performance*, not judgement (Theophrastus).
- **Paragraph 5.** James's "idea" as the object of willing, and "It's yours!" (giving by saying, possible only because property is conventional). These show the temptation and its limit.
- **Theophrastus in all three places (§2, §32, §45).** Each time the remark points to *another source of mistake*, not to knowledge independent of the facts.
  - §2's facts "impugned" for not agreeing with the words means that what gets fixed is what happens, not the utterance. Özaltun's example: printing a map next time.
  - §32's button-pressing case shows that "what happens falsifies what is said".

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Practical knowledge is a *success* notion, just as theoretical knowledge is. Practical thought gets at truth "by producing it". Özaltun cites Anscombe's later "Practical Truth".
- Knowing practically = **knowing by doing**. Performance failure is a source of mistake precisely because acting is a form of knowing.
- Practical knowledge is logically **prior** to the particular action it knows, so observational knowledge "would come too late".
- Against Haddock (2011): Haddock reads "my knowledge would have been the same" as Anscombe's own voice, limited to yet-to-be-completed actions with a "hiccup" (broadness of the progressive). Özaltun finds this "far more strained". She also lists Judith Jarvis's 1959 review of *Intention* (*Journal of Philosophy*) among readers taking that line.
- Agrees with McDowell (2010) that §45 must not be read as interiorising what is known.

## Positions on key debates
- **Knowledge without observation:** Non-observational knowledge of public events. The builder example builds the non-observational character into the set-up.
- **§48 "cause of what it understands":** Implicitly endorsed. Practical knowledge is efficacious and extends to "what is actually done and the immediate consequences of this doing". There is no separate analysis of §48.
- **Davidson / causalism:** Davidson's "Intending" is identified as the most influential criticism and the source of the modest-thesis literature.
- **Naive action theory:** Thompson's progressive restriction is classed as a modest version.
- **Intention for the future (§§2–3):** §2's discussion of statements of intention parallels §45. Statements of what I did, am doing and will do are all descriptions of what happens, and are expressions of intention when not based on evidence. Özaltun does not discuss the second §2 objection (commands as signs) "due to space".
- **Cognitivism:** Not directly addressed. Rejects views on which the object is an inner intention (Grice, Paul).
- **"Why?":** In the background. The agent supplies the in-order-to structure (practical reasoning, §§33–44).
