# Action (SEP, 2023) — Juan S. Piñeros Glasscock & Sergio Tenenbaum

## What this source is
The current SEP "Action" entry, replacing Wilson & Shpall (2012). Unlike its predecessor, it treats Anscombe's *Intention* as one of the two poles of the field (Davidson's causation vs. Anscombe's practical knowledge) and devotes its whole §4 (c. 4,000 words) to debates stemming from her notion of practical knowledge, with explicit § citations to *Intention*. It is the most § -precise encyclopedia treatment found, though a few § numbers look off (see pointers).

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

**Anscombe vs. Davidson (entry §2).** Davidson's "Actions, Reasons and Causes" targeted Wittgensteinian non-causal views; the authors say Melden clearly held the rejected theses but Anscombe (1957) and Ryle "do not clearly embrace" them, or not all of them. Anscombe's special sense of "Why?" is meant to pick out the distinctive way actions are explained — something Davidson does not deny. Davidson, "following Anscombe", notes that "x φ-ed intentionally" is an intensional context. The causal view is contrasted with the "neo-Aristotelian" view on which action is constituted by practical knowledge, "the [formal] cause of what it understands".

**4. Practical knowledge — the central notion.** Where Davidson's central notion is causation, Anscombe's is practical knowledge. They quote §48 at length: where the description of an event is of a type to be formally the description of an executed intention, and the event is actually its execution, Aquinas's account holds — practical knowledge is "the cause of what it understands", unlike speculative knowledge "derived from the objects known". On their reading she "appears to define" intentional action as an event that manifests practical knowledge. Recent sympathetic work (Moran, Thompson, Haddock, Rödl, Small, Marcus, Campbell, Frey…) and critics (Houlgate, Grice, Paul) are listed.

**4.1 Nature of practical knowledge.** Background: Aristotle (skill as cause of its products; epistemic conditions on voluntary action; practical syllogism beginning from a good and concluding in action) and the medievals on God's knowledge of creation. Anscombe first characterises intentional action via the special sense of "Why?" requesting a reason (§5); later she appeals to Aristotle's practical reasoning to connect reasons for action with the deliberative means–end structure (§33ff.). What the "Why?" reveals is the rational "order" of means and ends, and the answers give the descriptions under which the action is intentional. Pump example (§23): moving arms → operating pump → pumping water → poisoning inhabitants → (their gloss) killing them "to bring world peace". If asked "Why are you pumping water?" one answers "I was not aware I was doing that", the action is not intentional under that description (§6; §42). The descriptions manifesting the agent's understanding are "intrinsic" to the action, so the grasp cannot be a separate occurrence (§42). Hence practical knowledge is the **formal cause**: the agent's grasp determines what the action is. That explains why, "in the phrase Anscombe borrows from Aristotle's *Magna Moralia*", "the mistake is in the performance" (§32): as formal cause the knowledge sets the standard. Whether it is also an efficient cause is disputed (Setiya 2016a sceptical; Piñeros Glasscock 2020a in favour). Finally the agent knows "without observation" (§8). Three points of "wide agreement": practical knowledge is a proper subclass of non-observational knowledge, which also includes knowledge of limb position (cited §§9–10); non-observational means at least non-inferential; and it is the kind traditionally reserved for mental states — hence the striking thesis that public happenings can be known that way ("spontaneous knowledge of material reality", Rödl).

**4.2 Arguments for a knowledge condition.** (a) The "I didn't realize I was φ-ing" argument is judged unsound: the phrase expresses total unawareness (Schwenkler 2019), and the question itself may conversationally put the agent in a position to know. (b) A more promising argument "suggested by Anscombe": intentional φ-ing positions one to assert that one is φ-ing; correct assertion requires knowledge. (c) Openness of the progressive (Thompson, Falvey): the non-accidental link between action-in-progress and its completion holds via the agent's representation. (d) Naïve rationalization: "I'm φ-ing because I'm ψ-ing" requires knowing one is ψ-ing (Thompson, Hyman).

**4.3 Objections.** Davidson's carbon-copier; replies via the imperfective "openness" and a normal vs. one-shot distinction. Schwenkler's (2019) broken-cisterns case — explicitly a variant of the pump example — where "Why?" applies but the belief is unsafe; Piñeros Glasscock's anti-luminosity argument.

**4.4 Weakenings.** Davidson's "known under at least one description"; restricting to actions in progress is itself a weakening, since Anscombe "appears to include" knowledge of future actions (§§51–52), of completed actions such as having written one's name (§48), and of what is written (§19); knowledge of intentions/tryings/basic actions. Belief-condition views (Setiya, Velleman, Ross) are said to be arguably better supported by some of Anscombe's own arguments. "Normally/generally knows" views.

**4.5 Possibility of practical knowledge.** "Anscombe devoted little attention" to how practical capacities can be a source of knowledge; surveys Velleman, inferentialism (Paul), which gives up Anscombe's immediacy, know-how accounts, practical-inference accounts and entitlement accounts.

**5. Ontology.** The letter of Anscombe (1957), like Davidson, treats actions as events, but some argue her position is better captured by actions as **processes**, using the pump example: "I am moving the lever because I am pumping because I am poisoning" is natural in the progressive but distorted in the perfective. On individuation, the pump displays the Anscombean thesis (shared with Davidson) that one action has many descriptions and is intentional under only some (e.g. not under "scaring a squirrel"). Anscombe's Dickens analogy (author of *David Copperfield* = author of *Bleak House*) is cited from "Under a Description" (1979, p. 222). On animals, Anscombe (1957) held that animals could act intentionally, though not in the language-dependent way that allows a distinction between action and expression of intention.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Puts practical knowledge, read as **formal cause** (§48), at the centre of Anscombe's account, and reads her as "appearing to define" intentional action through it.
- Links §5 "Why?" and §§33ff. practical reasoning through the notion of a rational means–end "order".
- Says the knowledge condition is attributed to Anscombe but her simplest argument for it (the "I didn't know" answer) is unsound; her claim is stronger than all the weakenings.
- Notes Anscombe extends practical knowledge beyond actions in progress (past, future, §§19, 48, 51–52) — against purely imperfective readings.
- Suggests the process/imperfective ontology may fit Anscombe better than the event ontology she literally used.
- Attributes "the mistake is in the performance" to Anscombe borrowing from Aristotle's *Magna Moralia*; Sandis (2015) instead calls it "Theophrastus' point" (Anscombe's own text should be checked).

- §6 — "I was not aware I was doing that" refuses application.
- §8 — knowledge "without observation".
- §§9–10 — cited for non-observational knowledge of limb position; per the TOC that is §8 (§§9–10 concern cause vs. reason and "mental causality"). Probable mis-citation.

- §32 — "the mistake is in the performance"; knowledge as standard.
- §33ff. — Aristotle's practical reasoning connected to the order revealed by "Why?".
- §42 — cited both for "I was not aware…" and for the grasp not being a separate occurrence; the TOC gives §42 as practical reasoning describing an *order*, not a psychological process. Second use is defensible; first looks like a mis-citation (should be §6).
- §47 — (unnumbered) animals can act intentionally.
- §48 — quoted: practical knowledge "the cause of what it understands"; also knowledge of a completed act (writing one's name).
- §§51–52 — knowledge of future actions.
