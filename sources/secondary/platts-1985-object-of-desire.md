# The Object of Desire — Mark Platts (1985)

## What this source is
A systematic philosophy-of-desire paper. It takes from Davidson the Thomist doctrine that intentional action is done "in the light of some imagined good", and asks what it is to reveal that good by specifying the content of a desire. Anscombe's desirability characterisation (§37) is its main point of departure. Only the opening sections engage *Intention* directly. They are included here because they are a rare open-access discussion of §37 by a philosopher of that tradition.

## Account of Intention
- **"Fair nonsense" (p. 71).** Platts quotes Anscombe: it would be "fair nonsense" to say that because philosophers have taught that anything can be an object of desire, there is no need to characterise the objects as desirable, since it "merely so happens" that one wants them.
- **Attributed condition (p. 70).** Platts admits that Anscombe's concern "was somewhat different" from his. He attributes to her the view that an acceptable ascription of a specific desire requires that the ascriber have "reached and made intelligible" some desirability characterisation of the state of affairs desired. That characterisation reveals the imagined good in the light of which the agent acts.
- **Saucer of mud.** Mentioned as the paradigm of a puzzling, untransmissible "want". Platts adds in a footnote that its content is opaque: is the object to be given a saucer of mud, to own one, or to have one at one's disposal?

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- **Anscombe ↔ Aquinas.** He connects the desirability characterisation to Aquinas's "imagined good", via Davidson's "How is Weakness of the Will Possible?".
- **Needs and wants.** One paradigm of a desirability characterisation specifies the object as meeting a need or want (a lack) that the agent recognises. Such characterisations end the "why do you want it?" questioning and purport to be **objective**.
- **The "halfway house" against "fair nonsense".** An enquirer into an alien culture may come to share a ritual desire, seeing the activity "as somehow desirable", before being able to give any desirability characterisation of it. Platts holds it is *not* fair nonsense to credit some understanding here. Many of our own desires, such as substitution activities, live in this halfway house.
- A desirability characterisation may be "reached and made intelligible" only with great effort. Failing to understand someone at once does not license treating him as unintelligible.
- **Desire vs drive.** Full human desire requires *some* desirability conception of its object. That conception lets the agent relate to the future and the merely possible and choose deliberately. Platts illustrates this with a contrast between a "drive-soaked" Sisyphus (Taylor's) and his own Sisyphus.
