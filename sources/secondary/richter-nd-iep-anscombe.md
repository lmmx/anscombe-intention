# G. E. M. Anscombe (IEP) — Duncan Richter

## What this source is
The IEP's single entry on Anscombe, written by Duncan Richter (author of *Anscombe's Moral Philosophy*, 2011). Its §4 "Intention" (c. 1,400 words) is an introductory, example-driven exposition aimed at non-specialists; §§5 and 7 apply the intended/foreseen distinction to consequentialism and double effect. The IEP has no separate action-theory entry that discusses *Intention* (checked slugs for action, intention, practical reason, agency, double effect; the IEP Davidson entry does not mention Anscombe).

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

**Why intention matters (entry §4 opening).** If all behaviour were causally determined, the intended/unintended difference might seem not to matter and only consequences would count; Anscombe rejects both determinism and consequentialism. *Intention* aims to illuminate intention and hence intentional action and the difference between rational action and non-rational behaviour. Richter reports Davidson calling it the most important treatment of action since Aristotle.

**Action vs. mere behaviour.** Tossing in one's sleep is behaviour but not something one did; there is no answer to "why?" except a causal one that anyone (a physiologist) might give as well as I could. Action is what it makes sense to ask "Why?" about, meaning: what did the agent take herself to be doing and for what purpose. Questions of intention are thus "questions about the meaning of actions", unlike questions about causes: I may not know why I slept badly, but "I am going to the kitchen to get water" is neither a hypothesis nor a prediction but a statement of what I am doing. Many other descriptions are true (putting one foot in front of the other, making the floorboards creak), but the statement of intention is the most illuminating.

**Known without observation (p. 14 = §8).** Richter quotes that the class of intentional actions is "a sub-class" of the class of "things known without observation". Intentional actions are done for a reason; "Why?" expects an answer giving the significance of the action for the agent, not merely its cause.

**Intention is not a cause.** Causes are distinct from effects, whereas intentional actions are not distinct from the intentions they embody. Richter illustrates with a climber (an example best known from Davidson, though not so attributed here): letting go because one has lost the will to live is suicide; slipping is an accident; being so unnerved by a suicidal thought that one loses grip is still an accident even though caused by the thought or even by an intention. So intentional actions are not behaviour caused by intentions; "the intention is a part or an aspect of the act".

**Intelligibility and the good.** People act for reasons connected with what seems good to them; not everything that seems good is good, but not just anything can *seem* good. If someone's reason is to get all his green books onto the roof, knowing it gives no understanding. Human behaviour is intelligible only relative to a limited range of ideas of the good — Richter's illustration of a link Anscombe sees between metaphysics of action and ethics (cf. §§37–40).

**Against intentions as inner objects/events.** If intentions were mental items causing action like falling dominos, we could be ignorant of them and need a brain scan; but we normally know our intentions. And causes are transferable in a way intentions are not: an infant can be caused to ring the bell that opens the New York Stock Exchange but cannot intend to open trading, lacking the concept.

**Knowledge without looking; the blackboard.** We know what we take ourselves to be doing without observing our behaviour, even when things go wrong. Someone writes "Anscombe" on a freshly washed board to which chalk won't adhere: in a sense she is not writing; yet her sincere "I am writing 'Anscombe' on the board" is the best account of what she is doing. "Trying to write" would mislead (suggesting difficulty); ordinarily we don't try, we just do.

**Shopping list (p. 56 = §32).** Action can go wrong through bad intention or through errors in execution. If what I buy doesn't match my list, I have shopped badly — not a flaw in the list; if the detective's list doesn't match, his list is flawed. Thinking of the list as "in my head", my knowledge of what I am to buy is not falsified by how things turn out (forgetting tomatoes is a mistake in execution, not intention).

**Application to ethics (entry §§5, 7).** Consequentialism (Anscombe's coinage in "Modern Moral Philosophy") denies any significant moral difference between intended and merely foreseen results; Anscombe holds we cannot consistently live as if intention did not matter. On double effect, the objection that one can redescribe an act "in one's mind" ignores that intention is not an inner act "like a kind of whispering under one's breath"; referees can call intentional fouls without mind-reading.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Strongly anti-causal reading: intention is an aspect of the act, not its cause; argued with original examples (climber, infant at the stock exchange) that are Richter's, not Anscombe's.
- Reads practical knowledge as knowledge of "what we take ourselves to be doing" / of our intentions, holding even when execution fails — a weaker, more intention-centred reading than SEP accounts that make practical knowledge knowledge of what actually happens.
- Directly links §§37–38 (desirability) and §32 to ethics and to the rejection of consequentialism.
- Glosses the shopping-list point as "my knowledge … cannot be falsified by how the facts turn out" — close to Anscombe's §45 puzzle but stated without its problematic status (cf. Setiya, who calls Anscombe's position here "elusive").
