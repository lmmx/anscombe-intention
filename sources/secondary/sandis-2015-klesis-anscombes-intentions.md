# Anscombe's Intentions — Constantine Sandis

## What this source is
A short paper (pre-proof c. 6,000 words incl. references) cataloguing every change Anscombe made across four texts: the 1957 *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* paper "Intention"; the 1957 first edition (Blackwell); the 1958 second impression; and the 1963 second edition (reprinted 1966, 1985; the Harvard 2000 reprint is said to be identical to 1963). Sandis notes that no synoptic edition exists and offers the paper as a catalogue. It is the key source in this repo for **edition differences and citation hygiene**.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

**Prologue: a citation problem.** Philosophers treat *Intention* as one unified text, yet commonly quote the 1963 text while citing "1957". Sandis lists offenders and exceptions (footnote 1): many essays in Diamond & Teichman (1979) and Ford et al. (2011), Moran (2004), etc.; Teichmann (2008) cites only 1963; Bayne (2010) says she uses the 2nd-edition numbering but writes "1957"; Davidson cites a non-existent "1959" edition; recent American writers (e.g. Thompson 2008) cite the HUP 2000 reprint. All four translations (German, Spanish, French, Italian) are of 1963, but all except the German omit the page noting the changes between editions; the Spanish wrongly claims to translate 1957.

**I. Before *Intention*: the Aristotelian Society paper.** Anscombe's Introduction says that excerpts, with small modifications, on 'motive', 'intention' and 'mental cause' formed an Aristotelian Society paper delivered 3 June 1957. The overlap is with **§5 and §§10–15**, minus the pages on non-observational knowledge — the article simply asserts that a subject can give the cause of a thought as he can state the place of a pain or the position of his limbs, "not based on observation" (1957a: 176). The "small modifications" are minor (an italic, "i.e."→"e.g." p. 17, "Humeian"→"Humian" p. 16).

**II. First edition → second impression (1958).** Anscombe's note: "a few alterations; the only ones of any significance are on pp. 29, 58, 59, and 61." Apart from p. 29 (§19), all are in the Aristotle discussion (§33).
- **§19, p. 29.** In the passage about a supposed inner "I" (an extra feature) whose occurrence would be irrelevant unless a mechanism let the right one occur because of the agent's knowledge, the 1957 example "the movement of his hand will result in its grasping the hammer" becomes, in 1958, "his muscular contractions will result in his grasping the hammer". Sandis's interpretation: having committed herself to one action under several descriptions (§§23–6), she may have seen that hand-movement and grasping are not really cause and result; moving the example "inward" to muscle contractions avoids that, and suggests she did not regard muscle contractions as identical to outer bodily movements under any description. He relates this to von Wright's action/result distinction and to a process (rather than event) view of bodily movement.

**III. Second edition (1963), small changes in §§2, 6, 17.** Anscombe's note: "small alterations in §§ 2, 6, 17, 33 and 34."
- **§2 (p. 2).** "it" disambiguated to "the intention" in the sentence asking why intention should be essentially connected with the future; the parenthesis about Hebrew traditions altered and "most of" inserted before "the earliest historical traditions" (Sandis guesses she met counter-evidence); "command" replaced by "imperative" in the definition of a command/imperative as a description of a future action cast in a form meant to make the person do it (widening to requests etc.).
- **§6 (p. 11).** "To justify the proposed account … I need to explain" becomes "To clarify the proposed account … I will … explain" — Sandis reads this as a considerable weakening: from defence to clarification.
- **§17 (p. 25).** "has no application" → "is shewn not to apply" — judged purely stylistic.

**IV. Practical reasoning, 1957–1963 (§§33–34).** The main changes; Sandis links them to Anscombe's work on "Thought and Action in Aristotle" (1965) and later papers ("Von Wright on Practical Inference", "Practical Truth").
- **Footnote dropped (1958).** A 1957 footnote (p. 58) saying many practical syllogisms contain no entailments (no defect) but some do, and that Aristotle's grounds for calling these too non-"apodeictic" were not her concern, disappears from all later versions.
- **p. 59 (1958).** "would necessitate the conclusion for someone who accepts the premises" expanded: someone *professing to accept* the order and factual premise in the imperative example must accept its conclusion, as someone *believing* the premises of the categorical example must. Sandis discusses the odd professing/believing asymmetry.
- **p. 61 (1958), last paragraph of §33.** On "insanity" of positive universal practical premises (e.g. needing to eat all dry food one sees): 1958 adds "as practical premises" and that negative premises lead "only to not doing certain things" — relevant to omissions.
- **Opening of §33 (1963).** The 1957/58 opening said practical syllogism, one of Aristotle's best discoveries, "has been lost to modern philosophy through misinterpretation", is taken as an ethical syllogism concluding "I ought to do this", and that Aristotle's denials of its being "apodeictic" are read as a defect. 1963 replaces this: its "true character has been obscured"; it is commonly supposed to be ordinary reasoning to a truth like "I ought to do such-and-such" (with the beggar example). Sandis: emphasis shifts from blaming the non-apodeictic remarks to stating directly what practical reasoning is not.
- **p. 58 (1963).** "a proof – granted the premises" extended with "and saving their inevitable uncertainty or doubtfulness in application".
- **p. 59 (1963), "cause of the mischief".** "not exactly his fault" → "not entirely his fault"; 1963 blames Aristotle's division of reasoning by subject matter (demonstrative = invariable) and adds the **longest new passage**: the John-drives-from-Chartres-to-Paris argument is about something variable yet theoretical, so subject matter does not make reasoning practical; there is "a difference of form" between reasoning leading to action and reasoning to the truth of a conclusion; three types — theoretical syllogism, **idle practical syllogism** ("a classroom example"), and practical syllogism proper whose conclusion is an action, premises "on active service" (pp. 59–60).
- **p. 60 (1963).** The vitamin-X / pigs' tripes example is now prefaced as imitating "one of his classroom examples".
- **§34 opening (p. 61 → 62).** A categorical claim ("there is no reason … to confine the term 'practical reasoning' to [proof-like cases]") becomes a rhetorical question — a softening of tone.
- **Pagination.** Because of the insertion, 1963 pagination from p. 59 onward never realigns with 1957/58; the 1963 edition is almost a page longer. Most people citing "1957" quote 1963/2000 page numbers.

**Interpretive upshot for §45.** Sandis argues the idle vs. proper distinction matters for §45: the man "merely conceiving speculatively how a thing might be done" (p. 82) is idle practical reasoning; combined with "Theophrastus' point" that mistakes can lie in the performance not the judgement, Anscombe's claim that "my knowledge is independent of what actually happens" should be restricted to practical knowledge proper — connected to her later view that practical truth is created in acting ("Practical Truth", 1993).

**Epilogue.** It would be "going much too far" to speak of an early and a late *Intention*, but the practical-reasoning changes are substantial enough to matter to action theory and moral psychology (e.g. whether the conclusion of a practical syllogism is an action or a belief).

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Establishes four textual states (1957 article; 1957; 1958; 1963 = HUP 2000) and lists all significant variants.
- Explains the drift of §33 as moving from a historical diagnosis (Aristotle's "non-apodeictic" remarks) to a formal one ("difference of form"), culminating in the new idle/proper practical-syllogism distinction.
- Reads the §19 example change as evidence about action identity and about muscle contractions not being redescribable as actions.
- Reads the §6 change as a retreat from "justify" to "clarify".
- Uses the idle/proper distinction to limit §45's "knowledge independent of what happens".
- Practical warning: cite 1963/2000 pagination as such; page refs after p. 58 differ by edition.

- §17 — "has no application"→"is shewn not to apply" (1963, stylistic).
- §19 — hand-movement example → muscular contractions (1958).
- §§23–26 — invoked: one action, several descriptions.

- §34 — opening sentence made a rhetorical question (1963); page shift 61→62.
- §45 (p. 82) — "merely conceiving speculatively" read as idle practical reasoning; restricts "knowledge independent of what happens".
