# Anscombe's Intention: A Guide — John Schwenkler

## What this source is

A book-length, section-by-section commentary on *Intention* in OUP's "Oxford Guides to Philosophy" series, aimed (per the publisher) at advanced undergraduates but also at specialists in action theory, moral philosophy and the history of analytic philosophy. It has a contextual Introduction (the Truman degree protest and the state of Oxford moral philosophy), a short "Interpretive Précis", an "Outline of the Text" summarising each section or group of sections, seven commentary chapters with "Summary discussion" sections and "Suggestions for further reading", and a Glossary, Bibliography and Concordance.

**Access caveat.** I have NOT read the body of the book. Everything below comes from: (a) Oxford Academic's book abstract, chapter abstracts and in-chapter section headings (which give § ranges); (b) the extract of the Introduction and Précis shown on Oxford Academic; (c) Chiara Pellegrini's full review in *Universa* (2023), which gives page numbers and several short quotations from the book; (d) the first-page extract of Benjamin Schulz's review in *Philosophical Quarterly* (2021); (e) the publisher's description and endorsements; (f) Schwenkler's 3:16 interview (separate file). ## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation, attributed)

**Framing.** The Précis extract opens by calling *Intention* a philosophical inquiry into the concept in its title — the concept we use to treat some statements as expressions of intention, to classify actions as intentional or not, and to say with what intentions people act. Against the common view that "intention" names a mental state, Schwenkler's Anscombe holds that "the primary use of the concept of intention consists in a distinctive manner of describing what happens in the world" [OUP-extract, Précis]. The book abstract lists the main themes as: intention as a feature of what happens rather than of a mental state; action as a teleological unity; rationalising vs causal explanation; practical vs theoretical reasoning; non-observational knowledge of what one is doing [OUP-abs]. The Introduction begins from the 1956 Truman degree protest and Anscombe's claim that choosing to kill the innocent as a means to one's ends is always murder [OUP-extract, Introduction].

**Ch1 (§§1–4).** Covers the "three headings" under which the topic is introduced, how expressions of intention relate to prediction, what is described in expressing an intention, how expressions of intention are justified differently from estimates of the future, how they set the standard of correctness for the events they describe, and why Anscombe treats intentional action before intention as an inner state [OUP-abs]. Pellegrini reports that the chapter's main claim is that Anscombe's method is *not* behaviourist: she allows the possibility of a "purely interior" intention while taking an action-first approach (p. 3) [Pellegrini].

**Ch2 (§§5–18).** Starts from why Anscombe defines intentional action via "a certain sense of the question 'Why?'"; then the ways that question is refused, each yielding a necessary condition on intentional action (Schwenkler's "three epistemic conditions" C1–C3, §§6–8); then knowledge without observation, mental causality, motive, and the relation of causes to reasons for acting; then whether one can act intentionally "for no reason" or for reasons one cannot identify [OUP-abs]. Two distinctive moves: a comparison with Gilbert Ryle, and an argument *against Davidson's* reading that Anscombe was not an "anti-causalist" about action explanation [OUP-abs]. Pellegrini adds that the chapter separates "reason-giving explanations and explanations that identify the causes of what takes place" (p. 17) [Pellegrini].

**Ch3 (§§19–27).** Anscombe's argument that an action is not intentional in virtue of an "extra feature"; her claim (§§20–21) that intentional action depends on the practices of expressing intention for the future and of stating further intentions; the resulting picture of action as a teleological unity, compared with Davidson's; and the intention/foresight distinction, focused on how foreseen consequences relate to an action differently from what is brought about intentionally [OUP-abs]. Pellegrini quotes the chapter's conclusion that intentional action is a unity "that can be represented by a series of descriptions that relate to one another in an order of means to ends" (p. 90), and that calling an action intentional specifies the kind of description under which it falls rather than adding a feature [Pellegrini].

**Ch4 (§§28–32).** Connects knowledge without observation to Wittgenstein's *Blue Book* on knowing oneself "as subject" and to Anscombe's "The First Person"; sets out the difficulties Anscombe raises for her own view and why she holds that non-observational self-knowledge reaches beyond inner states and immediate bodily movements; then considers how her treatment of contradicting a description of intentional action, and the shopping-list contrast between a list functioning as an order and one recording facts, is meant to address those difficulties [OUP-abs]. Pellegrini: the difficulty concerns descriptions of an action "in terms of what is happening at a distance from the agent and her immediate bodily movements" (p. 95), where distance may be spatial or temporal; Anscombe concedes that background knowledge can be observational without conceding observational knowledge of the action; §§29–30 are rejected escape routes (interiorising the object of knowledge), and §§31–32 begin a solution [Pellegrini].

**Ch5 (§§33–43).** Argues that the premises of a practical syllogism are not meant to *prove* its conclusion; compares Anscombe with R. M. Hare and considers objections; covers the Aristotelian account of practical reasoning as calculation of means to ends, the role of desire, and the "guise of the good" thesis; ends with Anscombe's claim that the practical syllogism displays a means–end order already present in action itself [OUP-abs]. Pellegrini: the practical/theoretical difference is formal, "a difference in the way that the conclusion of a practical inference relates to its premises" (p. 117); following Aristotle, "the conclusion of practical reasoning is an action" (p. 120); desirability is a "formal restriction" on wanting (p. 140); "the premises show the goodness of the action one decides on" (p. 145); the account is of an order, not of psychological "mechanisms from which action results" (p. 148) [Pellegrini].

**Ch6 (§§44–48).** Places "practical knowledge" in relation to Aquinas; uses Aquinas to support the claim that an agent's knowledge of her act is "the cause of what it understands"; argues that Anscombe meant practical knowledge to be **both formal and efficient cause** of its object; then assesses whether she has shown that intentional action is necessarily known without observation — concluding that knowing one's act is **not a strict requirement** for acting intentionally, and that observation plays a different role in self-knowledge than evidence does in observational knowledge [OUP-abs]. Pellegrini: the chapter runs from Aristotle's practical *nous* to Aquinas's notion of a knowledge that is "the measure of its object" (p. 156), then a close reading of §48 [Pellegrini].

**Ch7 (§§49–52).** The relation of intentional to voluntary action; then the closing return to intention for the future, where intention is expressed in a statement about what is going to happen; with a parallel to Wittgenstein on Moore's paradox [OUP-abs]. Pellegrini says these sections confirm the "factualist" element Schwenkler identifies from the start: "in expressing the intention to do something, what a person says is simply that something will happen – namely, that she will do what she says she will" (p. 8) [Pellegrini].

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

1. **Factualism about expression of intention** — an expression of intention is a statement of what will happen (p. 8 via [Pellegrini]); expressions of intention set the standard of correctness for events [OUP-abs Ch1].
2. **Intention is primarily a form of describing what happens**, not a mental state [OUP-extract Précis]; links to §47 ("a *form* of description of events") [3:16].
3. **Not behaviourist** (Ch1) and **not anti-causalist** (Ch2, against Davidson's reading) [Pellegrini][OUP-abs].
4. **Action as teleological unity** via the A–D order (Ch3) [OUP-abs].
5. **Hidden interlocutors**: Ryle (the discussion of motive, within §§9–16; motives proper are §§12–14 per Anscombe's contents) and Hare (practical syllogism, §33 ff.) [Publisher][OUP-abs Ch2, Ch5][3:16].
6. **Thomistic practical knowledge**: formal *and* efficient cause of what it knows (Ch6) [OUP-abs].
7. **Critical verdict**: knowing what one does is not strictly necessary for intentional action; observation can play a role in self-knowledge (Ch6 §§6.4–6.5) [OUP-abs]. (In the 3:16 interview he says he has since given up his earlier view that practical knowledge can be "both practical and perceptual", and that the book gets the foresight case "flatly wrong" — see the separate interview file.)
