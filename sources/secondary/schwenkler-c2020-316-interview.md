# Anscombe's Intention and Related Issues — John Schwenkler (3:16 interview with Richard Marshall)

## What this source is

An extended written interview in Richard Marshall's 3:16 series with John Schwenkler (then editor of the Brains blog), author of *Anscombe's Intention: A Guide* (OUP 2019; see `schwenkler-2019-guide.md`). The first half concerns *Intention*: the hazards of reading it, its historical targets, its debts to Aristotle, Aquinas and Wittgenstein, foresight vs. intention, what intention "is", agential knowledge as practical, and whether agents always know what they intentionally do. It is valuable mainly as the guide author's own précis and **self-correction** of that guide on two points (foresight; perception). It is not itself section-level.

## Structure (relation to the 2019 guide)

Interview, not organised by sections. The only structural pointer to the guide: he says his view on perception and practical knowledge is worked out in its **penultimate chapter** (= Ch. 6, on §§44–48, per `schwenkler-2019-guide.md`), whose details he finds "not totally convincing" even to himself.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

**Why the book is hard; its interlocutors.** Anscombe sets up no context and almost never names contemporary interlocutors — a reflection of 1950s Oxford, where philosophers wrote for each other and assumed shared background. Schwenkler's guide fills these gaps, e.g. reading the long early discussion of **motive** (§§12–15) as "helpfully read as a response to" Gilbert Ryle's *Concept of Mind*, and the later discussion of the **practical syllogism** (from §33) as centred on a criticism of R. M. Hare's *Language of Morals*.

**Hare and Truman.** Hare mattered because Anscombe found his moral philosophy "totally perverse": explicitly consequentialist in the sense of "Modern Moral Philosophy" (the right action = the one producing the best consequences). Schwenkler cites Hare's line that in choosing "it is the effects that determine what I should be doing". As a Catholic, Anscombe held some act-types (deliberate massacre of non-combatants) are simply off the table; she argued contemporary philosophers could not see this not because they were bad at ethics but because their assumptions about action and intention obscured it — which is what the book attacks. (He thinks Hare almost certainly supported Truman's degree; only a tiny number of Oxford faculty backed her motion; he does not know Hare's view of the bombings.)

**Aristotle, Aquinas, Wittgenstein.** Aristotle's influence is evident (also in her 1956 *Mind* paper on future contingents in *De Interpretatione*). Aquinas's is hidden: her 1938 anti-war pamphlet (with Norman Daniel) argues straight from the *Summa*, but in *Intention* Thomas appears only in "a stray footnote", though Thomist ideas run throughout — "another main cause of the initial obscurity". In particular her discussion of **practical knowledge draws heavily on Aquinas's treatment of divine cognition, *Summa* I, q. 14** — a realisation that made much of the text clearer to him. Wittgenstein: her greatest teacher (Anthony Kenny reports her saying she had no idea in her head not put there by Wittgenstein — "surely inaccurate (what about Aquinas?)"); Schwenkler disclaims expertise to assess the influence in detail, and mentions his biographical essay drawing on her notebook of Wittgenstein remembrances (Collegium Institute archive, University of Pennsylvania).

**Foresight vs. intention.** For Anscombe the distinction comes down to what falls within the agent's **practical knowledge**: intentional action is known without observation and its knowledge is "the cause of what it understands" (§48), which is not true of what is merely foreseen. Equivalently (supposed to come to the same thing), only what is intentional falls within the **means–end order** elicited by the reason-seeking "Why?" (§§23–26). **Self-criticism:** the latter formulation is hard to sustain, since for a foreseen side-effect (polluting a river while increasing profits) "Why are you polluting the river?" — "In order to increase profits" can be an appropriate answer; he says his book "gets things flatly wrong" here and he is working to improve on it.
- *Ethical significance:* intentionally killing innocents is wrong in any circumstances, while actions foreseeably causing innocent deaths can be (not necessarily are) justified; double-effect reasoning does not make foreseen side-effects ethically irrelevant.
- *Not itself ethical:* the distinction is used constantly in describing what people do and why, hence belongs to "the philosophy of psychology", to be approached after "banishing ethics totally from our minds" ("Modern Moral Philosophy"). He flags Joshua Knobe's (and others') evidence that ethical considerations pervade how we describe actions as a challenge Anscombeans "have not … done enough so far to address".

**What intention is (§47).** The closest Anscombe comes to a direct answer is §47: "the term 'intention' has reference to a form of description of events", contrasted with intention as "an extra property" of intentional actions "which a philosopher must try to describe". The modern default — intention is a mental state, and intentional actions are those caused by it in the right way — is what she opposes. Schwenkler's formulation of her alternative: we bring the concept to bear whenever we describe what a person (or animal) does in a way that gives her "Why?"-question application — e.g. describing what further someone is doing in doing something more immediate (making an omelette by cracking eggs), or doing something so that something else comes about (attending college to earn a degree).

**Intending is believing.** Asked what it is to intend to do something, he defends (with Berislav Marušić) that to intend to do A is "nothing more than" to believe, *on the basis of practical reasoning*, that one will do A — claiming "a decent claim to fit" with Anscombe's ideas. The attraction: from the first-person perspective deliberation's only concern is what I will actually do; my answer to "What will I do?", given through practical reasoning rather than reflection on evidence, just is my intention.

**Agential knowledge as practical, not perceptual.** From the start he thought readers neglected Anscombe's claim that knowledge of one's actions is distinctive by being *practical* knowledge, not merely knowledge "without observation", and connected this with Richard Moran's *Authority and Estrangement* (first-person distinctiveness lies in rational authority, not special introspective access). In early papers he argued such knowledge could be both practical *and perceptual* (justified by perceiving what one does); "I now think this is mistaken", the mistake being a failure to apprehend the distinctive form of practical reasoning. Example: reasoning that since the bus I need departs at 11:30, I'll leave for the stop by 11:25 — a premise known by observation, but the conclusion (which he identifies with the belief that this is when I'll leave) is reached in order to decide, not predict, so it is not observational knowledge; the same holds for perception's role in knowing what I am doing now. Perception is integral to action and essential to knowing what one does, but not as evidence justifying belief. (This partly retracts the guide's Ch. 6 discussion, §6.5 "Practical knowledge through perception?", per `schwenkler-2019-guide.md`.)

**Do agents always know what they do?** His consistent answer: No. In the normal case one's practical thinking about what one is doing is practical knowledge of it, but there can be exceptional cases of intentional action without knowledge. He admits a barrier he has not addressed: for an Anscombean, "Because she is doing A" can answer why she is doing something else ("Why is Jack peeling bananas?" — "Because he is making banana bread"), and such explanation seems to presuppose knowledge of the reason-fact; so if Jack doesn't know he is making banana bread, that can't be his reason. Set against strong arguments that we don't always know everything we do intentionally, this leaves him in "philosophical aporia".

**Self-knowledge generally.** His slogan that introspection "isn't good for" much: it is unreliable about experience and nearly useless for knowing what one believes or intends, which are not introspectible features of conscious life; he favours Moran's "rationalism" (we know our minds largely through the rational capacities by which we make them up).

**Book recommendation.** *Intention* first — with the admission that much of it was "totally impenetrable" to him at first, and sympathy for those who would rather not make the effort; also Moran, McDowell's *Mind and World*, Thompson's *Life and Action*, and Beth Levin's *English Verb Classes and Alternations* (as a model of investigating concepts through "grammar").

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

1. Historical targets: **Ryle** for the motive sections (§§12–15); **Hare** (and his consequentialism) for the practical-syllogism sections (§33 ff.).
2. Aquinas as hidden source, specifically ST I q. 14 (God's knowledge) for practical knowledge. (Note: Small 2020 locates the "cause of what it understands" phrase Anscombe quotes in §48 at ST I-II q. 3 a. 5 obj. 1 — compatible: one claim concerns the quoted phrase, the other the background doctrine.)
3. **Self-correction on foresight:** foresight vs. intention = inside vs. outside practical knowledge; the "Why?"/means–end formulation fails for side-effects, and his guide was "flatly wrong" there.
4. **Self-correction on perception:** agential knowledge is practical, not perceptual, even where perception plays a role.
5. §47 "form of description of events" as Anscombe's nearest answer to "what is intention?".
6. Intending is believing on practical grounds (with Marušić), presented as Anscombe-friendly.
7. Agents need not always know what they intentionally do — with an acknowledged unresolved difficulty about "because she is doing A" explanations.
8. The foresight/intention distinction belongs to philosophy of psychology, not ethics, though Knobe-type data put pressure on that separation.

## Section pointers
- §§12–15 — motive; read as a reply to Ryle.
- §§22–27 — means–end order elicited by "Why?" as a mark of the intended; side-effect (river-polluting) counterexample; the guide's handling is wrong.
- §25 — (implicitly) foreseen but unintended consequences.
- §28 / §48 — exceptional intentional action without knowledge; the normal case is practical knowledge.
- §32 — practical knowledge as distinctive (not just "without observation").
- §33 ff. — practical syllogism; criticism of Hare's consequentialism.
- §§44–48 — subject of the guide's penultimate chapter, whose perceptual-knowledge line he now partly retracts.
- §47 — "intention" refers to a form of description of events, not an extra property.
- §48 — practical knowledge "the cause of what it understands"; Thomistic source (ST I q. 14).
