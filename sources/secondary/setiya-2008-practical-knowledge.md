# Practical Knowledge — Kieran Setiya (with "Practical Knowledge Revisited")

## What this source is
An influential analytic paper that starts from Anscombe's claim that we know what we are doing intentionally "without observation". Its question is how that knowledge can be epistemically justified. The answer it gives is: through **knowledge how**.

- It is not primarily exegesis. Anscombe (together with Hampshire, *Thought and Action*) supplies the datum, and Setiya then argues with Velleman, Grice, Davidson and Ryle.
- The 2009 reply answers Sarah Paul's critique, "Intention, Belief, and Wishful Thinking: Setiya on 'Practical Knowledge'" (*Ethics* 119, 2009: 546–57). That critique is not in this repo. Paul's objections appear here only as Setiya reports them.

## Account of Intention
- **Definition of intentional action.** Setiya opens from Anscombe's definition of intentional action as that to which "a certain sense of the question 'Why?' is given application" (p. 9, §5). He takes from it that what we do for reasons we do intentionally.
- **Knowledge in intentional action.** Anscombe's "knowledge without observation" (pp. 13–15, §8) is glossed as knowing *spontaneously*, not on the basis of empirical evidence. Setiya notes her comparison with knowledge of the position of one's limbs (pp. 49–50, §28). He finds that comparison unhelpful, because Anscombe also says knowledge of position is "speculative" or receptive while knowledge of action is not.
- **"Not inferential" as the key claim.** For Setiya the crucial epistemic claim is that knowledge of what one is doing is "not the conclusion of an inference" (a phrase from Hampshire). It is not inferred from sufficient prior evidence.
- **Anscombe's objections to inferential models, endorsed:**
  - Against inference from bodily movement: it is an error to push what is known "back and back" (p. 53, §§29–30). Example: keeping an object level with a pumping contraption (p. 54, §30).
  - Against Grice's inference from knowledge of one's will: Anscombe calls it "a mad account" (p. 52, §29). Setiya supplies a fuller argument for this.
- **Expression of intention.** Anscombe treats "I am going to φ" as the expression of intention for the future (p. 1). Setiya cites this as support for the view that the expression of intention is also the expression of belief.
- **Where he departs from Anscombe (n. 28).** On his reading, Anscombe treats intention and belief as *contrasting* states: when one fails to do what one intends, "the mistake is not one of judgement but of performance" (p. 57, §32). Setiya prefers to say the agent makes a mistake of judgement *and* of performance. He notes Moran (2004) responds similarly.
- **Knowledge how.** Setiya sees Anscombe as gesturing at the connection between practical knowledge and knowing how (pp. 88–89, §48, n. 44), "though without much development".

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- **The belief principle.** Davidson's carbon-copier ("Agency", p. 50) shows that one can act intentionally without believing one is succeeding. Setiya therefore qualifies the principle:
  - **(B)** If A is doing φ intentionally, A believes he is doing it, or else he is doing φ by doing other things in which he does believe.
  - **(B\*)** For cases like recovering from paralysis, the condition is a degree-of-confidence version.
- **Cognitivism.** The necessity of belief in intentional action shows that **intention involves belief**. It does not consist only in belief, because intention also has a motivational role. This is why knowledge in intention cannot be inferred from knowledge of one's intention: the belief is constituted by the intention.
- **The wishful-thinking puzzle.** If intention involves belief, forming an intention looks like forming a belief without prior evidence. Grice called this "licensed wishful thinking".
- **Against Velleman.** Velleman (*Practical Reflection*) says such beliefs are justified because they will be supported by evidence once formed. Setiya rejects this with Dorr's "benevolent spirit" case (shared with Langton 2004).
- **Setiya's answer.** One is justified in forming the intention-belief that one is (or will be) φ-ing iff the decision exercises **knowledge how to φ** and one knows one is able to φ (in a simple conditional sense).
  - Revised in 2009: knowing how is a capacity to *execute* intentions, not to form them. It makes the truth of the intention-belief non-accidental.
  - Knowing how is not reducible to knowing that.
- **Falvey and Thompson.** Setiya credits Falvey (2000) and Thompson's "Naive Action Theory" with blurring the line between intending and doing through the "broadness" of the progressive. He argues this cannot explain knowledge in *prospective* intention, which does not verify itself (n. 39).
- **Reply to Paul (2009).** Paul accepts Setiya's case against inference and against Velleman. She rejects (a) the belief requirement (B/B\*) and (b) the epistemic role of knowing how. Setiya concedes that "partial intention" was a bad phrase. He defends B\* against her "alien hand" and breathing cases. He says that without a skeptical argument the question "how is such knowledge possible?" lacks clear content.
- **Velleman and efficient cause.** In 2009, n. 36, Setiya says Velleman (2007) misreads Anscombe as holding that an intention is knowledge if it *efficiently* causes its truth-makers. For Anscombe the "cause of what it understands" is a formal cause, and only for formal descriptions of executed intentions (pp. 87–88). He cites her "The Causation of Action" for the denial that being done intentionally is a causal relation between act and intention.

## Positions on key debates
- **Knowledge without observation:** Accepted, with qualifications (carbon-copier, paralysis, degrees of belief). It is re-described as *non-inferential* knowledge "constituted by our will".
- **§48 "cause of what it understands":** Not central in 2008. The 2009 reply reads it as formal, not efficient, causation, restricted to descriptions that are formally descriptions of executed intentions.
- **Davidson / causalism:** Takes Davidson's carbon-copier seriously as a counterexample to unqualified knowledge. Lists Davidson ("Intending" pp. 91–94), Bratman and Mele as critics of the belief condition. Setiya's own framework is psychological and broadly compatible with causal theories (contrast the Pittsburgh–Chicago readers).
- **Naive action theory:** Thompson's progressive-based solution is noted but judged insufficient for prospective knowledge.
- **Intention for the future:** Explicitly covered. Intending to φ involves believing one will φ, and this is "knowledge in prospective intention". The same epistemology (knowledge how) applies.
- **Cognitivism:** The paper is a central defence of (moderate) cognitivism: intention *involves* belief. The opponents are Davidson, Bratman, Mele and Paul.
- **The "Why?" question:** Used only as the definition of intentional action.
