# Anscombe on Practical Knowledge — Kieran Setiya

## What this source is
A short, explicitly exegetical chapter (Setiya says the essay is "mainly exegetical", n. 4). It sets out to correct two widespread misreadings of Anscombe's "practical knowledge":

1. that practical knowledge is *always* "the cause of what it understands";
2. that practical knowledge covers only the present progressive ("what I am doing"), not what I will do or have done.

Setiya quotes Anscombe at length. All page references are to the 2nd edition (Blackwell 1963), and they match the HUP 2000 reprint. The chapter also places itself in the post-2011 literature, especially the essays in Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland (eds.), *Essays on Anscombe's Intention* (2011).

## Account of Intention
- **The thesis.** Anscombe holds that we have "knowledge without observation" (§8) of our intentional actions. This knowledge is not perceptual, not proprioceptive and not inferential. It is "knowledge in intention" of what one is doing or is going to do. Setiya's anchor passage is from p. 50 (§28): when one has knowledge or correct opinion that doing ABC brings about Z, doing Z can be intentional, and then one does not know by observation that one is doing Z. Setiya reads Anscombe as holding that it is a *condition* of doing A intentionally that one have practical knowledge of doing A.
- **The "cause of what it understands" passage (§48, p. 87) is restricted.** Anscombe draws up a two-column list of descriptions (§47, pp. 84–85). The right-hand column holds descriptions that "can only be voluntary or intentional", such as paying, hiring and marrying. These are "formally descriptions of executed intentions" (p. 87). The left-hand column holds descriptions like kicking or dropping, which can apply to goings-on that are not intentional. Setiya stresses that the §48 conclusion has two conditions:
  - (a) the description of the event is of a type to be formally the description of an executed intention;
  - (b) the event actually is the execution of an intention.

  If practical knowledge were always the cause of what it understands, condition (a) would be redundant. So the causality holds only where the content of the knowledge is formally the description of an executed intention.
- **What kind of cause.** Practical knowledge is the *formal* cause of its object, not the efficient cause, in that it "forms the essence" of its object (p. 159 of the chapter). You cannot be marrying unless you are doing so intentionally. Practical knowledge of marrying therefore partly constitutes the marrying.
- **The basic case.** Descriptions that answer the "Why?" question are formally descriptions of executed intentions: "doing A in order to do B", "doing A because q", "for no particular reason". This follows from §47's claim that "intentional" refers to a *form of description* of events. So in every intentional action the agent has practical knowledge of a description of this form, and that knowledge is the cause of what it understands. Other practical knowledge (e.g. that I am sliding on ice) rests on this basic knowledge but is not itself causal.
- **Against the "extra feature" view.** Anscombe's target in §§47–48 is the view that being intentional is "a mere extra feature of events whose description would otherwise be the same" (p. 88). That view would analyse the extra feature as caused by belief and desire or by intention.
- **Practical knowledge and knowing how.** Setiya cites p. 88 (§48): the exercise of the relevant capacity is "nothing but the doing or supervising" of the operations. He reads this as linking practical knowledge to knowledge how.
- **Scope in time.** Practical knowledge is not confined to the progressive:
  - *Future.* §2 frames expressions of intention as statements later called true or false. §52 (p. 92) says that if someone else knows my walk "is not going to happen", what he knows is the very thing I said was going to happen.
  - *Past.* In §25 Anscombe moves without comment from "Why are you moving your arm?" to "Why did you replenish the water-supply with poisoned water?"
  - *Completed deeds.* "Now I press button A" (p. 57, §32) only makes sense as a mistake once the wrong button *has been* pressed. §45 (p. 82) begins with "knowledge of what is done", such as a house built or words written. Writing with the eyes closed (p. 53) gives knowledge of what *has been* written.
- **Against interiorising.** The formula "I do what happens" (p. 52, §29) and the protest against pushing the content of intention "back and back" (p. 53) count against restricting practical knowledge. Such a restriction would make "what happens" an object only of theoretical knowledge. In the same spirit, the hole in the pipe (p. 56) can falsify what the pumper says.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- The causality of practical knowledge is **conditional and content-dependent**: sometimes, not always, the cause of what it understands.
- What makes practical knowledge *practical* is its **source**, not its content or causality. It is "the exercise of knowledge how" and is subject to mistakes of *performance* (pp. 56–57).
- Setiya names readers who make the first mistake: Moran (2004), Stoutland's "Summary" (2011), McDowell (2011) in passing, and Rödl (2011).
- He names readers who make the second (progressive-only) mistake: Stoutland, Rödl, Thompson (2011, practical knowledge "progressive, imperfective, *in medias res*") and Falvey (2000, on the "broadness" of the progressive).
- **Safety.** On Setiya's gloss, the "failure to execute intentions is necessarily the rare exception" (p. 87) means that what one intends must not be true by accident. He puts this in terms of safety, and says it needs neither reliabilism nor efficient causation (contrast Velleman 2007).
- Footnote 12 discusses an alternative reading, developed in Ford 2015 ("The Arithmetic of Intention"). On that reading, action verbs have an "intentional" use analogous to "stone" as a count noun. Setiya argues it makes condition (a) or (b) redundant.

## Positions on key debates
- **Knowledge without observation:** Accepted as Anscombe's thesis (§8). It is non-observational and non-inferential, and it is knowledge of what happens in the world, not of something inner.
- **Practical knowledge as "cause of what it understands" (§48):** Formal cause, and only where condition (a) holds. In the basic case (descriptions that answer "Why?") it always holds. Knowledge of perfective facts and of non-intentional descriptions is not causal.
- **Davidson / causalism:** Davidson ("Agency" 1971; "Intending" 1978) and Bratman are named as the main critics of Anscombe's principle (n. 2). The "extra feature" theory Anscombe opposes includes belief–desire and intention-causal analyses. Setiya himself elsewhere (2007) defends a version that he groups with Velleman's (n. 11).
- **Naive action theory:** Rejects Thompson's (2008, 2011) restriction of practical knowledge to the imperfective/progressive.
- **Intention for the future (§§1–4, 50–52):** Anscombe allows practical *foreknowledge* (§2; §52 p. 92). Against Thompson, the content of prospective knowledge is that I will φ, not merely that I am "going to" φ in a failure-tolerant sense. But practical foreknowledge is not required for future intentional action.
- **The "Why?" question:** Central to the solution. Descriptions answering the special "Why?" (§5, p. 9) are formally descriptions of executed intentions.
