# Intention (SEP) — Kieran Setiya

## What this source is
The SEP thematic entry on intention, organised around (a) how to unify the three "guises" of intention and (b) how intention relates to evaluation and belief. It is not an Anscombe exposition, but Anscombe frames the whole entry and recurs as the inspiration for one camp (non-mental-state, "naïve action theory", knowledge in intention). Citations are to the 1963 2nd edition by page, with one § reference (§47). Below I extract only the Anscombe-relevant material.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

**Opening frame (Anscombe p. 1 = §1).** Philosophical perplexity begins with intention's three guises: intention for the future, the intention with which one acts, and intentional action. Setiya quotes Anscombe that it is implausible the word is equivocal across these, and that the temptation to speak of "different senses" shows we are "pretty much in the dark" about the concept. The "principal task" of the philosophy of intention is to find the unity of the three forms. Theories are sorted by whether they take intention to be a mental state that explains intentional action or not.

**1. Intending as doing.** Davidson's 1963 view reduced intention-with-which to primary reasons; his 1978 "Intending" admitted irreducible "pure intending", and most later work follows him in treating prospective intention as a mental state and explaining the other guises by it. Philosophers "inspired by Anscombe" resist this. Setiya's evidence: Anscombe denies a sharp distinction between "I am doing A" and "I am going to do A" as answers to "Why are you doing B?" (pp. 39–40 = §23), and she does not treat intention for the future as needing further explanation once intentional action and intention-with-which are understood (pp. 90–4 = §§50–52). So "Anscombe appears to solve the problem of unity without acknowledging intention as a mental state." Setiya then sets out the neo-Anscombean development (Thompson 2008; Moran & Stone 2009; Falvey 2000): intending to do A is already being embarked on doing A (openness of the progressive; "Kieran is visiting the zoo next Thursday"); arguments from the action-directed object of intention and from the unity of "naïve" and "sophisticated" rationalization. He objects: preparatory steps vs. beginning; intentions requiring no steps; mistakes such as a wrong turn when intending the shortest route; and logically complex intentions (negative, disjunctive, conditional).

**2. Intention in action.** One way to unify is to explain intentional action through intention-with-which, treating teleology ("A in order to B") as primitive and non-causal. Difficulty: idle behaviour done "for no particular reason" (p. 25 = §17), and the fact that chains of reasons must end. Wilson and Ginet's de re intention is offered as a fix. Against mental-state accounts there is a circularity worry (the content of intention includes intentionality); Setiya notes Anscombe "apparently" holds (§47) that for some act-types — paying, hiring, marrying — it is essential to doing them that one does them intentionally. On causation: Anscombe ("The Causation of Action", 1983, p. 95) denies that being done in execution of an intention is a causal relation. When she calls practical knowledge "the cause of what it understands" she means **formal, not efficient** cause — such knowledge is essential to the action when the description is "formally the description of an executed intention" (pp. 87–8 = §48). Setiya lists a dozen conflicting interpretations (Hursthouse 2000; Vogler 2002; Moran 2004; … Campbell 2018). On deviant causal chains, Anscombe (1989, pp. 110–11, "Von Wright on Practical Inference") is listed among those who think the problem hopeless for causal theories.

**3. Intention and the good.** It is consensus that intending entails wanting in the motivational sense whose "primitive sign … is trying to get" (p. 68 = §36). (Anscombe otherwise features little in this section.)

**4. Intentions as plans.** Bratman-style planning theory is said not to explain why acting on a ground — i.e. the applicability of Anscombe's question "Why?" (p. 9 = §5) — requires intention.

**5. Intention and belief.** The idea that intention involves belief is widespread even outside Anscombe's influence; Anscombe is cited for the point that the ordinary expression of intention for the future is "I am going to do A" (p. 1), which looks like an assertion. Setiya surveys cognitivist views (Velleman, Harman, Davis, Grice) and disjunctive "knowledge in intention" views (Rödl, McDowell, Marcus). Then:
- **"Anscombe's own view"**: a *normative* contrast between intention and ordinary belief: if one fails to act as one intends (not through false belief about means), the mistake is "not one of judgment but of performance" (pp. 56–7 = §32). Intention sets the standard of success for what one does (cites Frost 2014; Setiya 2016a; Campbell 2018). And there is "a difference of form between reasoning leading to action and reasoning for the truth of a conclusion" (p. 60 = §33): intention is justified by practical, not theoretical, reasoning (pp. 57–62). This is where Anscombe "differs most sharply" from Velleman, for whom practical reasoning is a kind of theoretical reasoning ("cognitivism about practical reason", Bratman).
- **Epistemic objection.** If forming an intention involves coming to believe one is doing/will do A, what entitles us? Not evidence. That is why Anscombe calls practical knowledge "knowledge without observation", excluding inference as well as observation (p. 50 = §28). Setiya says Anscombe (and Velleman) concede that knowledge in intention often rests partly on observation; the claim is that it goes beyond what observation or inference supports. Grice's "licensed wishful thinking" objection is presented. Self-verification answers are said to deflate practical knowledge; Anscombe warns against the "false avenue of escape" on which I "do" whatever I think I am doing (p. 52 = §29) and seems to allow practical knowledge of what I will do or have done (Moran 2004; Haase 2018).
- **"Anscombe's position on the matter is elusive."** Perhaps practical knowledge is exempt from evidential requirements because a false object means a mistake of performance not judgement (pp. 4–5 = §2; pp. 56–7 = §32). Would it then be knowledge of what is not the case? Anscombe "may seem to suggest as much" (p. 82 = §45), but Setiya says it is unclear whether she accepts or merely entertains this.
- **Closing.** Part of the aim of *Intention* was "to break the Cartesian prejudice" against self-knowledge of what is happening in the world; if she failed, she set the task for future work.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Presents *Intention* as offering a unity of the three guises without positing intention as a mental state (via §23 and §§50–52), and the Thompson/Moran-Stone "intending as being embarked" view as its contemporary heir — then criticises that heir.
- Reads practical knowledge as "cause of what it understands" in the sense of formal, not efficient, cause (§48).
- Identifies Anscombe's distinctive contribution in the belief debate as *normative* (mistake in performance; practical vs. theoretical form of reasoning), not as a cognitivist identification of intention with belief.
- Treats Anscombe as allowing that practical knowledge partly rests on observation and as possibly extending to past/future acts.
- Openly calls her position on the epistemology of practical knowledge "elusive", flagging §45 ("knowledge of what is not the case"?) as unresolved.
- Uses "knowledge in intention", "naïve rationalization" etc. — terminology from later literature, not Anscombe's.
