# Anscombe on Action and Practical Knowledge — Will Small

## What this source is

A handbook chapter asking whether Anscombe's "practical knowledge" (the agent's knowledge of her own intentional actions — "agential knowledge") depends on skill/know-how, and what an Anscombean view implies about know-how. To answer, Small first gives a concise, well-sourced sketch of the book's account of agential knowledge from §5 to §48, with references by section number to the 1963 2nd edition. The first half of the chapter is therefore a good orientation to the arc §§5–8 → §§23–26 → §§28–33 → §42 → §48 → §52. The second half (know-how, intellectualism, Dreyfus, Wiggins) is Small's own argument.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

### Framing
*Intention* is foundational for analytic action theory (conceptual links between action and reasons, explanation and practical reasoning; actions intentional "under descriptions"). Less influential until the twenty-first-century revival was its claim that **practical knowledge** must be central to any account of intentional action. This is not Rylean know-how (standing general knowledge) but the agent's knowledge of her particular intentional actions. Anscombe introduces it negatively — non-observational (§8) — and at the culmination (§48) replaces that with a positive characterisation: practical knowledge, which "modern philosophy has blankly misunderstood" (§32), and for which she adopts Aquinas's formula that practical knowledge is "the cause of what it understands", unlike speculative knowledge "derived from the objects known" (§48, citing *ST* I-II q. 3 a. 5 obj. 1).

### Agential knowledge as non-observational (§§5–8, 17–18)
- Anscombe's aim is to "outline the area of intentional actions" (§18). Small warns against the popular attribution that actions are intentional iff done for reasons: (a) §5's "Why?" criterion leaves room — endorsed with qualifications in §§17–18 — for intentional action done for no reason (the question applies with a negative answer; cf. "None" in my pocket, §17, vs. "I don't have any pockets"); (b) more importantly, §5 itself warns that defining action via "reasons for acting" is circular (why a start or gasp is not an action while sending for a taxi is).
- So she proceeds *negatively*: responses that neither answer "Why?" positively nor negatively but show it doesn't apply. Two key ones: "I didn't know I was X-ing" (§6; Jones sawing Smith's plank) and "I knew I was doing that, but only because I observed it" (§8; Small's example: noticing that walking back and forth opens an automatic door).
- Non-observational knowledge is *not* the kernel of agential knowledge. Many things are known without observation (maths, one's own mind, limb positions — §8), including some of the very things she contrasts with intentional action: the jerk on falling asleep (Small cites §7) and involuntary actions like jumping at the crocodile's bark (§8). For intentional X-ing, one knows without observation *that* and *why* one is X-ing, where "why" answers "Why?" in the special sense; "because of the crocodile's bark" or "because I saw a face at the window" answer a different sense of "Why did you jump?", revealing the jump to be unintentional. This negative specification yields only a "rough outline"; the rest of the book develops the positive account from which non-observationality should follow.

### The object of agential knowledge (§§23–26, 29–30, 32, 42, 52)
- It is what is done intentionally — e.g. painting the wall yellow — not merely what I intend or try to do (a fact about my mind) nor merely what I do with my body to bring that about; Anscombe "strikingly" argues this in §§29–30.
- Such doings have a rich means–end structure — Anscombe's **A–D order** (§§23–26): doing A in order to do B, B to C, C to D. Iterated "Why?" moves A→D; the corresponding means-seeking **"How?"** (§26) moves D→A.
- This is the same order revealed by practical reasoning; her interest is in the "order which is there whenever actions are done with intentions", not in actual mental processes — it would be very rare to go through all the steps (§42; also Anscombe 1989). There may have been real deliberation about how to redecorate, none about opening the paint can; still the agent knows the whole order. So she knows *what* she is doing, *how* (B by doing A), and *why* (B because doing C).
- Agential knowledge is knowledge "in intention" (§32): its vehicle is not a mental state distinct from the intention. It extends to what one is *going* to do: what is known is a happening in material reality, not a psychological fact. Small cites §52: if I say I am going for a walk and someone else knows this won't happen, it would be absurd to deny that what he knows won't happen is the very thing I said would. Others can know the same fact "in belief" (perception, inference, testimony). Locating practical reason *in action*, not in a mind behind action, is a distinctive Anscombean feature.

### Agential knowledge as practical (§§32, 33, 48)
- The culmination (§48): it is the agent's knowledge of what he is doing that gives the description under which what goes on is the execution of an intention, and this is practical knowledge — practical in *form* (cf. §33), relating to its object differently from theoretical knowledge, which "is judged as such by being in accordance with the facts", the facts being prior (§32).
- Critics (Velleman 1989: "causally perverse… epistemically mysterious"; Paul 2009) offer deflationary alternatives. Small's reading (after Moran 2004): agential knowledge is the **formal cause** of what it understands — the agent's knowing what she is doing is *constitutive* of its being intentional action. The practical reasoning that unifies the events into one teleological course of action thereby constitutes her knowledge of that order. §48 says this means more than that knowledge (or an idea of doing such-and-such) is observed to be a necessary condition of the results (as an efficient-cause reading would have it); without it, what happens would not fall under the description "execution of intentions". Hence agential knowledge is non-observational *because* practical.

### Does agential knowledge depend on know-how? (§28 vs. §48)
- **Tension.** (T1) §28 (p. 50): the topic of an intention may be a matter of knowledge or mere opinion (observation, hearsay, superstition, even no foundation); if one knows or believes that doing ABC brings about Z, one can intend Z in doing ABC, and if the belief is correct, doing Z is intentional and not known by observation — suggesting true belief about means suffices, without know-how. (T2) §48 (p. 89): "practical knowledge" is not confined to specialised skills; intentional action always presupposes "knowing one's way about" the matters described, and this knowledge is exercised in the action.
- Small favours T2: it comes at the culmination, after practical knowledge (§32) and the practical reasoning it presupposes (§33) are introduced; T1 precedes them.
- Independent argument: agential knowledge must accord with the facts, though we check it by asking whether the facts accord with the claim, not vice versa (the §32 contrast). "She is painting the wall yellow" can be true while she is asleep, at the store, priming in white or painting green by mistake (progressive/imperfective). If she is doing A, success must be no accident; a non-completion needs special explanation, completion does not (Rödl 2012; Small 2012). So doing A intentionally requires knowing how to do A, which also makes her belief non-accidentally true. Answers: yes, metaphysically and epistemically.
- Note 11: §45 seems to say she could have agential knowledge that she is writing "I am a fool" on the blackboard though she is not; Small calls this "a mis-step" (refs. Moran 2004, Haddock 2011, McDowell 2013).
- Note 9: Davidson's carbon-copier challenges the necessity of agential knowledge (responses: Thompson 2011; Small 2012).

### Commitments about know-how (§48, §33)
- §48 (p. 88), T3: a man has practical knowledge who knows how to do things — but not enough, since one could lecture on it yet be helpless at doing it; practical knowledge is a general capacity whose exercise is the doing or supervising of operations, not mere effects (like reciting the alphabet), being formally subject to "Why?" and displaying the A–D order. Suggests anti-intellectualism, but compatible with some intellectualisms (Pavese); incompatible with Dreyfus (who denies that exercises of know-how are intentional and known).
- Small's proposal: a "bifurcationist" view (Wiggins) — propositional means-knowledge and intelligent practical capacity are both know-how; agential knowledge and know-how are both *species* of practical knowledge. Since practical knowledge can only be understood via practical reasoning (§33), and means-knowledge figures in genuine (not "idle", classroom) practical reasoning only if practicable, there must be non-derivatively practicable **basic know-how**, not of the form "___ is a way to do A" (cf. §30's physiologist's experiment on bodily skill). Open question: how basic actions, lacking the means–end order (§42), are agentially known.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Non-observationality is a negative *outline*, not the core; the core is practical knowledge (§48).
- The object of agential knowledge is worldly, teleologically structured activity (present and prospective), not inner states or bodily movements (§§29–30, 52).
- "Why?" and "How?" traverse the A–D order in opposite directions (§26).
- Practical knowledge as **formal** cause (with Moran), against efficient-cause and deflationary readings.
- §45's apparent non-factivity is a "mis-step" (contrast Holton, who argues Anscombe does not actually say this).
- §28 vs. §48 tension resolved in favour of §48: intentional action presupposes know-how.
- Anscombe is not simply an "anti-causalist" (note 8): she was as critical of modern conceptions of causation as of causal action theory (cites §5 and "Causality and Determination", 1971).
