# Davidson's "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" — Jeff Speaks (2004 seminar notes)

## What this source is

A short seminar handout on Davidson's 1963 paper, written by Jeff Speaks for the same McGill course on the will and action for which he wrote his Anscombe guide ([Speaks 2009](speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md), first version dated 8 Sept 2004). This handout is dated a week later (14 Sept 2004). It has three parts: Davidson's argument for the causal theory; his replies to traditional objections; and deviant causal chains, drawing on Davidson's later "Freedom to Act" and on Setiya's "Explaining Action". Several sub-headings are discussion prompts with no text under them, such as "Difficulty with the idea of logical relations" and "The analogy with the causal theory of perception". So the notes show what Speaks took the key issues to be more than they show his view on each.

It is useful here because it is the companion to Speaks's Anscombe guide and shows the causal-theory frame in which he read *Intention*. It is not a commentary on Anscombe. She is mentioned once.

## Account (faithful paraphrase of how Davidson's paper is presented)

### 1. Intentional action and action for a reason
- Davidson's topic is reasons for action rather than intentional action. Speaks links the two through an equivalence that "many take" to hold: *A φs intentionally ≡ A φs for a reason*. If it holds, an account of acting for a reason gives an account of acting intentionally. (Speaks does not attribute this equivalence to Anscombe. It is close to her §5 criterion, but compare her §§17–18 on "for no particular reason".)
- Davidson's two theses are: **(1)** a reason for φing is a pro-attitude towards actions of some kind plus a belief that φing is of that kind, which together make up a **primary reason**; **(2)** primary reasons are the **causes** of actions.
- Speaks notes, as a discussion point, the distinction between the nature of reasons and the nature of *having* something as one's reason.

### 2. Primary reasons (thesis 1)
- This rests on examples rather than a deductive argument. In the **burglar** case, the agent flips the switch and turns on the light intentionally, but alerts the prowler unintentionally. It seems "almost impossible" not to locate the difference in what he wanted and what he believed or knew. So the difference between intentional and unintentional acts lies in the agent's beliefs and desires. The same doing is intentional under one description and not under another; Speaks does not make this point explicitly.
- Prompts with no text: why we often cite only a pro-attitude or only a belief in rationalising, and Davidson's reply to the claim that some actions are done "for no reason".

### 3. Primary reasons are causes (thesis 2): the "because" argument
- The main argument is that there is "no real alternative". Speaks quotes Davidson's central passage. A person can have a reason and act, "and yet this reason not be the reason why he did it". What is central is that the agent acted *because* he had the reason. Any non-causal "justification" account stays "as dark as the notion of reason" until it accounts for "the force of that 'because'".
- Prompt: the putative non-causal alternatives Davidson rejects. These are that giving reasons describes the action, puts it in context, or places it in a pattern, and "non-causal bringing about". Speaks does not say who held them.

### 4. Status: only necessary conditions
- Speaks stresses that the paper supplies **necessary** conditions only. Combining the equivalence with "A φs for a reason → (for some F) A has a pro-attitude to F-actions, believes her φing is F, and this belief and desire cause her to φ" gives only a one-way conditional for intentional action. Davidson is "explicit" about this (footnote 5 of the reprint).
- Speaks thinks Davidson had **deviant causal chains** in mind. To make the account constitutive, add "cause … *in the right way*". The difficulty is then to say what "in the right way" means.

### 5. The "logical connection" argument
Speaks calls this the most prominent objection of the 1950s and early 1960s. Reasons are conceptually connected to actions, but causes and effects must be distinct events. He presents Davidson's replies to three versions:
1. **Redescription.** To cite a reason is to *redescribe* the action, so there is one event under two descriptions, not two events. *Reply:* that one event can be redescribed in terms of another does not make them identical. A pain can be described as the event caused by the dentist's drilling without being the drilling. (This is where "one action, many descriptions" enters the notes.)
2. **Logical relations.** Reasons and actions are logically related, and causally related events cannot be. *Reply:* what is the logical relation? There seem to be no obvious entailments. A prompt notes a difficulty with the very idea of logical relations between events.
3. **Analytic vs. empirical.** Reason statements are analytic and causal statements empirical. *Reply:* there is a problem with calling reason claims analytic. Davidson's point is quoted: whether a causal statement is true depends on *what* events are described, and whether it is analytic depends on *how* they are described.

### 6. Privileged access (non-observational knowledge)
- This is the only place Anscombe is named. Speaks says that, "as Anscombe pointed out", privileged access seems to extend to which intentional actions we are performing. Davidson adds, "correctly", that we usually also know our *reasons* without evidence or observation. Speaks quotes Davidson: though one may be wrong, one "usually" has "no evidence and make[s] no observations".
- The puzzle is how such knowledge can be knowledge of a *causal* relation. Speaks finds Davidson's reply thin. Davidson says there is no reason to suppose they are incompatible: knowledge that is not inductive is not therefore non-causal, since one can be "persuaded without direct inductive evidence" of a causal relation. Speaks judges that this "does nothing to make this kind of self-knowledge intelligible".
- Speaks does not connect this to Anscombe's own "mental causes", causes known without observation (§§9–11). The notes do not discuss mental causes in her sense.

### 7. Deviant causal chains
- If matching belief-and-desire causation is meant as necessary *and* sufficient, the **climber** (Davidson, "Freedom to Act") is a counterexample. The want and belief so unnerve him that he loosens his hold, yet he "never chose" to and did not do it intentionally.
- Prompts: Davidson's "modest" response, the analogy with the causal theory of perception, and a division of labour between philosophy and empirical science. Speaks asks whether this leaves Davidson's "because" "as unexplained as that of his opponents".
- A "less modest" response, from "Freedom to Act" and Setiya's "Explaining Action", divides basic from non-basic actions. Non-deviance for basic actions is continued guidance; for non-basic actions it is causation according to a plan.

## Distinctive claims

- The "because" argument is Davidson's main support for thesis (2), and it argues by elimination: there is "no real alternative".
- Davidson's analysis is explicitly only necessary-conditional. Making it constitutive needs "in the right way", which is the deviant-chains problem.
- Against the redescription version of the logical connection argument: describing one event in terms of another does not make them identical.
- Davidson's handling of privileged access is weak. It shows non-inductive causal knowledge is possible, but not how self-knowledge of reasons-as-causes is intelligible.
- An open challenge: whether the causal "because", once qualified by "in the right way", is any better off than the anti-causalist's "because".

## Positions on key debates

- **Reasons as causes (Anscombe/Davidson).** Speaks sets out Davidson's causalism sympathetically and does not present it as a reply to Anscombe. Read with his Anscombe guide, which argues that §§9–11 deny only that reasons are *mental* causes, Speaks seems to see less direct opposition between the two than the "opposed poles" reading in [debates.md §3](../../debates.md) does. This is an inference from the pair of handouts, not something this handout says.
- **One action, many descriptions.** This appears only in the reply to the redescription argument. It sits alongside Anscombe's §§6, 23–26, but the notes do not connect them.
- **Non-observational knowledge.** Speaks credits Anscombe with the action case and Davidson with extending it to reasons. He treats the resulting puzzle (non-observational knowledge of a causal relation) as unsolved by Davidson.
- **Deviant causal chains.** The notes present this as the causal theory's main open problem and point to guidance and plan-based fixes (Setiya).

- §6, §§23–26 — one doing intentional under one description and not another (burglar); the redescription reply (drilling and pain). Indirect.
- §8, §28 — the only explicit mention: Anscombe on non-observational knowledge of one's intentional actions; Davidson extends it to reasons.

- §12 — indirect: Davidson's thesis that primary reasons are causes is the opposite of the contents line "a motive is not a cause at all"; the notes do not mention motives.
- §§17–18 — indirect: a prompt on actions done "for no reason".
- §19 — editorial link only: "cause in the right way" is the test case Speaks's Anscombe guide raises against §19.
