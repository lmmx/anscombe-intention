# A guide to Anscombe's Intention, §§1–31 — Jeff Speaks

## What this source is

A graduate/upper-level seminar handout written by Jeff Speaks for a course on the will / philosophy of action (McGill, 2004; reused with small revisions at Notre Dame, 2009, where it sits on the syllabus alongside Davidson, Wilson, Thompson and Setiya). The 2004 HTML and 2009 PDF are substantively the same text (the 2009 version adds a cross-reference to Setiya "as we saw"; the 2004 version footnotes Setiya, "Explaining Action", 2004). It is explicitly a "reading of the structure of the first half" of the book, on the grounds that Anscombe, like Wittgenstein in the *Investigations*, "moves back and forth across the topics". It covers only §§1–31 and stops by pointing forward to §32. The companion question sheet (one page) poses four seminar questions, noted at the end.

Speaks is not a partisan Anscombean: he writes from within the mainstream causal-theory literature and is frequently critical. That makes the guide valuable for structure and for seeing the standard objections, less so as a sympathetic exposition.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

### 1. Different kinds of intention (§1)
Three ways we talk of intention: intentions to act, intentions in acting, intentional action. Focusing on one alone risks claims false of the others. Anscombe rejects treating "intention" as ambiguous like "bank"; the moral, for Speaks, is that although the three differ, an account must explain their very close relations.

### 2. Intentions to act and prediction (§§2–4)
- **The problem (§2).** What distinguishes intentions from predictions? Speaks clarifies that "prediction" can mean a speech act about the future or an unvoiced belief about the future. Anscombe's question is first about two kinds of utterance (prediction vs. *expression* of intention), as a route to two kinds of mental state. The intuitive distinction is clear (his gloss on her example "I am going to fail this exam"); the task is a theoretical account.
- **Grammar won't do (§2).** In a narrow sense of "grammar", one sentence can be either prediction or expression of intention depending on use.
- **Justification (§3).** Predictions are justified by evidence that the future state will obtain; expressions of intention by reasons for finding that state attractive. Speaks's example: "I've failed every other one" (evidence) vs. "I really want to annoy my parents" (reason). He notes an apparent counterexample ("Because I always do what I want") which only works as evidence on the supposition that the agent intends. Anscombe does not press this line because the reasons/evidence distinction is only worked out later under intentional action (§5 ff.); Speaks says the "implicit conclusion" here — that intention vs. prediction cannot be explained just by reasons vs. evidence — is not argued at this stage.
- **How we know others' intentions (§4).** Asked what someone is doing, we usually list intentional actions, needing no special verification, and these mostly give the person's intentions too. Speaks finds this puzzling as a step in the argument: (i) §4 shifts from intentions-to-act to intentional action without explanation; (ii) that we can easily tell what people are doing no more explains what intention *is* than our ease in telling predictions from expressions of intention explains that distinction. He reads the end of §4 as negative: an attack on the tempting picture that intention is a purely interior state whose connection to action is inessential, so that what a man actually does is "the very last thing" to consider (Anscombe says it is the first).
- **Speaks's critique.** If the argument is from how we *come to know* intentions to what intentions *are*, it confuses epistemic and metaphysical priority (his analogy: electrons and the blips they cause on a detector). A better reading: §§2–4 contain an implicit argument that intentions to act cannot be understood independently of intentional action, which is why §5 turns to intentional action. The positive claim is one of *priority*: an account of intention to act must go via an account of intentional action.

### 3. Intentional action, the question 'Why?', and non-observational knowledge (§§5–8)
- **Proposed definition (§5).** A acts intentionally iff a certain sense of "Why?" applies to the action. Glossing this as "a request for reasons for acting" is too tight a link to be illuminating (the circle Anscombe warns of). Speaks distinguishes three senses of "Why?" with his own examples: *evidential* (the cafeteria sign), *causal* (the blown fuse), and *reason-giving* (left the lecture "to get coffee"). Anscombe's aim is to explain the third without relying on the intuitive reason/cause distinction, first by saying when it is refused application.
- **Two refusals (§§6–7).** (i) The agent does not know she is doing it (§6); (ii) the act was involuntary (§7). (ii) threatens circularity, since voluntary/involuntary is too close to intentional.
- **Non-observational knowledge (§8).** Anscombe's fix: knowledge without observation — knowing something with no separable sensory event on the basis of which one knows it (Speaks quotes her limb-position passage). Things not known this way (her "peristaltic movement of the gut") are already excluded by refusal (i). Among things known without observation, some are intentional and some not; the difference is that in some cases we also know *without observation the cause* of the movement. Speaks's examples: I hear a knock (by observation) but know without observation that I opened the door *because* of the knock; whereas a knee-jerk reflex is known without observation but its cause is not. (Anscombe's own example in §8 is the jerk when falling asleep; the knee-jerk is Speaks's substitute.) He thinks Anscombe's restriction to "purely physical" descriptions here is unnecessary.
- **Tightening.** Speaks's physical-therapy counterexample (seeing in a mirror that a machine moves one's arm) is not excluded by (i) as Anscombe states it, so he strengthens (i) to: the agent lacks *non-observational* knowledge of what he is doing. Result:
  - *A φs intentionally ≡ (i) A knows non-observationally that he is φing and (ii) A knows non-observationally why he is φing.*
- **Status of the principle.** Either it gives the *nature* of intentional action (Speaks finds this backwards: my non-observational knowledge seems explained by my acting intentionally, not vice versa), or it states a necessary truth that any account must explain. He prefers the latter.
- **Counterexamples.** (1) Knowledge that one is φing is not necessary: a professor who rudely insults a student without realising it was rude (Speaks's reply: maybe not intentional; responsibility can attach to what one should have known); making ten carbon copies without knowing whether one is succeeding (a well-known example from Davidson, not credited by Speaks). Fix: Setiya's weaker principle — if one φs intentionally, there is *something* one does in the belief that one is doing it — but this is useless as a demarcation criterion (flipping the switch intentionally, alerting the burglar unintentionally). A "possible fix" adds an in-order-to clause linking φ to some non-observationally known ψ. (2) Knowledge *why* is not necessary: self-deception about one's reasons. Speaks sees no way round this. (3) Not sufficient: falling when drunk/dizzy, known proprioceptively and "felt" as caused by dizziness. **Verdict:** the demarcation "is a failure"; its merit is drawing attention to the necessary truth that intentional action involves doing something in the belief that one is doing it.

### 4. Reasons and mental causes (§§9–11)
Anscombe is interested not in causes generally but in "mental causes": roughly, something in the agent's stream of consciousness — an awareness-state (desire, urge, image) or a perceived external event — that leads to action. She argues (i) some mental causes are not reasons (Speaks's example: an image of a book reminding one of something left at home) and (ii) some reasons are not mental causes, since (§11) a reason may never become conscious (a desire for apples that makes one fetch some without any particular conscious episode). §10's parallel between the object and the mental cause of an *emotion*, which can coincide, suggests some reasons can also be mental causes (Speaks thinks the first paragraph of §14 implies this). **Key interpretive claim:** these passages do *not* endorse the thesis often attributed to Anscombe that reasons are not causes; only that some reasons are not *mental* causes — weaker, since not all causes are mental causes.

### 5. Motives and intentions in acting (§§12–15)
The third kind of intention: intention *in* acting, closely related to motive (his example: I intend to write the handout; my intention in writing it is to clarify Anscombe). §12: motives explain action, but Anscombe seems to deny this is determining or causing; knowing motives lets us "interpret" the action, a word Speaks finds left "pretty much open". If knowing motives is knowing reasons, she seems to say reasons cannot be causes — unclear whether only in the restricted "mental cause" sense. §15 muddies this: in many cases whether to call something a reason or cause seems arbitrary. Plausible reading: clear reason-cases, clear cause-cases, and cases where one item is both. Speaks wonders why, if a reason for acting just is a motive / intention in acting, Anscombe does not say so. (§§13–14 — backward-looking and "interpretative" motives — are not separately discussed.)

### 6. Summary of conclusions (§§16–18)
Three criteria Speaks extracts from §16: (1) the agent can know, without observation, that and why he performs the act; (2) "Why?" applies where it is not a request for evidence or a cause; (3) positively, "Why?" can be answered by (a) past history whose relevance depends on the agent's taking it as good or bad, (b) an interpretation of the action, or (c) mention of something future. §17: a question having application is not its having a substantive answer; "No reason" is compatible with intentional action. (§18 is not separately treated.)

### 7. No "extra features" (§19)
Anscombe's "surprising claim": an action is not called intentional in virtue of any extra feature existing when it is performed; to call it intentional is to assign it to a class to which "Why?" is relevant. Speaks finds the slogan uninformative read literally (cf. calling an animal "canine") and reads it as a metaphysical claim: no facts of a certain sort make actions intentional. Reconstruction: suppose (reductio) a feature I of the agent at the time makes a movement intentional; being intentional is being intentional *under a description*; intrinsic features of the agent are connected only contingently (a "happy accident") with satisfaction of such descriptions; so they cannot make the movement intentional. Speaks: the argument works for *intrinsic, physical* features and has limited scope beyond them; he poses as an open test case whether an intention to greet, causing a movement "in the right way", could be such a feature.

### 8. Dependence of intentional action on intentions in acting (§§20–22)
So far intentional action looked most basic; §20 argues it cannot exist without the other two. Two arguments: (1) the "style" model (an action's being intentional like a face's being sad) fails, since actions are intentional under some descriptions and not others, unlike sad faces. (2) If the only answer to "Why?" were "I just am", intentional actions could not be distinguished from involuntary movements known without observation (e.g. gasping). Speaks's objection: Anscombe herself allows intentional action done for no reason; why not a possible world where that is the only kind? Perhaps no one there would have the *concept*, but that is not the same as there being no intentional actions. §21: this vindicates a weakened version of the classical claim that humans always act for an end — without some acting with ends in view we would lack the concept. §22: intentions in action are expressed by answers to "Why?" that give a broader description of present activity or mention a future state the activity leads toward. (Speaks does not mention §22's test about acknowledging "but if P, Q won't happen".)

### 9. Descriptions of intentional actions (§§23–26)
Three questions: (i) when is a description of an action "intentional"? (ii) how are descriptions related? (iii) when is it one action under several descriptions vs. several actions? §23 addresses (i)–(ii), §§24–26 (iii). Speaks uses his own example (typing / preparing a handout / making the computer crash) rather than Anscombe's pump: the first two are intentional, the third refused application by "I didn't know I was". Descriptions are linked by "by"/"in order to". §26: one action under a series of descriptions; we may speak of one intention or several. He notes Anscombe's suggestion that further descriptions apply because of *circumstances* (my typing counts as preparing a handout in this situation) and finds "circumstances" hard to pin down. (He does not treat the pumping case, §24's "when does he poison them?", or §25's "I didn't care".)

### 10. Interior acts of intention (§§27–31)
We still lack an account of what it is to act intentionally. Tempting view: an inner act of will causes the bodily movement ("two-factor" picture), attractive (i) because we have nothing else to explain raising vs. rising arm and (ii) because, as §29 notes, non-observational knowledge cannot seem to be of external fact, so it must be of an interior act ("two ways of knowing… two different things known"). Anscombe (i) argues against each version and (ii) reconciles the rejection with non-observational knowledge — the latter only in §§32–45. Versions rejected: **conscious episodes** (§27; e.g. saying inwardly "I hereby intend…", which could occur without the intention, e.g. as a joke); **non-physical acts of willing** contingently related to the world, as Speaks says the early Wittgenstein seemed to think (§29); **physical events that "set things rolling"** such as muscle contractions (§30) — but we know what we are doing without knowing how, physically (skilled activities: shooting a basketball, swimming butterfly). A fourth option Anscombe does not consider: coming to be in brain states that qualify as intentions by their relations. The guide closes: Anscombe looks for the answer in ancient/medieval practical knowledge and practical reasoning, from §32. (§31 is not discussed.)

### Companion question sheet (17 Feb 2009)
Four questions: Does §4's claim that intentions-to-act must be understood via intentional action rest on (i) only evidential/reason-giving answers distinguishing them and (ii) that distinction presupposing intentional action — and why believe (ii)? Would necessary and sufficient conditions in terms of non-observational knowledge be an analysis? How should the in-order-to biconditional be modified for self-deception? Does Anscombe defend her hostility to inner states not characterisable in terms of the outer?

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

- Reads §§2–4 as an implicit *priority* argument (intention-to-act must be understood via intentional action) and charges the §4 argument with confusing epistemic and metaphysical priority.
- Formalises Anscombe as offering a biconditional (non-observational knowledge *that* and *why*) and judges it refuted by counterexamples on three fronts; salvages only Setiya's weaker necessary truth.
- Insists Anscombe did *not* claim that reasons are not causes in §§9–11 — only that some reasons are not *mental* causes. (Caution: the analytic contents line for §12 says flatly that "a motive is not a cause at all"; Speaks admits §12 seems to deny that motives determine or cause, and treats the question as left open rather than settled.)
- Reads §19 as a metaphysical thesis about what does not make actions intentional, with an argument limited to intrinsic features.
- Objects to §20 that the concept of intentional action could be absent while intentional actions exist.
- Identifies the "two-factor" (inner act + movement) model as the target of §§27–31 and flags the contemporary brain-state version Anscombe does not address.

## Section pointers
- §1 — three heads; "intention" not equivocal like "bank"; account must explain their close relations.
- §2 — intention vs. prediction; prediction as utterance vs. belief; grammar (narrowly) can't separate them.
- §3 — evidence vs. reasons as justification; "I am going to fail this exam".
- §4 — we mostly see what others are doing intentionally; attack on intention as purely interior; "what a man actually does" comes first; Speaks: epistemic/metaphysical confusion, or implicit priority argument.
- §5 — "Why?" definition; three senses of "Why?" (evidential, causal, reason-giving); circularity.
- §6 — refused by "I didn't know I was doing that".
- §7 — refused for involuntary acts; circularity threat.
- §8 — non-observational knowledge; limb position; gut (not known without observation); involuntary movements known without observation but whose cause is not.
- §§9–11 — mental causes (conscious states or perceived events); some mental causes not reasons; §11 some reasons never conscious; §10 object vs. mental cause of emotion.
- §§12–15 — motive/intention in acting; "interpret"; §15 reason/cause distinction sometimes pointless.
- §16 — three formulations; answers (a) past history, (b) interpretation, (c) future.
- §17 — "No reason" doesn't refuse application.
- §19 — no extra feature; contingency ("happy accident") argument.
- §20 — sad-face model rejected; without further intentions, couldn't distinguish intentional action from known involuntary movements.
- §21 — weakened Aristotelian "end in view" claim.
- §22 — intention-with-which: broader description or future state.
- §§23–26 — descriptions; one action, many descriptions; §26 one or many intentions; role of "circumstances".
- §27 — inner conscious act of intention rejected.
- §29 — "two ways of knowing… two things known"; early-Wittgenstein-style acts of will.
- §30 — muscle-contraction / initiating-event view rejected (we know what we do without knowing how).
- §§32–45 — flagged as where the positive account (practical knowledge, practical reasoning) lies; not covered.
