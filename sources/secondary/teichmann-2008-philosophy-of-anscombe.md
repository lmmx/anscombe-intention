# The Philosophy of Elizabeth Anscombe — Roger Teichmann

## What this source is

OUP's "first major study of Anscombe's philosophical oeuvre" (publisher), covering action, ethics, mind, metaphysics, and logic and language, with a complete bibliography of her writings. Teichmann was Anscombe's student and friend in Cambridge and edited a Festschrift for her (*Logic, Cause and Action*) [Publisher]. Only the first two chapters are directly about *Intention*.

**Access caveat.** Not read. Sources: publisher page [Publisher]; Hustwit's NDPR review [Hustwit]; Wiseman's *Mind* review first page [Wiseman-rev]; Sandis footnote [Sandis]. The NDPR review is the only detailed account and has reliability problems (see below).

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation, attributed)

From [Hustwit] (paraphrased; see reliability note):
- **Ch1 "Intentional Action".** Teichmann begins with intentional action as the central topic from which the rest of Anscombe's work is approached. Hustwit's opening paragraph on this chapter says Teichmann reviews Anscombe's claim that there is a separate, describable "sensation of intention", that one sees the intention in seeing the action, and that intentional actions produce a visual sensation — **this reads as garbled** (it seems to conflate *Intention* with "The Intentionality of Sensation") and should not be relied on.
- **Ch2 "Practical Reasoning".** (Hustwit's account here also covers material one would expect in Ch1.) Inner vs outer: an observer can say what someone did and seemingly intended, but can be wrong about the first-person facts (Hustwit's shopping-mall example). Anscombe rejects both the Humean picture of intentions as separable inner causes and behaviourism. It is "doubtful" that intentions are inner processes; they are discovered through "Why?", which can be iterated. Teichmann discusses an Aristotelian grouping of ends under *eudaimonia*. A catalogue of distinctions with grammatical remarks follows: a motive is a reason, not a cause; there are reasonless actions ("for no reason", "I just wanted to"); subconscious intentions, spasms and doodling as cases like and unlike intentional action. Teichmann endorses Anscombe's insistence that "true" and "false" can apply to wanting: wanting something as good when it is not involves a false judgement.
- **Ch3 "Ethics"** (relevant to §§24–25): evaluation of action requires knowing intentions; consequentialism drops intention; double effect and casuistry discussed, including abuses.
- **Ch4 "Mind and Self"**: intentionality of the first person; "I" not a referring term.

From [Wiseman-rev] (first page): the book is the first introduction to and overview of Anscombe's writings; its thematic organisation helps newcomers; Anscombe is "one of the giants of twentieth-century philosophy" whose writing assumes familiarity with Aristotle, Hume, Wittgenstein and her Oxford contemporaries. Wiseman's assessment of the *Intention* chapters is paywalled.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

1. **Intention as the root** of the whole philosophy — the book's order (action → practical reason → ethics → mind) embodies the claim that philosophy of psychology comes first (cf. "Modern Moral Philosophy") [Hustwit][Publisher].
2. **Two-part reading of Intention**: intentional action / practical reason [Publisher TOC].
3. **Cognitivism about wanting**: wanting can be true or false (desirability as truth-apt) [Hustwit].
4. **Anscombe vs "ordinary language" philosophy and vs later Wittgenstein** — Hustwit stresses Teichmann's contrast of Anscombe's grammatical method with ordinary-language philosophers (later chapters; Tractarian truth-conditions).
5. Teichmann cites only the 1963 second edition (his bibliography distinguishes it from the first but omits the 1958 second impression) [Sandis].

## Section pointers (§N — what the source says)

All via [Hustwit]; § numbers are my alignment with Anscombe's contents, not Teichmann's citations.
- §§5–6, 18 — "Why?" and its iteration; demarcation of intentional action.
- §§10–16 — motive as reason, not cause.
- §§17–18 — "for no particular reason" / "I don't know why I did it".
- §§20–21 — Aristotelian final end / eudaimonia (Anscombe criticises the Aristotelian proof in §21).
- §§37–40 — wanting under the aspect of good; truth of wanting (cf. §40 wanting/good vs judging/true).
- §§24–25 — intention vs foresight underpinning double effect (Ch3).

## Reliability notes

- **Hustwit (NDPR 2009)**: positive ("an excellent tool", "not an easy read") but notes gaps (practical-ethics writings, Catholic faith); says Anscombe's and Teichmann's voices "often blend into one". Its description of the *Intention* chapters is loose and in places apparently mistaken (the "sensation of intention" passage). Depth for *Intention*: low. Use only for chapter scope.
- **Wiseman (Mind 2011)**: authoritative reviewer (later author of the Routledge guide) but only page 1 visible.
