# The Oxford Handbook of Elizabeth Anscombe — Roger Teichmann (ed.)

## What this source is

A 22-chapter OUP handbook in five parts: I Intention; II Ethical Theory; III Human Life; IV The First Person; V Anscombe on/and Other Philosophers. Only abstracts were accessible. Part I is the only part titled after *Intention*; a handful of later chapters bear on it.

## Structure (chapter → §§ of Intention)

No division of *Intention* is proposed. Part I's four chapters treat: practical knowledge/truth; intention in action; knowledge and responsibility; practical knowledge and testimony. § alignments in the front matter are mine.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation, attributed to abstracts)

- **Introduction (Teichmann):** presents Anscombe's several "personae" and uses the *Intention* idea that an action can be intentional under several descriptions as an analogy for her many-sidedness; identifies practical knowledge, knowledge of what one is doing, as a concept she "famously investigates", notably in *Intention*.
- **Campbell:** a "Simple View" that practical knowledge is always knowledge of practical truth is hard to sustain, since we have practical knowledge of all intentional actions while Aristotelian practical truth requires acting or living well. Distinguish thicker (ethical) and thinner (action-theoretic) versions of each notion; then a complex Simple View holds. Anscombe is committed to these distinctions without drawing them.
- **Capps:** *Intention* opens with three divisions (intention for the future, intentional action, intention in action); attempts to unify them usually start from intention for the future, occasionally from intentional action; starting from **intention in action** has unappreciated merit.
- **Clot-Goudard:** much of Anscombe's action theory answers what knowledge an agent must have of an action to be accountable for it (Aristotle's voluntary). In *Intention*, an action is intentional under a description only if the agent has practical knowledge of it under that description — hence ignorance can excuse; but ignorance can be culpable, revealing "a fundamental obligation to know", rooted in her Thomism.
- **Roessler:** statements claiming practical knowledge face two kinds of error — of performance, and (less familiar) of practical judgement. Practical knowledge is ordinarily intelligible partly in the light of the agent's non-defective practical judgement; Anscombe's account is not primarily designed to make it intelligible "to philosophers" but to show how practitioners who share it find it intelligible and their claims justified; bearing on her quarrel with a "contemplative" conception of knowledge.
- **Michon:** Anscombe was concerned with double effect from her first writings through *Intention*; the doctrine rests on intended vs merely foreseen consequences. Addresses the "closeness problem" and a charge that her double-effect views are inconsistent with *Intention*'s analysis of action; she allows broad and narrow scopes of intention that can ground moral differences.
- **H. Teichman:** "purely exegetical"; Wittgenstein's post-1945 work on psychological concepts explains *Intention*'s method; traces "knowledge without observation" from Wittgenstein (limb position) to intention, which requires rethinking how such knowledge can be corrected; identifies it with practical knowledge, which resolves puzzles about Theophrastus's principle and Aquinas's causal practical knowledge.
- **Haldane:** traces Anscombe's engagement with Aquinas from student years to last writings, cataloguing explicit and implicit references and agreements/disagreements.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

1. **Which of §1's three heads is basic?** Capps: intention in action — against the common future-intention-first and action-first strategies (contrast Moran & Stone's expression-of-intention unification; Schwenkler's "action first" in §§3–4).
2. **Knowledge and responsibility** link *Intention* to ethics via culpable ignorance (Clot-Goudard; compare Narboux in the Anscombean Mind).
3. **Correctability of non-observational knowledge** as the Wittgensteinian crux (H. Teichman) vs Roessler's two error types.
4. **Consistency of double effect with Intention's action theory** (Michon).
