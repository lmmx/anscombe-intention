## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

**Status.** *Intention* (1957) is "the founding document of contemporary philosophy of action"; Anscombe's later thought radiates from it toward ethics, explanation/"causality", and philosophy of mind. The article summary calls her "the founder of contemporary action theory".

**Three connections.** We speak of "events in a man's history" as intentional actions; of the intention with which an action is done; and of the expression of intention, or the corresponding "pure" intention for the future, which may exist with no action yet done (= §1).

**Priority of intentional action and the "Why?" test.** Taking intentional action as prior, she argues an action is intentional when a certain form of explanation applies, i.e. when "a certain sense of the question 'Why?' has application" (= §5). The task is to isolate this form. It is without application (a) if the agent is unaware of doing the thing, (b) if the agent knows it only "by observation", or (c) where, though the agent knows without observation that it is going on, he can give no account of it, or none without observation/conjecture (= §§6–9). Behaviour passing these tests is intentional unless it is "mental causality", like a gasp at the hissing of a snake, which she excludes with more complex tests (= §§9–11).

**Under a description.** Like Oedipus, I may strike a man intentionally and strike my father unintentionally, though these are not two events or two actions: actions are intentional only "under a description". The answer to "Why?" is often a further description of the same action, so a series of "Why?" questions reveals an order among the descriptions: moving one's arm → pumping water → replenishing the house supply (= §§23, 26). The chain may be pressed into the future, beyond what is now happening; then the answers express the intention *with which* the earlier-mentioned action is done (= §22). Generally, if "Why?" applies to a first-person future-tense description, it is an expression of intention, not a mere prediction (= §50).

**Practical knowledge and the shopping list.** The idea that practical knowledge — knowledge of one's intentional action — does not spring from observation leads to the book's most striking claims. The shopping list vs. detective's list (= §32): the difference lies in what is "now called" direction of fit — the detective corrects a mismatch by altering his list, the shopper by altering the contents of the basket.

**Practical syllogism.** These matters are elucidated by an extended discussion of Aristotle's practical syllogism (= §§33–42). Among other things, the order of descriptions elicited by "Why?" is "the reverse of" the order articulated by the agent reasoning from end to means (= §42, with §26).

**Other works (§2, brief).** Much of her ethics concerns intention and thought–action relations. "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) blames a naive philosophy of mind and action for "consequentialism" — that whether one intends something is irrelevant to responsibility; "Mr Truman's Degree" argues the familiar justifications of Hiroshima presuppose such thinking. Clarifying 'virtue', 'human nature', 'flourishing' requires renewed philosophy of mind and action. "Causality and Determination" (1971) explains how events not physically determined may yet be explicable otherwise.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Takes intentional action as Anscombe's starting point and future intention as derivative (expression of intention = future-tense statement to which "Why?" applies).
- Presents the refusal conditions (§§6–9) and mental causality (§§10–11) as a sequence of tests — a "filter" reading of the first third of the book.
- Uses the "Oedipus" example (not one of the book's examples as far as the TOC shows) to explain "under a description".
- Freely uses "direction of fit, as it is now called" for §32 — contrast Wiland & Driver, who reject that reading — and glosses the shopper's correction as altering the basket (Anscombe herself says the mistake lies in the performance; she does not prescribe a correction).
- Key structural thesis: the "Why?"-order is the *reverse* of the practical-reasoning order (end → means).
