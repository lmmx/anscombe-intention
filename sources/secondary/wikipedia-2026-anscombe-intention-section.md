## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)
- **Aim.** The book's aim was "to make plain the character of human action and will", approached via the concept of intention, which has three "modes of appearance" in English, tabulated as: "She is X'ing intentionally" (intentional action); "She is X'ing with the intention of doing Y / to Y" (intention with which, further intention in acting); "She intends to Y / has expressed the intention to do Y" (expression of intention for the future — what Davidson later called "pure intending") (= §1).
- **Unity.** She suggests a true account must connect the three uses; later writers have sometimes denied this or disputed her presuppositions under the first and third heads. The article says the *second* (intention with which) is crucial to her main purpose: understanding how human thought and conceptualisation relate to the "events in a man's history".
- **Order of treatment.** Rather than define intention apart from action (starting with the third head), she begins with intentional action, which soon connects with the second.
- **"Why?" (cited sec. 5–8).** What is up with a human being is an intentional action if "Why?", taken in a certain sense and addressed to him, has application. Answers give reasons or purposes ("To do Y", "because I want to do Y"); these are not the only answers but are crucial to the phenomenon (cited sec. 18–21).
- **Under a description.** The answers supply the descriptions under which an action is intentional. The article claims Anscombe was "the first to clearly spell out" that actions are intentional under some descriptions and not others: the man moving his arm on a pump handle acts intentionally as "pumping water" but not as "contracting these muscles" or "tapping out this rhythm". This influenced Davidson, though he went on to defend a causal theory Anscombe "never accepted".
- **Direction of fit (cited sec. 32).** *Intention* is called "the classic source" for the idea of a difference in "direction of fit" between cognitive states (belief) and conative states (desire), later taken up by Searle. The shopping list can be an observational report (like a cognitive state) or function as a command or desire; if the agent fails to buy what is listed we say the mistake is in the action, "not the desire".
- **Practical knowledge (cited sec. 48).** This difference in direction of fit is said to be the major difference between speculative and practical knowledge (the latter glossed as "knowledge of actions and morals"); practical knowledge is, in the phrase Anscombe takes from Aquinas, "the cause of what it understands".

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Places the weight on the *second* head (intention with which) as the key to her purpose.
- Explicitly endorses the direction-of-fit / belief–desire reading of §32 — precisely the reading that Wiland & Driver (SEP) call a misattribution originating with Searle.
- Equates the practical/speculative knowledge contrast with direction of fit and glosses practical knowledge as knowledge "of actions and morals" — an imprecise gloss not supported by §48's own wording.
- "First to clearly spell out" intentional-under-a-description: a priority claim without source.
