# Gertrude Elizabeth Margaret Anscombe (SEP) — Eric Wiland & Julia Driver

## What this source is
The main SEP biographical-thematic entry on Anscombe, covering life, ethics/politics, metaphysics, action theory, perception and testimony. Section 4 ("Action Theory", c. 1,400 words) is a compact reconstruction of *Intention*; Section 2 sets up the motivation (Truman's degree, "War and Murder", "Modern Moral Philosophy", double effect), and Section 4 ends with Anscombe's application of the intentional/further-intention distinction to contraception. Page references are to the book as "INT" (the bibliography lists 1957/1963/2000 HUP reprint; the page numbers match the 2nd-edition pagination).

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

**Motivation (entry §§1–2, §4 opening).** The authors frame *Intention* as growing out of Anscombe's 1956 opposition to Oxford's honorary degree for Truman. Defences of Truman ("he merely signed a piece of paper"; "then killing sleeping soldiers would be murder too") rested, they say, on an implicit and mistaken view of what action is, and opposing that view motivated her work on action and intention. They quote Haldane (2000) that she wrote the book to show that in doing one thing (moving one's hand) one may intentionally be doing another (directing the deaths of human beings). The double-effect discussion ("War and Murder") is presented as needing a correct philosophy of intention: killing the innocent as a means just *is* intentionally killing them, whatever passes through the agent's mind; a "direction of intention" (privately telling oneself one only intends x) cannot change that. "Modern Moral Philosophy" lists 'action', 'intention', 'pleasure', 'wanting' among the concepts of philosophy of psychology that ethics needs; Anscombe traced "consequentialism" (her coinage) to Sidgwick's definition of intention as covering all foreseen consequences of voluntary action.

**Status of the book.** *Intention* is called a classic and standard point of reference, such that contemporary theories are often classed as "Anscombean or Davidsonian". The authors cite Velleman's back-cover line that it is "often quoted, sometimes read, rarely understood".

**Reading strategy.** They recommend taking the claims in a different order from the book's, because its narrative deliberately leads into "dead ends and incomplete solutions" before the positive claims arrive.

**Starting from the conclusion (INT 84 = §47).** What might be the conclusion of the argument is that "intentional" refers to "a form of description of events". To unpack this: (1) the events in question are ones human beings are the subject of, not things that merely happen to them; (2) one action can be described in many ways (moving his arm, pumping water, doing his job, clicking out a rhythm, making a shadow on a rock); (3) it will usually not be intentional under all of them. The question is what makes some descriptions the intentional ones.

**The disjunctive first answer (INT 24 = §16 summary).** At first Anscombe answers disjunctively. Answers to "Why are you doing that?" that show the action is *not* intentional under that description: "I didn't know I was doing that"; "I merely observed/inferred that I was doing it"; "I merely observed/inferred how my movement was caused". Answers that show it *is* intentional: one mentioning something future ("To end the war"); an interpretive one ("Out of curiosity"); one mentioning something past as the ground of good or harm to its target ("They attacked us first"); and "No reason". The authors comment that this "hodgepodge" says little about intention itself, so Anscombe "shifts methods".

**Knowledge without observation and "practical knowledge".** In every case of doing something intentionally, one need not observe oneself to know what one is doing (you know you are reading this entry, not by testimony, inference or a mirror). The authors note later writers call this a "first-personal" way of knowing. Anscombe calls it "practical knowledge", and concludes that practical knowledge can only be understood once "practical reasoning" is (INT 57 = §33).

**Practical reasoning as an order.** One familiar picture is deliberation from end to means (Aristotle). Anscombe, they say, focuses on a second way that "winds up being equivalent": the different descriptions of what someone is doing stand in an order — he is moving his arm *because* (not merely *and*) he is operating the pump, which he does because he is pumping water to the house, and so on. This is an order that "really exists among events", not a sum of physical events plus something in his head; it is "the order of intention".

**The director of a project (unnumbered; = §45).** The agent is specially placed to grasp this order. Anscombe compared it to a man directing the erection of a building purely by giving orders, without seeing it or receiving reports; if all goes well his knowledge of what is done is practical knowledge. On this reading practical knowledge is not knowledge of atomic acts but of the whole means–end order; he can say "I'm moving my arm up and down" because he can say "…because I'm pumping water".

**Intention baked into description (= §§46–47).** Many concepts for describing human (and animal) movements — 'greeting', 'hiring', 'sending for', even 'offending', 'dropping', 'placing' — are ones we would lack without the concept of intention. So intention is not something "added" to behaviour that was first conceptualised independently; the order of intention is built into how we represent our lives "from the very start".

**The shopping list (INT 56 = §32).** The entry quotes the passage in full. Its gloss: when a piece of paper and what happens disagree, where the error lies depends on the paper's role. If it is a record, the error is in the paper; if it is an order or intention, the error is in what happens. So there are two ways to fail to know what happens — misrecording and misperforming — and "one who practically knows doesn't misperform".

**Against the "direction of fit" reading.** Since Searle (1975) the passage has commonly been taken as a thesis about belief vs. desire having different directions of fit. The authors say Anscombe "says nothing here" about believing, desiring or mental states; the example displays *two ways of knowing* — knowing by believing something already true, and knowing by intentionally doing it — possibly of one and the same thing (citing Frost 2014).

**Application: contraception (CAC).** Anscombe distinguished "intention" as the intentionalness of what you are doing from the further intention with which you do it; contraceptive intercourse and use of infertile periods may share a further intention (limiting family size) yet differ as acts (her forged-cheque analogy). Williams and Tanner (1972) objected that she was choosing descriptions opportunistically, contrary to her own thesis that acts fall under many descriptions.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Reads the book "out of order", treating §47's "form of description of events" as its conclusion and §§5–18 as a provisional, disjunctive survey that Anscombe herself moves beyond.
- Identifies the "order of intention" (§26's A–D series) with the order of practical reasoning (end–means), i.e. treats the two as equivalent presentations of one structure (cf. §42).
- Practical knowledge is knowledge of the whole means–end order, not of atomic movements.
- Strongly rejects the Searlean "direction of fit" reading of §32; the contrast is between two *modes of knowledge*, not two kinds of mental state.
- Emphasises the ethical and polemical background (Truman, double effect, MMP, contraception) as the motive for the book.
- Gloss "one who practically knows doesn't misperform" is the authors' own compression and is stronger than Anscombe's text, which locates the mistake in the performance.
