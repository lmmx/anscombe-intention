# Action (SEP, 2012 revision) — George Wilson & Samuel Shpall

## What this source is
The previous SEP "Action" entry (the one the brief named), now available only in the SEP archives (Winter 2022 edition); it was replaced in 2023. It is a general survey of action theory — nature of action, intentional action and intention, explanation of action, intentions and rationality — written largely within the Davidson/Bratman framework. Anscombe appears in about half a dozen places, never with § or page numbers. Its Anscombe coverage is thin but documents how the mainstream of c. 2002–2012 positioned her.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

**Non-observational awareness (entry §1).** The authors note the agent's "immediate awareness" of his physical activity and its goals and attribute to Anscombe [1963] the notion of "knowledge without observation": the agent knows without observation that he is making certain movements (perhaps only under a rough description) and what purposes they serve (also citing Falvey 2000). They call her discussion "rich and suggestive" but her conception of knowledge "through observation" "problematic": surely proprioception and kinaesthetic sensation inform us of our limbs, so why should they not count as inner observation? What Anscombe explicitly denies, they say, is that we know our bodily positions/movements by means of "separably describable sensations" serving as criteria. They object that seeing a goldfinch is not inference from separably describable visual impressions either, yet is still knowledge by observation. (This is the §8 material, though not so cited.) They then present Velleman's "spontaneous", self-fulfilling knowledge and the Strong vs. Weak Cognitivism distinction (Paul 2009) as later attempts to make sense of such practical knowledge.

**The "Davidson/Anscombe thesis" on act-individuation.** Davidson, "concurring with Anscombe", held that if a person Fs by Ging, her act of Fing = her act of Ging (flipping the switch = turning on the light = illuminating the room = alerting the burglar), although the last is unintentional. The entry uses this to discuss whether trying to move = moving (Hornsby) and whether there is one action or a causal chain of actions; it says Wilson's own (1989) chain view supports only a "modified version" of the thesis. (This corresponds to Anscombe's one-action-many-descriptions view in §§23–26, not cited by number.)

**Three constructions (entry §2).** "Anscombe opened her monograph" by noting that 'intention' figures in: (5) the agent intends to G; (6) the agent G'd intentionally; (7) the agent F'd with the intention of G-ing. The authors add (7′) "In F-ing, the agent intended to G", and discuss how (6) relates to (7) using Davidson's and Mele's deviance/luck cases. Anscombe herself is not further discussed here.

**Reason explanation (entry §3).** Wilson (1989) and Ginet (1990) "follow Anscombe" in holding that reason explanations are grounded in the agent's intentions in action, construed as non-causal, de re teleological ascriptions.

**Intentions and rationality (entry §4).** Cognitivism (intentions as special beliefs) attracts some because it promises to vindicate Anscombe's "(admittedly controversial)" claim that in acting intentionally we know what we are doing without observation; the opposing Bratman planning tradition is then surveyed.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves
- Assimilates Anscombe to Davidson on act-individuation (the "Davidson/Anscombe thesis").
- Raises the proprioception objection to "knowledge without observation" and interprets her denial narrowly as a denial of "separably describable sensations" as criteria.
- Treats Anscombe's practical knowledge as something cognitivism (Velleman, Setiya) tries to vindicate — i.e. frames her within belief-based theories rather than as an alternative to them.
- No engagement with the "Why?" question, the A–D order, practical reasoning (§§33–44), or §§45–48.
