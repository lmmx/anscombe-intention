# Routledge Philosophy GuideBook to Anscombe's Intention — Rachael Wiseman

## What this source is

An introductory guidebook in Routledge's long-running "Philosophy GuideBooks" series, which nonetheless argues a strong, contestable reading of *Intention*. Per the publisher, it situates *Intention* within Anscombe's moral philosophy and philosophy of mind; considers the influence of Aquinas, Aristotle, Frege and Wittgenstein on its method and content; adopts a structure showing how Anscombe unifies the three aspects of the concept of intention; and distinguishes *Intention* from later work in the philosophy of action. The publisher calls *Intention* "a dense and largely misunderstood text".

**Access caveat.** I have not read the book. Content below comes from (a) the Routledge description/TOC/endorsements, (b) Marie McGinn's *Nordic Wittgenstein Review* article, which summarises and quotes Wiseman with page numbers, (c) reading-list and course notices, (d) abstracts of two Wiseman papers. ## Structure (chapter → §§ of Intention)

The book is **organised by Anscombe's three heads (§1)**, not by the sequence of sections: one chapter each on (1) expressions of intention, (2) intentional action, (3) intention with which, then a chapter on their unification [Publisher]. This is itself an interpretive decision: it treats the book's architecture as a three-into-one argument whose completion is the "unification" chapter.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation, attributed)

- **Method: grammatical investigation.** Wiseman claims that *Intention* "is an exemplification of the method of grammatical investigation" (Wiseman 2016: 16, quoted by [McGinn]). The investigation works against temptations — that "intention" names an inner mental state that its expressions express, and that what makes actions intentional is some extra property for philosophers to identify [McGinn's paraphrase].
- **Object: an order belonging to the concept.** It proceeds by examining and describing "a human life shaped by the concept of intention" (p. 4) and thereby uncovers "an order that belongs to the concept of intention" (p. 4) — an order characterising how we describe what we and others are doing whenever we use intention-dependent concepts [McGinn].
- **No explanatory ambition.** On Wiseman's reading Anscombe's aim is purely conceptual clarification: to expose the order in the tacit conventions we have already mastered (in expressing and attributing intentions and motives, giving grounds, offering excuses), investigated "from within" as practitioners — McGinn compares PI §127 [McGinn].
- **Against "Anscombe's Question".** Wiseman names the standard reading's question "Anscombe's Question": how to give a unified account of the three aspects of intention by describing the causal, explanatory and rational connections between the mental states the concept picks out in each guise. Wiseman argues that "Anscombe herself neither poses nor answers this question" (p. 49), and that reading *Intention* as an answer to it renders the book "at worst incoherent, at best suggestive but incomplete" (pp. 52–3). For someone answering Anscombe's Question we should look more closely at the alleged mental states and bodily movements; for Wittgenstein and Anscombe we should look instead at the linguistic practices and activities in which "intention" comes to refer to those phenomena (p. 60) [McGinn, n. 5].
- **Reading strategy.** Without this Wittgensteinian conception of her task, Anscombe's "seemingly meandering reflections" (p. 55) cannot be made sense of [McGinn].

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

1. **Wittgensteinian method as the key** to the book's structure — McGinn (sympathetic but adding a "question of influence") calls this argued "persuasively", and Wiseman's guide is reported in her own Durham bibliography as the only general discussion of Anscombe's method and its relation to Wittgenstein's [McGinn][Durham-snippet — wording from search snippet; treat as unverified].
2. **Anti-Davidsonian reception thesis.** The "standard" philosophy-of-action reading (unifying three mental phenomena causally) is a misreading; the publisher's "distinguishing it from subsequent work" points the same way [Publisher]; a Goodreads reader (low reliability) likewise reports that Wiseman diagnoses the overall uptake of *Intention* as misguided.
3. **Ethical-political context.** Her 2016 ACPQ paper (abstract) argues that *Intention* must be understood as a product of the ethical and political debates of 1956–58 (Truman degree, Oxford colleagues), and that its effects on ethics were foreseen and intended by Anscombe [paper-abs]. Ch1 "Background: Intention in context" presumably presents this.
4. **Self and the first person.** Ch8 covers implications for "the self"; her 2017 *J. Phil.* paper (abstract) argues that accepting *Intention*'s account of action while resisting the no-reference thesis about "I" rests on a misreading of "The First Person" [paper-abs].
5. **McGinn's partial demurral.** McGinn accepts Wiseman's methodological reading but argues that §1's question — whether the concept of intention is univocal across its three uses — is "not typically Wittgensteinian" and leads Anscombe to conclusions about the essential nature of intention tied to her conception of the human soul [McGinn].

## Reception / reliability notes

- Endorsements: Candace Vogler — "clearly, beautifully, and thoroughly organized"; Adrian Haddock — "an essential companion for anyone trying to understand Anscombe's brilliant but somewhat elusive book", which lets readers see unity in a disparate-seeming work [Publisher]. (Haddock was later Wiseman's co-editor; endorsements are promotional.)
- Diversity Reading List: "essential reading for a course directly on Anscombe's work"; early chapters usable for first-year teaching; difficulty rated "Easy" [DRL].
- Holton (Cambridge 2025) recommends Wiseman and Schwenkler as the two recent introductions and sets Wiseman as secondary reading for the lecture on §§1–4 ("Background, methodology and the three heads") [Holton].
