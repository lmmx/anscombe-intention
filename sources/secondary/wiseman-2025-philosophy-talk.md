# Elizabeth Anscombe (Philosophy Talk) — Rachael Wiseman, with Josh Landy and Ray Briggs

## What this source is

A public-radio philosophy programme in Philosophy Talk's NEH-funded "Wise Women" series, with Rachael Wiseman (University of Liverpool; author of the *Routledge Philosophy GuideBook to Anscombe's Intention*; co-author of *Metaphysical Animals*) as guest. The page carries a full transcript (apparently machine-generated and lightly edited: some garbles). Roughly the first two-thirds concerns *Intention*; the rest covers "Mr Truman's Degree", her sexual ethics, and closing segments (a biographical report featuring Roger Teichmann; a comic "Sixty-Second Philosopher" monologue, not used here). The value is Wiseman's compact statement of her overall reading; it is not section-level.

## Account of Intention (faithful paraphrase/condensation)

**Opening framing (hosts).** The hosts' teaser poses Anscombe's pump example informally: an agent's action can be described several ways (pumping water, supplying the house, poisoning people, "killing Nazis"); which is his intention? Landy's version (water going "into a well", killing "Nazis" only if he knows who lives there) is a loose paraphrase, not Anscombe's text (§23 has the man pumping water into a cistern, the poison introduced by someone else, the inhabitants "party chiefs").

**Truman and means vs. side-effect.** Asked what Anscombe would say to the defence that Truman foresaw but did not *want* the deaths, Wiseman says she would call it absurd: dropping a bomb would not by itself induce surrender — only killing many innocents would; so the deaths were not a by-product but the *means* to his chosen end, and absence of warm feelings toward the victims is irrelevant to whether he wanted them in that sense. (Truman pamphlet / double-effect context; bears on §25 and the means–end order of §§23–26.)

**Three heads, not three things (§1).** Correcting a host who said Anscombe thought intention was "three different things": she *introduces her topic under three heads* — expressions of intention for the future, describing actions as intentional, asking the intention with which something was done. These look like a state of mind, a property of bodily movements, and a rationalising explanation; if you are unclear about the concept you will set off looking for a theory connecting three things. Anscombe wants to stop that by first describing what work the concept does and its conceptual structure; once described, it will no longer look like three things needing a "weird philosophical theory" to connect them.

**Not an ethics book.** Though ethics needs an account of action ("Modern Moral Philosophy"), she held one should investigate action with ethics "banished"; running hard ethics and hard action theory together muddles both. So *Intention* does not discuss responsibility, rightness or justification (beyond reasons for action), even though many cases (the poisoned water supply) have an ethical character with parallels to Truman.

**What the concept does: the "Why?" question (§§5–6).** In everyday life we understand each other through intention and already have a good grip on it. Example: someone barges past you; asked why, she says she didn't see you — at once you know "barging into this guy" is not a description under which she acted (cf. refusal by "I didn't know I was doing that", §6). Anscombe "reminds" philosophers of deep structure already present in linguistic practice which they skip when they start from "mental state + bodily movement, now connect them". The question "Why?" is the specific linguistic tool for finding out intentions; she explores in detail the many answers ("I didn't know I was doing it", "in order to…", "a sudden feeling") to bring out the nuance of the everyday concept.

**Intention everywhere in language (§47).** A huge number of descriptions of what happens in a life are descriptions of intentional doing; ordinary language contains hundreds of verbs with the structure of intention built in (telephoning, walking, commanding, pointing, pumping, poisoning, killing). Landy cites her list including kicking, dropping, crouching, marrying; Wiseman calls the variety "hilarious" but revealing of our interest in *why* things happen, which we even project onto inanimate things.

**Philosophy's role.** As a student and translator of Wittgenstein, she undertakes a "grammatical investigation": describing the structure of a language-using life we all inhabit. Wiseman recalls a phrase about growing up to "the age of reason in a shared world" (attributed to the beginning of *Intention*; not checked against the text here). Ordinary competence needs no philosophical correction, but philosophical pictures (Wiseman says she blames Descartes and Oxford philosophers) can seep into everyday thinking about abstract moral questions — e.g. the Truman debate — making people "forget what they already know". She wanted to give philosophers the structure of the concept so they stop "corrupting"/befuddling.

**"Not a performance in the mind"; seeing what people do (§4, §27).** Landy objects to claims he attributes to Anscombe: intention is never a performance in the mind; "roughly speaking, a man intends to do what he does" (Wiseman stresses "roughly speaking"); anyone who sees me sitting in a chair writing will know what I'm up to. Wiseman's reply: anyone grown to reason in a shared world can, on entering the room, immediately give descriptions of what a person is doing that are descriptions of intentional actions — like a court witness ("lurking behind the windows", "distracting the security guard"). Further intentions are not visible, which is exactly where "Why?" has its place: asking repeatedly ("Why are you writing?" — a letter to my aunt — "Why?" — to find out if she remembered me in her will…) expands the description to wider circumstances not visible in the moment (cf. the A–D series, §§23–26). "Performance in the mind" is problematic for her because intention concerns how we move in the world and realise goals; one can of course say inwardly "I'm going to…" or have a future intention never acted on, but the point is to drop the idea that every intentional doing has an inner performance as cause (cf. §27). Making intentional action the main subject shows that the structure of intention is "out there in the world" to see and describe.

**Concealment and its limits.** One can mislead about what one is doing (Austin's thief pretending to be a window-cleaner; Landy mentions an "ironic hug" which he says is Anscombe's example — not verified here). Learning descriptions (paying at a till with a child) is at once learning a word, learning to do the thing, and learning to recognise it; one is brought into the "space of intentional action", which is also the space of giving and following orders. Sophisticated concealment comes later, and there are limits: an action's being intentional requires that it be ultimately intelligible within a shared life, so privacy of intention is limited.

**Wider themes.** Wiseman emphasises animality, sociability and mutual intelligibility; she concedes Anscombe's applied sexual ethics are conservative Catholic, ties the contraception argument to the idea of acts with an essential character (sex as essentially generative, eating as essentially nutritive), and says moral absolutism (some act-kinds prohibited) is "core to the structure" of her philosophy.

## Distinctive claims / interpretive moves

- *Intention* is a description of the structure of an everyday concept ("grammatical investigation"), not a theory linking state, movement and reason.
- The three heads are three *contexts of use* of one concept, not three things.
- The book deliberately brackets ethics, though its examples are ethically loaded.
- Intentional action is publicly visible "to a large degree"; "Why?" is the tool for extending descriptions beyond what is visible; concealment is possible but bounded by intelligibility in shared life.
- Truman's killing of innocents was intended as a *means*, so the foresight/intention defence fails.
