# L0 — Overview of Anscombe's *Intention*

[README](../README.md) · ↓ [L1 Parts](L1-parts.md) · [Glossary](../glossary.md) · [Debates](../debates.md) · [Reading paths](../reading-paths.md)

G. E. M. Anscombe, *Intention*. Oxford: Blackwell, 1957; 2nd edition 1963; reprinted Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2000. 52 numbered sections, about 94 pages.

## In one paragraph

*Intention* asks what it is for something someone does to be *intentional*. It answers without appeal to inner mental acts or to causes. Intentional actions are those to which a special sense of the question **"Why?"** applies, the sense whose answer gives a reason for acting (§5). That sense is marked off by the answers that refuse it ("I didn't know I was doing that", §6; "I knew only by observation", §8) and by its range of acceptable answers, including "for no particular reason" (§§16–18). From this Anscombe draws a set of consequences:

- An action is intentional only **under a description** (§6).
- "Intentional" adds no extra feature to an action (§19).
- One action can be described as a series of means and ends, the **A–D order** (§§23–26).
- An agent knows what they are doing **without observation** (§§8, 28).

That knowledge is not a speculative record of what happens. It is **practical knowledge**, modelled on Aristotle's practical reasoning (§§33–43) and on the medieval formula that practical knowledge is "the cause of what it understands" (§48). "Intentional" turns out to name a **form of description of events** (§47). The book ends by applying this to the expression of intention for the future, where it began (§§50–52).

## About a page

### The question

"Intention" figures in three contexts:

- expressions of intention for the future ("I'm going to…");
- the classification of actions as intentional;
- the intentions *with which* people act (§1).

Anscombe neither assumes that these are three senses of a word nor that one is fundamental. Her question is what the concept is, and the book works it out by examining how we actually use the relevant forms of words.

### The method

The method is broadly Wittgensteinian. It avoids introspection: introspective accounts of intention are "useless" (§3). It looks at what can be said and asked, and at what would make an answer unintelligible. It moves outward from what is publicly visible. We usually know what someone intends from what they are actually doing (§4), so Anscombe starts from intentional action rather than from an inner state. Her targets include:

- the picture of intention as an inner act or extra feature accompanying a movement (§§19, 27, 29–30);
- treating motives as causes (§12);
- the modern misreading of the practical syllogism as a proof of what one ought to do (§33). Commentators identify Ryle and Hare as foils (see [Schwenkler interview](../sources/secondary/schwenkler-c2020-316-interview.md)).

### Main theses

1. **The "Why?" question.** An action is intentional when a certain sense of "Why?" applies to it, the one asking for a reason for acting (§§5–18). The sense is specified negatively, by refusals, and by the range of answers it admits:
   - mention of something future (an intention with which);
   - an interpretative motive;
   - a backward-looking motive;
   - "no particular reason".

   It is distinct from asking for evidence (§9) or for a "mental cause" (§§10–11). Motives are not causes (§12).
2. **Description-relativity.** One and the same action may be intentional under one description and not another (§6). "Intentional" is not an extra property the action has at the time (§19).
3. **The A–D order.** In the pump example, the agent moves his arm, operates the pump, replenishes the water supply and poisons the inhabitants. These are descriptions of one action, ordered so that each later one gives the intention with which the earlier is done (§§23–26). The same order is what practical reasoning sets out (§42).
4. **Knowledge without observation / practical knowledge.** An agent knows what they are intentionally doing without looking to see (§§8, 28). This seems to split the object of knowledge in two: what I intend and what actually happens (§29). The shopping list and the detective's record contrast two relations of a description to what is done (§32). Practical knowledge, like the knowledge of someone directing a project they cannot see (§45), is "the cause of what it understands" (§48). Whether, and in what sense, it is knowledge of what actually happens is the most debated question about the book (see [debates](../debates.md)).
5. **Practical reasoning and wanting.** Practical syllogisms are a distinctive form of reasoning that starts from something wanted (§§33–35). Wanting must be intelligible through a **desirability characterisation** at which "What for?" comes to an end (§§37–38). No such characterisation is compelling (*bonum est multiplex*, §39). Wanting stands to "good" as judging stands to "true" (§40).
6. **"Intentional" is a form of description.** Many descriptions of what humans do (building a house, paying a bill) are formally descriptions of executed intentions (§§46–48).
7. **Intention for the future** is covered by the same account. A prediction is an expression of intention when "Why?" applies to it (§50). Expressing an intention and predicting can come apart (§52).

### Why it matters

The book is widely treated as the founding text of modern analytic philosophy of action. Its relation to Davidson's causal theory, and the revival of a "new Anscombean" reading since about 2000 (Thompson, Rödl, Moran, Ford and others), are traced in [debates.md](../debates.md) and [sources/EVALUATION.md](../sources/EVALUATION.md). It was written in the wake of Anscombe's 1956 protest against Oxford's honorary degree for Truman, and it supplies the concepts of intention and of means and side-effect that her ethics relies on (see [Wiland & Driver](../sources/secondary/wiland-driver-2022-sep-anscombe.md)).
