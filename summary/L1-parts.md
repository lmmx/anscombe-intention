# L1 — The Parts of *Intention*

[README](../README.md) · ↑ [L0 Overview](L0-overview.md) · ↓ section groups in [L2-sections/](L2-sections/) · [Glossary](../glossary.md) · [Debates](../debates.md)

*Intention* has no chapters. It is 52 numbered sections running continuously, about 94 pages in the 2nd edition. This file divides it into seven movements, gives a few paragraphs on each, and links down to the seventeen section groups (L2).

## Why these boundaries

Anscombe did not mark parts, so any division is an editorial decision. The division here follows John Schwenkler's *Guide* (2019), whose seven chapters map onto §§1–4, 5–18, 19–27, 28–32, 33–43, 44–48 and 49–52 (verified from the OUP chapter headings; see [schwenkler-2019-guide](../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md)). It was checked against Anscombe's own analytic table of contents, where the transitions are signposted:

- §5 begins a new topic: "intentional action" is defined by the question "Why?".
- §16 is explicitly a "summary of results reached so far".
- §19 opens a new question: whether "intentional" names an extra feature of the action.
- §28 announces a "further enquiry" into non-observational knowledge.
- §32 asks whether there is "practical knowledge" and §33 says this can only be understood through Aristotle's practical reasoning.
- §§45–48 return to practical knowledge.
- §49 gives an account of the voluntary.
- §50 announces a "return" to expression of intention for the future.

**Alternative divisions:**
- [Holton](../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md): §§1–4 / 5–21 / 22–45 / 46 to the end.
- [Speaks](../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md), within §§1–31: §1 / 2–4 / 5–8 / 9–11 / 12–15 / 16–18 / 19 / 20–22 / 23–26 / 27–31.
- Frey's syllabus: §§1–19 / 20–32 / 33–52.
- [Wiseman](../sources/secondary/wiseman-2016-guidebook.md) organises her guidebook by the three heads of §1 rather than by section order.

*Editorial note:* two boundaries are contestable. **§44** could close Part V; it is the last of the discussions of practical-reasoning forms. It is placed in Part VI with Schwenkler because the contents line asks when "If I do this, this will happen…" counts as practical reasoning, which bears directly on the practical knowledge of §45. **§27** (interior acts of intention) leads straight into the epistemological questions of §§28–31. It stays in Part III because it closes the discussion of what "the" intentional action is.

## Part I: Preliminaries

**§§1–4** · ↓ [G01 (§1)](L2-sections/G01-s01-three-heads.md) · [G02 (§§2–4)](L2-sections/G02-s02-04-intention-for-the-future.md)

The book opens by noting that "intention" turns up in three kinds of context:

- when someone expresses an intention for the future ("I'm going to walk");
- when we call an action intentional;
- when we ask with what intention something was done, i.e. intention *in* acting (§1).

Anscombe does not assume these are three uses of a word with different meanings, nor that one of them is basic. The book is in effect an attempt to understand how they hang together. That project is only completed when §§50–52 return to the first head.

§§2–3 start with the expression of intention for the future, because it is a form of words we can examine. Anscombe declines to rest on an intuitive contrast between "prediction" and "expression of intention". Instead she defines prediction broadly enough to include orders and expressions of intention as well as estimates of the future (§2). Such a statement can be false in two ways: as a lie, or because the intention is not carried out. In the second case the mistake lies in what is done, not in what was said (§2). An introspective account of intention is useless (§3). What distinguishes an expression of intention from an estimate of the future is the kind of justification, if any, that is offered for it (§3).

§4 changes direction. We can rarely say with confidence what someone intends if we try to look into their mind. We can very often say it from what they are actually doing: the true descriptions of what a person is doing are frequently just what they intend. So Anscombe will begin with intentional action, which is visible, rather than with intention as an inner state. She also asks why we suppose an agent to be the sole authority on their own intentions.

## Part II: The Question "Why?"

**§§5–18** · ↓ [G03 (§§5–8)](L2-sections/G03-s05-08-why-question-and-its-refusal.md) · [G04 (§§9–11)](L2-sections/G04-s09-11-mental-causality.md) · [G05 (§§12–15)](L2-sections/G05-s12-15-motives.md) · [G06 (§§16–18)](L2-sections/G06-s16-18-the-range-of-answers.md)

This is the core of the account of intentional action. Intentional actions are those to which a certain sense of the question "Why?" has application, namely the sense in which the answer, if positive, gives a reason for acting (§5). The difficulty is to say which sense that is without circularity, since "reason for acting" and "action" are themselves in question.

Anscombe proceeds negatively, by the answers that *refuse* the question application:

- "I didn't know I was doing that" (§6). The same action can be known under one description and not under another, so an action is intentional only *under a description*.
- The action was involuntary (§7). This notion presupposes the very distinctions at issue.
- The agent knew of the movement only by observation (§8).

This is where she introduces knowledge "without observation": the knowledge one has of some of one's own movements, and of the cause of, e.g., a start. Some movements are known without observation even though there is no non-observational knowledge of their cause, such as the jerk on falling asleep.

§§9–15 separate reasons for acting from neighbouring things:

- **Evidence** (§9), which answers a different sense of "Why?".
- **"Mental causes"** (§§10–11). These are causes the agent knows without observation, like what made one jump, and they are distinct from motives, from the objects of feelings, and from intentions with which one acts.
- **Motives.** Philosophers had treated motives as causes; Anscombe argues a motive is not a cause at all (§12). She distinguishes forward-looking intentions from backward-looking motives such as revenge, and from "interpretative" motives, i.e. motive-in-general, such as acting "out of friendship" (§13). Backward-looking motives involve the ideas of good and harm (§14). The line between a mental cause and a reason is not always sharp ("I put it down because he told me to", §15).

§16 summarises the results. §§17–18 make a key move: "For no particular reason" and "I don't know why I did it" do *not* refuse the question. They accept it with a null answer, like answering "None" to "How much money have you got?". But not every action could be like that. The question "Why?" is identified as one *expecting* an answer in the range described, and that range is what defines the class of intentional actions (§18).

## Part III: The Unity of Action

**§§19–27** · ↓ [G07 (§§19–21)](L2-sections/G07-s19-21-no-extra-feature-and-chains-of-reasons.md) · [G08 (§§22–27)](L2-sections/G08-s22-27-intention-with-which-and-the-a-d-order.md)

Having defined intentional action by the form of an explanation, Anscombe draws consequences.

- **§19.** To call an action intentional is not to mention any extra feature that it has at the time it is done. She proves this by supposing there were such a feature and showing it would be idle.
- **§20.** Could actions still be intentional if there were no such thing as expressing intention for the future, or as further intention with which one acts? If the only answer to "Why?" were ever "For no particular reason", there would be no such question and no intentional action.
- **§21.** She criticises Aristotle's argument that there must be a single final end of all action, while granting that some chains of reasons must occur if there is intentional action at all.

§§22–27 analyse intention *with which* one acts, i.e. further intention. To say an agent does P in order that Q, the agent must treat "But if P, Q won't happen" as incompatible with having that intention (§22).

Then comes the central example (§23). A man moves his arm up and down, operating a pump, replenishing a house's water supply, poisoning the inhabitants (the water is poisoned), while also doing other things with the pump handle at the same time (see [§23](L3-sections/§23.md) for the further descriptions Anscombe lists). Is there one description that is *the* description of the intentional action? All the true descriptions that pass the "Why?" test are descriptions of intentional actions. So are there as many actions as descriptions?

Difficulties follow:
- When does he poison the inhabitants? How is moving his arm an act of poisoning (§24)?
- If he says he didn't care about the poisoning and was only doing his job, that answer falls outside the range, even if he knew. Can one settle one's intentions just by saying what they are? What is the interest of a person's intentions apart from what they did (§25)?

The answer (§26) is the **A–D order**: the descriptions form a series in which each later term is an intention with which the act under the previous description was done, and the last term is the intention of the whole. There is one action, under four descriptions, each dependent on wider circumstances. §27 rejects the idea that an interior act of intention could fix what kind of action is going on.

## Part IV: Knowledge Without Observation

**§§28–32** · ↓ [G09 (§§28–30)](L2-sections/G09-s28-30-knowledge-of-ones-own-action.md) · [G10 (§§31–32)](L2-sections/G10-s31-32-orders-and-the-shopping-list.md)

Anscombe returns to the knowledge an agent has of their own intentional actions: I can say what I am doing without looking to see (§28). That produces a puzzle. Surely there are two objects of knowledge here: what I am "doing" (my intention), known without observation, and what is actually happening, which only observation can tell me (§29)?

She surveys philosophical views of will and intention that arise from this split (§29). By an example, she argues it is wrong to push the "real" intention or act of will back to something that initiates the movements, such as muscle events or a willing (§30). She then tries comparing the facts that may falsify a statement of intentional action with the facts that make an order "fall to the ground", and judges the comparison inadequate (§31).

§32 is the turning point. A man goes shopping with a list, and a detective follows him writing down what he buys. If the man's purchases and his list disagree, the mistake is in the performance. If the detective's record and the purchases disagree, the mistake is in the record. The two lists have different relations to the same facts. Anscombe asks whether there is such a thing as "practical knowledge" in the sense of ancient and medieval philosophy.

## Part V: Practical Reasoning

**§§33–43** · ↓ [G11 (§§33–35)](L2-sections/G11-s33-35-practical-syllogism.md) · [G12 (§§36–40)](L2-sections/G12-s36-40-wanting-and-desirability.md) · [G13 (§§41–43)](L2-sections/G13-s41-43-the-order-of-practical-reasoning.md)

Practical knowledge, Anscombe says, can only be understood by first understanding what Aristotle called practical reasoning (§33). The practical syllogism is not a demonstration of what one ought to do. It is a different kind of reasoning from a proof syllogism, and this was misunderstood in modern times; Schwenkler identifies Hare as a target. The 1963 edition adds a passage on the "difference of form" and on "idle" practical syllogisms ([Sandis](../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md)).

Practical syllogisms need not look parallel to proof syllogisms. Their starting point is something wanted (§34). Aristotle's first premises contain evaluative terms. Not every statement of a reason shows practical reasoning. "I want" does not rightly occur among the premises, though the first premise must mention something wanted (§35).

§§36–40 analyse **wanting**:

- In the relevant sense, not everything can be wanted. Volition and sense-knowledge cannot be described independently of each other. There are puzzles about wanting a wife, or wanting what one does not yet suppose to exist (§36).
- Whoever wants something can be asked what for, or in what respect it is desirable, until they give a **desirability characterisation** (§37). At that point "What for?" lapses. Anscombe's example is the horrifying "It befits a Nazi…", which shows that this does not put practical reasoning beyond criticism so long as it is valid (§38).
- No desirability characterisation compels wanting: *bonum est multiplex*, the good is manifold (§39).
- Wanting relates to "good" as judging relates to "true" (§40).

The mark of practical reasoning is that the thing wanted is at a distance from the particular action (§41). Setting out such reasoning in full is "absurd" if taken as a psychological report. Its point is to describe an *order*: the same order found in analysing what "the intentional action" was in §§23–26 (§42). §43 contrasts "the stove is burning" with "the man is paying his gas bill" to show how complex the descriptions of human doings are.

## Part VI: Practical Knowledge

**§§44–48** · ↓ [G14 (§§44–45)](L2-sections/G14-s44-45-practical-knowledge-and-failure.md) · [G15 (§§46–48)](L2-sections/G15-s46-48-form-of-description-and-practical-knowledge.md)

§44 considers "If I do this, this will happen; if that, that" followed by action, and asks when that is practical reasoning and when it is not.

§45 presents practical knowledge through a man directing a project, e.g. a building, which he cannot see and of which he gets no reports, purely by giving orders. He knows what is being done. The problem is how this is knowledge if his orders are not carried out. (The section's interpretation is disputed; see [debates §2](../debates.md#2-practical-knowledge-as-the-cause-of-what-it-understands-48-and-failure-45).)

§§46–48 explain where the concept of human action comes from:

- Describing something as building a house or writing on a blackboard uses the concept of human action, which was defined by means of the question "Why?" (§46).
- "Intentional" relates to a **form of description of events**. Anscombe adds a discussion of intention in animals (§47).
- Many descriptions of what humans bring about are formally descriptions of executed intentions (§48). Here practical knowledge is elucidated as "the cause of what it understands". On Anscombe's account, without such knowledge what happens would not come under the description of an executed intention (see [§48](L3-sections/§48.md)). This is the culmination of the book's account of agential knowledge.

## Part VII: Voluntary Action and Intention for the Future

**§§49–52** · ↓ [G16 (§49)](L2-sections/G16-s49-voluntary-action.md) · [G17 (§§50–52)](L2-sections/G17-s50-52-intention-for-the-future-revisited.md)

§49 gives an account of "voluntary" action as distinct from intentional action. Commentators disagree about how much weight this section bears: Narboux treats the voluntary as the book's hidden topic, while Schwenkler treats §49 as concluding material.

§§50–52 return to the first head of §1. What has been said about intention in present action also applies to intention for the future. A prediction is an expression of intention when the question "Why?" applies to it (§50). §51 considers "I just want to, that's all" in reply to "Why?" about an expression of intention for the future. §52 considers "I am not going to…" as an expression of intention alongside "I am going to…" as an expression of belief, and cases where both might be said together. This closes the circle opened in §§1–4.
