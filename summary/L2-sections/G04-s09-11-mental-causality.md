# G04 — Mental causality (§§9–11)

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › **G04** · [← G03](G03-s05-08-why-question-and-its-refusal.md) · [G05 →](G05-s12-15-motives.md)

## Where this group sits
G03 left a residue: movements whose occurrence *and* cause the agent knows without observation (a start at something seen) pass the epistemic tests yet are not intentional. §§9–11 isolate this class as "mental causality" and separate it from reasons, motives, objects of feeling and intentions — not because mental causality matters in itself but because the distinctions sharpen the sense of "Why?".

## The argument, step by step
1. **Evidential "Why?" set aside (§9).** One sense of "Why?" asks for evidence; it is irrelevant. But a non-evidential answer is not thereby a reason for acting.
2. **Locating the hard cases (§9).** Cause and reason are hard to tell apart precisely where the agent knows the causation of his movement without observation.
3. **Naming it (§10).** Such causation is "mental causality"; a mental cause is what the agent can report, without observation, as what made him move or feel — often a perceived event.
4. **Two distinctions (§10).** Mental causes are not motives; and the mental cause of an emotion is distinct from (though it can coincide with) its object.
5. **Not intentions either (§11).** The intention with which one acts is not a mental cause, even when expressed as "I wanted …".
6. **Status (§11).** Mental causality is unimportant in itself; the distinctions it forces are what matter.

## Key examples
- "I saw such-and-such and it made me jump"; a face at the window (p. 16, per Holton and Frey).
- Gasp at a snake's hiss (Thompson's illustration).
- †The child terrified of red stuff on the stairs after the nursemaid's remark: object vs. mental cause of fear (§10).

## What it establishes / hands on
- A sharpened negative boundary: answers giving evidence or a (mental) cause do not grant "Why?" application (summarised in §16).
- Non-observational knowledge is not sufficient for intentionality — a point later readers use against defining practical knowledge by non-observation (§§28–32, 48).
- The ground for §§12–15: if motives are neither intentions only nor mental causes, what are they?

## Commentators on this stretch
- Do these sections claim reasons are not causes? [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md): no — only that some mental causes are not reasons and some reasons not mental causes; [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md): Anscombe is not much interested in the question and uses "mental causation" in a special sense; [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) and [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) resist the "anti-causalist" label; [Richter n.d.](../../sources/secondary/richter-nd-iep-anscombe.md) and [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) read her as anti-causal; [Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md) (Hornsby) says the reason/cause framing misses the deeper dispute about agents' causal powers.
- [Thompson 1998](../../sources/secondary/thompson-1998-rep-anscombe.md): mental causality is excluded by "more complex tests" after the epistemic filters.
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md): the face-at-the-window case refutes non-observational definitions of practical knowledge.
- Taxonomy divergence: Holton files mental causes under "question gets application", contrary to §16.
- §§10–15 overlap with the 1957 Aristotelian Society paper ([Sandis 2015](../../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md)).
