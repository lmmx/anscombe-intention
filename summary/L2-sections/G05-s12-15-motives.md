# G05 — Motives (§§12–15)

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › **G05** · [← G04](G04-s09-11-mental-causality.md) · [G06 →](G06-s16-18-the-range-of-answers.md)

## Where this group sits
G04 separated mental causes from intentions. The remaining positive answers to "Why?" that are not further intentions — revenge, gratitude, "out of friendship" — need a place. §§12–15 locate them under "motive", attacking the philosophers' view (Schwenkler: Ryle's) that motives are causes, and identifying what makes a backward-looking motive a reason rather than a cause: the agent's conception of good and harm.

## The argument, step by step
1. **Motive vs. intention, popularly (§12).** Philosophers separate motives sharply from intentions and call them causes; ordinary usage makes "motive" the wider notion, including intention with which.
2. **A motive is not a cause (§12).** Giving a motive does not report what made one act; it helps us understand (†"see the action in this light").
3. **Kinds of non-future motive (§13).** Backward-looking motives cite a past ground (revenge: "because he killed my brother"); motive-in-general ("out of friendship") is interpretative.
4. **The mark of backward-looking motives (§14).** They differ from mental causes because they involve the notions of good and harm: the act is a response to something taken as a benefit or injury.
5. **Concession (§15).** The cause/reason line is not everywhere sharp: "I put it down because he told me to" may fall on either side.

## Key examples
- "I killed him because he killed my brother" (§13).
- "I did it out of friendship" (§13).
- "I put it down because he told me to" (§15).

## What it establishes / hands on
- Two of the three positive answer-types listed in §16: past history taken as good or harm, and interpretation; the third (something future) leads to intention with which (§§22 ff.).
- The tie between reasons for acting and the good (§14), later developed in the desirability characterisation (§§37–40).
- A question left open: backward-looking motives are reasons for acting, but practical reasoning (§§33 ff.) is modelled on the syllogism from something wanted — do they fit?

## Commentators on this stretch
- Historical target: Ryle ([Schwenkler c2020](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-c2020-316-interview.md); [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md)).
- Causation: [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) finds §12's "interpret" unclear and grants §12 leans towards "reasons are not causes"; §15 suggests clear cases on both sides plus overlaps. [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) cites "not everywhere sharply distinct" as evidence of Anscombe's indifference to the reasons/causes debate.
- Backward-looking rationality: Müller (in [Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md)) argues Anscombe wrongly excludes it from practical reasoning; Piedrahita replies that *Intention* never claimed to cover all practical rationality. [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) assimilates backward-looking motives to desirability characterisations.
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md): §12 is where intention-with-which first enters the "Why?" inquiry.
- The whole group overlaps with the 1957 Aristotelian Society paper ([Sandis 2015](../../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md)); [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) passes over §§13–14.
