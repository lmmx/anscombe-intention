# §01 — Three heads under which the topic is introduced

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part I: Preliminaries](../L1-parts.md#part-i-preliminaries) › [G01: §1](../L2-sections/G01-s01-three-heads.md) › **§01** · [§02 →](§02.md)

*Pages: from p. 1 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: the subject is introduced under three headings — expressing an intention for the future, acting intentionally, and the intention with which one acts.*

## In one sentence
Anscombe lays out three ordinary contexts in which the concept of intention is used, denies that the word is equivocal across them, and resolves to begin piecemeal with expressions of intention for the future.

## Summary
The book opens by noting that "intention" figures in three kinds of statement (§1): when someone says "I am going to do such-and-such", we may call this an *expression of intention* for the future; we describe some actions as *intentional*; and we ask *with what intention* something was done (intention in acting, or the intention with which). The ordinary first-person form of the first is "I am going to …" (§1; Setiya cites p. 1 for this). Two tempting simplifications are blocked at once. First, the claim that intention always concerns the future is false of intentional action (§1, as quoted in Holton's handout). Second, it is implausible that the word is simply equivocal across the three uses, like an ambiguous word; the urge to speak of "different senses" of "intention" shows only that we are largely in the dark about the concept (§1). So the task is to understand one concept whose uses differ but are intimately related. Rather than starting from a general theory, Anscombe proposes to proceed piecemeal, beginning with expressions of intention for the future (§1), which is the topic of §§2–4.

## Main claim(s)
- The concept of intention shows up under three heads: expression of intention for the future, intentional action, intention with which (§1).
- "Intention always concerns the future" is false (it fails for intentional action) (§1).
- "Intention" is not equivocal across these uses; talk of "senses" reflects our confusion (§1).
- The inquiry should begin piecemeal, with expressions of intention for the future (§1).

## Examples
- The utterance "I am going to do such-and-such" as the standard expression of intention for the future (§1).
- No developed examples; the section is programmatic.

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- That "intention" has several senses (rejected as a symptom of confusion) (§1).
- That intention is essentially future-directed (rejected via intentional action) (§1; the future-connection question returns in §2).

## Cross-references within *Intention*
- §§2–4 — carry out the proposal to start with expressions of intention for the future.
- §5 — turns to intentional action (the second head) and defines it via "Why?".
- §§12, 16, 22 — intention with which (the third head) comes into focus.
- §23 — the three heads shown to be interchangeable "to a certain extent" in answers to "Why?".
- §§50–52 — return to intention for the future, now in the light of the account of intentional action.

## In the secondary literature
- [Setiya 2022](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) — frames the whole philosophy of intention around the "three guises" of §1 and makes finding their unity its "principal task"; reads Anscombe (via §23 and §§50–52) as achieving unity without positing intention as a mental state.
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — quotes §1 at length as an example of method; maps the heads onto the book (§§2–4 and §§50–52; §§5–20; §22 ff.); stresses that their equivalence is the *conclusion* of the inquiry and holds only "to a certain extent" (§23).
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — the moral of §1: focusing on one head risks claims false of the others; any account must explain their close relations.
- [Wiseman 2025](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2025-philosophy-talk.md), [Wiseman 2016](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2016-guidebook.md) — three heads are three *contexts of use* of one concept, not three things (state, movement, explanation) needing a theory to connect them; Wiseman's guidebook is organised by the three heads, and rejects "Anscombe's Question" (a causal unification of three mental phenomena) as not Anscombe's.
- [Wiseman 2016](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2016-guidebook.md) (reporting McGinn) — *divergence*: McGinn holds that §1's univocity question is "not typically Wittgensteinian".
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — reads the three heads as an order of inquiry, not a ban on "I intend to …"; treats the opening linguistic accent as a concession to a 1950s audience.
- [Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md) — Moran & Stone unify the heads through a general sense of "expression of intention" (any answer to "Why?"); Clark objects that never-acted-on intentions resist this.
- [Teichmann (ed.) 2022](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2022-oxford-handbook-anscombe.md) — Capps argues that intention *in action* is the fundamental head, against future-first and action-first strategies (*divergence* from Thompson/Setiya, who take intentional action as prior).
- [Thompson 1998](../../sources/secondary/thompson-1998-rep-anscombe.md) — summarises §1 as three "connections", including "pure" intention for the future that may exist with nothing yet done.
- [Wilson & Shpall 2012](../../sources/secondary/wilson-shpall-2012-sep-action.md), [Wikipedia 2026](../../sources/secondary/wikipedia-2026-anscombe-intention-section.md) — restate §1 as three schemata; Wikipedia adds Davidson's later label "pure intending".
- [Setiya 2008](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2008-practical-knowledge.md) — uses p. 1's "I am going to" to support the view that expressing intention is also expressing belief.
