# §02 — Expression of intention versus prediction; how an expression of intention can be false

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part I: Preliminaries](../L1-parts.md#part-i-preliminaries) › [G02: §§2–4](../L2-sections/G02-s02-04-intention-for-the-future.md) › **§02** · [← §01](§01.md) · [§03 →](§03.md)

*Pages: from p. 1 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: our intuitive grasp of the difference between a "prediction" and an "expression of intention" is rejected as a foundation; "prediction" is defined widely enough to cover orders and expressions of intention as well as estimates of the future; an expression of intention in the simple future tense can be false either (a) as a lie or (b) because the intention is not carried out.*

## In one sentence
Anscombe refuses to rest on an intuitive contrast between predicting and expressing intention, widens "prediction" to every description of the future (estimates, commands, expressions of intention), and examines the peculiar way an unexecuted expression of intention is false — the fault lying with the facts, not the words.

## Summary
The section asks what distinguishes an expression of intention from a prediction. We can usually tell them apart intuitively, but that intuitive grasp cannot serve as the foundation of an account (§2, contents line). Grammar in a narrow sense will not separate them: the same sentence ("I am going to …") can be either, depending on its use ([Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) on §2). Anscombe therefore stipulates a wide sense of "prediction": any description of something future, which includes estimates of the future, commands (in the 1963 wording, "imperatives": a description of a future action cast in a form whose point is to get the addressee to do what is described) and expressions of intention (§2; wording reported by [Sandis 2015](../../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md) and [Hubbs 2016](../../sources/secondary/hubbs-2016-intentions-and-commands.md)). Commands are thus a model case: their correctness lies in execution, not in matching independently obtaining facts (Hubbs's reading).

The section also raises why intention should be essentially connected with the future at all (§2, as reported by Sandis), a question §1 has already complicated.

The second half concerns falsity. If I say in the simple future that I will do something and do not, my statement may be false in two ways: (a) I lied, never having the intention; or (b) I had the intention but did not carry it out (§2, contents). In case (b) Anscombe holds that what I said was false, yet the falsehood does not impugn what I said in the way a mistaken estimate is impugned: rather, the *facts* are faulted for not according with the words (§2, as reported by [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) and [Özaltun 2016](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md)). Several commentators identify this as the first appearance of the point Anscombe later attributes to Theophrastus — that the mistake lies in the performance, not the judgement (§§32, 45). The section also contains Anscombe's rejection of a view on which a decision or expression of intention is a kind of self-fulfilling prediction ([Millgram & Bowman 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-bowman-2026-sep-practical-reason-structure-of-actions.md); [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md)).

## Main claim(s)
- The intuitive prediction/intention contrast cannot be the foundation of an account (§2).
- In a wide sense, estimates, commands/imperatives and expressions of intention are all "predictions" — descriptions of the future (§2).
- An expression of intention can be false through lying or through non-execution (§2).
- In non-execution the discrepancy is charged to what happens, not to the statement (§2).

## Examples
- Contrast pairs between a mere prediction and an expression of intention: †"I am going to be sick" vs. "I am going to take a walk"; Speaks reports "I am going to fail this exam" as her example of a prediction.
- Commands as descriptions of future actions whose point is to make the addressee do what is described (§2).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Resting on the intuitive distinction (rejected) (§2).
- That grammatical form fixes the distinction (rejected) (§2; Speaks).
- That an expression of intention is just a (self-fulfilling) prediction (entertained and rejected per Millgram & Bowman).
- That an unexecuted expression of intention was simply a mistaken judgement (resisted: the facts, not the words, are at fault).

## In the secondary literature
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — "prediction" can mean a speech act or an unvoiced belief; Anscombe's question is first about two kinds of utterance as a route to two kinds of state; narrow grammar cannot separate them.
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — traces the §45 Theophrastus remark back to §2: what is impugned is the facts; so the lesson in §45 concerns where the *blame* for falsehood lies, not knowledge of falsehoods.
- [Özaltun 2016](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md) — Theophrastus' point operates in §2, §32 and §45 alike; in each it marks another source of mistake, not knowledge independent of the facts; §2's statements of intention parallel §45.
- [Hubbs 2016](../../sources/secondary/hubbs-2016-intentions-and-commands.md) — §2's command–intention parallel is foundational: both are justified by what it would be good to bring about, not by evidence; execution-conditions correspond to truth-conditions; finds the first use of Theophrastus here.
- [Setiya 2022](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) — cites pp. 4–5 (with §32) as a possible basis for exempting practical knowledge from evidential requirements; [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md) — §2's treating expressions of intention as later true or false supports practical *foreknowledge*.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — Velleman adopts the prediction view Anscombe "considers and dismisses" in §2; the opening and closing prediction discussions (§§2, 50–52) frame the book and show predictability does not contradict intention. (The report on this source flags its use of §2 for a "future-directedness" argument as doubtful.)
- [Millgram & Bowman 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-bowman-2026-sep-practical-reason-structure-of-actions.md) — cites "sec. 2" for a self-fulfilling-prediction view of decision that Anscombe entertains and rejects.
- [Campbell 2022](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2022-practical-knowledge-practical-truth.md) — canonical expression of intention is saying what one is doing or going to do, and why (§2 *et passim*).
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — Ch. 1.2 treats §§2–3; expressions of intention "set the standard of correctness" for the events they describe; his "factualist" reading: in expressing intention one says simply what will happen.
- [Teichmann (ed.) 2022](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2022-oxford-handbook-anscombe.md) — Roessler's chapter (§§2–4 among others) distinguishes errors of performance from errors of practical judgement.
