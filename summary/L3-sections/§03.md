# §03 — Why study the verbal expression; justification by reasons, not evidence

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part I: Preliminaries](../L1-parts.md#part-i-preliminaries) › [G02: §§2–4](../L2-sections/G02-s02-04-intention-for-the-future.md) › **§03** · [← §02](§02.md) · [§04 →](§04.md)

*Pages: from p. 5 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: considering the verbal expression of intention for the future helps avoid dead ends; explaining intention by introspection is useless; expressions of intention differ from estimates of the future in the justification, if any, offered for them.*

## In one sentence
Anscombe defends her focus on what people *say* when expressing intention (introspective description of an inner state leads nowhere) and locates the difference between expressing an intention and estimating the future in the kind of justification each admits: reasons for acting versus evidence.

## Summary
The section first justifies method. Concentrating on the verbal expression of intention for the future keeps the inquiry out of dead ends (§3, contents line). The alternative — trying to say what intention is by looking inward and describing a felt state — is useless: introspection yields nothing that would explain the concept (§3). This is not behaviourism; commentators stress that Anscombe allows intentions that remain "purely interior" (§4), but she declines to make an inner object the explanatory starting point ([Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md); [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md)).

The section then offers the criterion §2 withheld: an expression of intention differs from an estimate of the future by the justification, if any, the speaker gives (§3). An estimate is backed by evidence that the predicted state of affairs will obtain; an expression of intention is backed, if at all, by reasons for acting — considerations showing why it would be good, useful or attractive for what is described to come about (§3; Speaks's paraphrase). †Anscombe frames this roughly as: an expression of intention describes something future in which the speaker is an agent, a description justified (when it is) by reasons for acting rather than by evidence that it is true. Yet she does not rest the account here: the reasons/evidence contrast is only worked out later, under intentional action (§5 ff.), so here it is noted and postponed (Speaks; Holton). Holton's handout records in this stretch (§§2–3) an "odd" Wittgenstein passage about leaves blown about, before the postponement.

## Main claim(s)
- Attending to verbal expression of intention avoids dead ends (§3).
- Introspective description of an inner state does not explain intention (§3).
- Expressions of intention are distinguished from estimates by their justification: reasons for acting versus evidence (§3).
- (Implicitly) the reasons/evidence distinction itself needs the account of intentional action, so the topic is deferred (§3 → §5).

## Examples
- Speaks's illustrations (his, not Anscombe's): "I've failed every other one" (evidence for "I am going to fail") versus "I really want to annoy my parents" (a reason).
- †A Wittgensteinian image of a leaf blown about that "thinks" it is going where it chooses (Holton places a leaves passage in this stretch; precise wording and section unverified).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Introspective accounts of intention (rejected as useless) (§3).
- Grounding the distinction purely in reasons vs. evidence now (deferred, since "reason for acting" is not yet explained) (§3; cf. §5's circularity worry).

## In the secondary literature
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — gives the evidence/reason contrast with his own examples; flags an apparent counterexample ("Because I always do what I want", which works as evidence only if the agent intends); notes that the implicit conclusion — intention vs. prediction cannot be explained by reasons vs. evidence alone at this stage — is not argued.
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — asks why Anscombe works with expressions rather than states (not behaviourism; perhaps Wittgensteinian attention to practice); compares expressions of intention and of belief, noting the English progressive "I am intending" and Wittgenstein (*RPP* I §598).
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — Ch. 1.2–1.3 treat §§2–3 and §§3–4: different justification from estimates; "action first" without behaviourism.
- [Teichmann (ed.) 2022](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2022-oxford-handbook-anscombe.md) — contrasts Schwenkler's "action first" reading of §§3–4 with Capps's intention-in-action-first strategy and Moran & Stone's expression-first unification.
- [Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md) — Moran & Stone (via Piedrahita) list features distinguishing linguistic expressions of intention from behavioural manifestations, explaining why Anscombe denies a natural, non-verbal expression of intention.
- [Özaltun 2016](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md) — reads §§2–3 together: statements about what I did, am doing and will do are expressions of intention when not based on evidence.
