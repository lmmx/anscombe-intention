# §04 — What we can say with fair certainty about others' intentions

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part I: Preliminaries](../L1-parts.md#part-i-preliminaries) › [G02: §§2–4](../L2-sections/G02-s02-04-intention-for-the-future.md) › **§04** · [← §03](§03.md) · [§05 →](§05.md)

*Pages: from p. 7 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: are there statements of the form "A intends X" that can be made with fair certainty? Descriptions of what a man is doing can often truly fill the place of X; and the reasons we take a man to be the sole authority on his own intentions are examined.*

## In one sentence
Shifting from the first to the third person, Anscombe observes that the safest way to say true things about someone's intentions is to say what he is visibly doing, diagnoses the pull of the idea that only the agent can know his intentions, and concludes that what a man actually does is the first, not the last, thing to consider — while conceding that an intention can be purely interior.

## Summary
Anscombe asks whether there are third-person statements "A intends X" that we can make with fair certainty (§4). Her answer is yes: if one wants to say true things about a man's intentions, one does well to state what he is actually doing; descriptions of his action can very often be substituted truly for X (§4, contents line). Anyone who sees her sitting in a chair writing, and who has grown up in the same shared world, would say as much at once ([Wiseman 2025](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2025-philosophy-talk.md), on §4). Most of what observers would say about what someone is doing comes out as descriptions of intentional actions, needing no special verification ([Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md)).

She then examines why we nevertheless suppose a man to be the sole authority on his own intentions (§4, contents). The temptation is a picture on which intention is a purely interior state whose connection to action is inessential, so that what a man actually does would be "the very last thing" to consider; Anscombe replies that it is the first (Speaks's paraphrase of the end of §4). At the same time she grants that a man may form an intention and do nothing to carry it out, so that the intention can be complete while remaining a purely interior thing (§4, per [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md)). The upshot is not behaviourism but a reorientation: the next step is to study intentional action (§5), which is why Part II begins there. Speaks notes the move from intentions-to-act to intentional action is made without much explanation.

## Main claim(s)
- Statements of the form "A intends X" can often be made with fair certainty by describing what A is doing (§4).
- The idea that the agent is the sole authority on his intentions is understandable but rests partly on a misleading interior picture (§4).
- What a man actually does is the first thing to look at in understanding his intentions (§4).
- Nonetheless an intention can be complete and purely interior (never acted on) (§4).

## Examples
- Someone sitting in a chair writing: anyone sharing our world would immediately say what she is doing (§4, per Wiseman and Speaks).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The "purely interior" picture of intention with only an inessential link to action (rejected as a starting point) (§4).
- The agent's sole authority over his intentions (partly explained, partly deflated) (§4).

## In the secondary literature
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — puzzled by §4 as an argument: it shifts heads without explanation, and our ease in telling what people do no more shows what intention *is* than ease in telling predictions from intentions explains that distinction (an epistemic/metaphysical priority confusion; analogy of electrons and detector blips). Preferred reading: an implicit argument that intention-to-act cannot be understood independently of intentional action. His seminar question asks whether this rests on the evidence/reason contrast presupposing intentional action.
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — juxtaposes the end of §4 ("what a man actually does" first) with its admission of complete, purely interior intention.
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — "action first" (Ch. 1.3, §§3–4); per Pellegrini, the chapter's main claim is that Anscombe's method is not behaviourist, since it allows "purely interior" intention.
- [Wiseman 2025](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2025-philosophy-talk.md) — anyone grown "to the age of reason" in a shared world can give descriptions of what someone is doing that are descriptions of intentional actions (witness analogy); further intentions are not visible, which is where "Why?" comes in; concealment is possible but bounded.
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — §§4–5 (pp. 7–9): intentional descriptions can be picked out from the third person; the special "Why?" ties them to the agent's knowledge and reasons.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — reads "I therefore turn …" (§4) as a section marker showing the book executing its own structure; §4 begins the "Twenty Questions" peeling-off of rival topics.
- [Teichmann 2008](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2008-philosophy-of-anscombe.md) — (per a review) inner/outer and first/third-person issues open his treatment of intentional action.

## Notes and uncertainties
- The "grown to the age of reason in a shared world" wording is attested only through Wiseman's radio paraphrase, which the source file marks as unchecked against the text; treat as approximate.
- No edition variants recorded for §4 ([Sandis 2015](../../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md)).
