# §07 — Second refusal: involuntariness, and why it cannot be used yet

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › [G03: §§5–8](../L2-sections/G03-s05-08-why-question-and-its-refusal.md) › **§07** · [← §06](§06.md) · [§08 →](§08.md)

*Pages: from p. 12 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: "Why?" is also refused application when the action was involuntary; but this notion cannot be introduced without treating the very problem as already solved; the notion "involuntary" has difficulties of its own.*

## In one sentence
The obvious second refusal — "it was involuntary" — is noted and then shelved, because "involuntary" is too close to "unintentional" to serve without circularity and is itself unclear.

## Summary
A short, transitional section. Besides ignorance (§6), a natural ground for refusing the "Why?" is that the movement was involuntary (§7, contents line). But Anscombe declines to rely on this. To introduce "involuntary" as a criterion would be to treat as solved the very problem under investigation, since the voluntary/involuntary contrast is just as much in need of explanation as the intentional/unintentional one ([Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md): the threat is circularity). Moreover, the notion "involuntary" is itself troublesome (§7, contents): †it covers heterogeneous things — movements of quite different kinds that we are inclined to call involuntary — and it is not obvious what unifies them or how they relate to what is merely unintentional. The section therefore sets a task rather than completing one: find a way to mark off a class of involuntary movements that does not beg the question. §8 does this, using the notion of knowledge without observation.

## Main claim(s)
- "It was involuntary" is a ground on which "Why?" is refused application (§7).
- But "involuntary" cannot be introduced as a criterion without presupposing the account sought (§7).
- The concept of the involuntary has its own difficulties (§7).

## Examples
- None developed in the saved sources for §7 itself; the falling-asleep jerk that some commentators attach to §7 is placed by the contents line in §8.

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Using voluntariness as an independent criterion of intentional action (rejected as question-begging) (§7).
