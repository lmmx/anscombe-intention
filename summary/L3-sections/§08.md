# §08 — Knowledge without observation, and a non-question-begging class of involuntary movements

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › [G03: §§5–8](../L2-sections/G03-s05-08-why-question-and-its-refusal.md) › **§08** · [← §07](§07.md) · [§09 →](§09.md)

*Pages: from p. 13 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: "non-observational knowledge" is introduced — e.g. knowledge of some of one's own movements — and also non-observational knowledge of what caused a movement (why I gave a start); one class of involuntary movements can then be defined without begging questions: those known without observation but where there is no room for non-observational knowledge of their cause (e.g. the jerk when dropping off to sleep).*

## In one sentence
Anscombe introduces the notion of knowledge "without observation" — illustrated by knowledge of the position of one's limbs — and uses it, together with non-observational knowledge of causes, to sort movements: those known only by observation, those known without observation but whose cause is not, and those whose movement and cause are both so known.

## Summary
To replace the question-begging appeal to "involuntary" (§7), Anscombe introduces *knowledge without observation* (§8). Her paradigm is that one normally knows the position of one's limbs without observation: there are no separately describable sensations on the basis of which one knows it, even if there are sensations of some kind ([Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md); [Wilson & Shpall 2012](../../sources/secondary/wilson-shpall-2012-sep-action.md); Speaks quotes the limb-position passage). Some of one's own movements are known in this way; others — the peristaltic movement of one's gut — are known, if at all, only by observation, and these are already excluded since the "Why?" has no application to them (§8; Speaks; Holton).

Anscombe adds that there can also be non-observational knowledge of the *cause* of a movement: I know without observation why I gave a start (§8, contents line). Holton reports the contrast of jumping back at a crocodile's leap or bark, where the cause is known without observation. This allows a class of involuntary movements to be defined without begging questions: movements known without observation, but where there is no room for non-observational knowledge of their cause — such as the jerk one gives when falling asleep (§8). The intentional actions will thus be a sub-class of things known without observation ([Richter n.d.](../../sources/secondary/richter-nd-iep-anscombe.md) quotes p. 14). But knowledge without observation of movement-plus-cause is not yet sufficient: starting at the crocodile is so known but is not intentional; distinguishing reasons from such mental causes is the work of §§9–11.

## Main claim(s)
- There is knowledge "without observation", e.g. of the position of one's limbs (§8).
- Movements known only by observation (gut) fall outside the "Why?" (§8).
- There is also non-observational knowledge of the causes of some movements (§8).
- Involuntary movements of one kind = known without observation, with no room for non-observational knowledge of their cause (sleep-jerk) (§8).
- Intentional actions are a sub-class of things known without observation (§8, p. 14 per Richter).

## Examples
- Knowing the position of one's limbs without "separately describable sensations" (§8).
- Peristaltic movement of the gut — known only by observation (§8).
- Giving a start; jumping back at a crocodile's leap/bark — cause known without observation (§8, per Holton).
- The jerk on dropping off to sleep — movement known without observation, cause not (§8).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- That the involuntary must be presupposed (answered by the definition via non-observational knowledge) (§§7–8).
- †That limb-position knowledge must rest on sensations as signs (rejected: no separately describable sensations serve as criteria).

## In the secondary literature
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — reconstructs a biconditional (intentional iff non-observational knowledge *that* and *why*) and judges it refuted (self-deception; falling when dizzy); substitutes a knee-jerk for Anscombe's sleep-jerk (his example, not hers); thinks the restriction to "purely physical" descriptions unnecessary.
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — taxonomy of refusals: (ii) known only by observation (gut), (iii) known without observation but cause not (sleep-jerk) vs. crocodile; *critique*: proprioception is often classed as perception; "separately describable sensations" criterion shaky (blindsight, Ian Waterman).
- [Wilson & Shpall 2012](../../sources/secondary/wilson-shpall-2012-sep-action.md) — calls the notion "problematic": proprioception could count as inner observation; seeing a goldfinch is not inference from separable impressions either.
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) — non-observational knowledge is a negative *outline*, not the kernel; practical knowledge (§48) is the core, from which non-observationality follows; cites §7 for the sleep-jerk (contents: §8).
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md), [Campbell 2022](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2022-practical-knowledge-practical-truth.md) — non-observation cannot define practical knowledge, since limb position and mental causes are also known non-observationally.
- [Piñeros Glasscock & Tenenbaum 2023](../../sources/secondary/pineros-glasscock-tenenbaum-2023-sep-action.md) — "wide agreement" that practical knowledge is a proper subclass of non-observational knowledge; but cite §§9–10 for limb-position knowledge, which is §8 material (mis-citation).
- [Setiya 2008](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2008-practical-knowledge.md) — glosses pp. 13–15 as knowing "spontaneously"; finds the limb comparison unhelpful because limb knowledge is receptive.
- [Thompson 1998](../../sources/secondary/thompson-1998-rep-anscombe.md) — refusals (b) known only by observation, (c) no account without observation (§§6–9).
- [Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md) — McDowell on bodily self-knowledge: limb-knowledge is neither practical nor receptive in the ordinary way; Anscombe did not use it to explain knowledge of action.
- [Teichmann (ed.) 2022](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2022-oxford-handbook-anscombe.md) — H. Teichman traces "knowledge without observation" to Wittgenstein's limb-position remarks.
- [Richter n.d.](../../sources/secondary/richter-nd-iep-anscombe.md) — quotes p. 14: intentional actions a sub-class of things known without observation.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — §§6–8 clear away rival kinds of non-observational knowledge in advance ("Twenty Questions").

## Notes and uncertainties
- The 1957 Aristotelian Society paper omits these pages and merely asserts that one can give the cause of a thought as one states the position of one's limbs, "not based on observation" ([Sandis 2015](../../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md)).
- † = writer's recollection of the text, not corroborated by the contents line or a saved source; check against the book. Applies to the framing of the "sensations as signs" objection.
- Whether the crocodile case belongs to §8 or §9 is not settled by the sources; Holton cites it with §8 and §9's remark that the causation itself is known without observation.
