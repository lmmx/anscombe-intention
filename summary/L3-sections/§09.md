# §09 — Evidence, causes, and the hard cases between cause and reason

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › [G04: §§9–11](../L2-sections/G04-s09-11-mental-causality.md) › **§09** · [← §08](§08.md) · [§10 →](§10.md)

*Pages: from p. 15 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: in one sense of "Why?" the answer cites evidence; but an answer that does not cite evidence does not on that account give a reason for acting; the cases in which it is hard to tell cause from reason prove to be those involving non-observational knowledge of causation.*

## In one sentence
Having excluded the evidential "Why?", Anscombe notes that not every non-evidential answer gives a reason for acting, and locates the real difficulty in cases where the agent knows without observation what caused his movement.

## Summary
"Why?" can ask for evidence — why do you think so? — and then the answer cites grounds for a belief (§9, contents line). That is plainly not the sense relevant to intentional action. But it does not follow that any answer which does not mention evidence thereby gives a reason for acting (§9). Many answers give causes. Where the cause is known only by observation or inference, as a physiologist might know it, the case is easily separated from reason-giving. The cases where cause and reason are genuinely hard to tell apart turn out to be precisely those in which the agent has non-observational knowledge of the causation of his own movement (§9): he knows, without looking or inferring, *what made him* do it. Holton records §9's remark that in such cases the causation itself is known without observation ([Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md)). This isolates the class that §10 names "mental causality" and that must be distinguished from reasons, motives and intentions in §§10–15.

## Main claim(s)
- There is an evidential sense of "Why?", irrelevant to intentional action (§9).
- An answer not mentioning evidence is not thereby a reason for acting (§9).
- The difficult cause/reason cases are those where causation is known without observation (§9).

## Examples
- No example of Anscombe's for §9 is recorded in the saved sources beyond the contents line; Speaks illustrates the evidential sense with his own case (a cafeteria sign).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Treating "non-evidential answer" as equivalent to "reason for acting" (rejected) (§9).

## In the secondary literature
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — groups §§9–11 as "reasons and mental causes"; argues these sections claim only that some mental causes are not reasons and some reasons not mental causes — *not* that reasons are not causes (contrast §12; see G05).
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — §9: causation itself known without observation; in his taxonomy the crocodile case contrasts with the sleep-jerk.
- [Thompson 1998](../../sources/secondary/thompson-1998-rep-anscombe.md) — refusal (c): agent knows without observation that something is going on but can give no account, or none without observation (§§6–9); then mental causality excluded by further tests (§§9–11).
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — §§9–10 (p. 16): knocking a cup off because one saw a face at the window is known without observation yet not intentional — decisive against defining practical knowledge by non-observation.
- [Piñeros Glasscock & Tenenbaum 2023](../../sources/secondary/pineros-glasscock-tenenbaum-2023-sep-action.md) — cite §§9–10 for knowledge of limb position, which is §8 material (mis-citation).
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — Ch. 2.3 (§§9–16) on reason, motive and cause; argues against Davidson's reading of Anscombe as anti-causalist.
