# §10 — "Mental causality": distinguished from motives and from objects of feeling

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › [G04: §§9–11](../L2-sections/G04-s09-11-mental-causality.md) › **§10** · [← §09](§09.md) · [§11 →](§11.md)

*Pages: from p. 16 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: the kind of causation identified in §9 is labelled "mental causality"; mental causes are distinguished from motives and from the objects of feelings.*

## In one sentence
Anscombe names as "mental causes" whatever an agent can report, without observation, as what made him move or feel as he did, and separates such causes both from motives and from the objects of emotions.

## Summary
The causation isolated in §9 — causation of one's movement or feeling that one knows without observation — is labelled "mental causality" (§10). A mental cause is what the subject would describe if asked what produced the movement or feeling; it need not itself be a mental event, since a perceived external event (a noise, something seen) can qualify, as well as a thought, image or feeling ([Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md)). Typical answers have the form "I saw such-and-such and it made me jump" ([Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md), p. 16), or knocking a cup off the table because one saw a face at the window ([Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md), p. 16). Thompson's illustration is a gasp at the hissing of a snake ([Thompson 1998](../../sources/secondary/thompson-1998-rep-anscombe.md)). Such answers satisfy the epistemic conditions of §§6–8 yet do not give reasons for acting.

Two distinctions are drawn (§10, contents). (i) Mental causes are not motives: the motive of an act is not what "made" one do it in this sense (developed in §§12–14). (ii) The mental cause of a feeling must be distinguished from its object. Anscombe's example: a child saw a bit of red stuff on a turn in a stairway, asked what it was, and thought his nurse said it was a bit of Satan (she presumably said "satin"); what he feared was the red stuff — the *object* of the fear — while the *cause* of his fright was the nurse's remark (§10; corroborated, see Notes). Object and mental cause can coincide (e.g. what one is afraid of may also be what made one afraid), which Speaks takes to suggest that some reasons might also be mental causes.

## Main claim(s)
- Causation of one's own movements or feelings known without observation is "mental causality" (§10).
- A mental cause may be an external event perceived, not only an inner episode (§10; Speaks).
- Mental causes are distinct from motives (§10; §12).
- The mental cause of an emotion is distinct from (though it may coincide with) its object (§10).

## Examples
- "I saw such-and-such and it made me jump" (p. 16, per Holton).
- Knocking a cup off the table because one saw a face at the window (p. 16, per Frey).
- The child, the red stuff on a turn in a stairway and the nurse's misheard remark (object vs. mental cause of fear); Holton lists the case among mental-cause examples (corroborated, see Notes).
- "The martial music excites me, that is why I walk up and down": a mental cause of an action (§10; see Notes).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Assimilating mental causes to motives (rejected) (§10).
- Identifying the object of an emotion with its cause (rejected as a general thesis) (§10).
- A Humean conception of causality: Anscombe remarks that this sort of causality fits Hume's explanations so poorly that those who think Hume dealt with causality would leave it out or call "cause" inappropriate or equivocal, or might give a Humean account of the outside observer's recognition of the cause, "hardly for the patient's" (§10; corroborated, see Notes).

## In the secondary literature
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — mental cause = something in the agent's stream of consciousness or a perceived event leading to action; §10's object/cause parallel suggests some reasons can be mental causes; key interpretive claim that §§9–11 do not assert "reasons are not causes".
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — "mental causation" is a special usage; Anscombe says little about causation in *Intention* and seems too unsure what causation is to draw big conclusions. *Divergence*: Holton's taxonomy files mental-cause cases under "the question gets application", whereas the §16 summary treats an answer giving a (mental) cause as not granting it.
- [Thompson 1998](../../sources/secondary/thompson-1998-rep-anscombe.md) — behaviour passing the epistemic tests is intentional unless it is mental causality (gasp at a snake), excluded by more complex tests.
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — the face-at-the-window case shows non-observational, non-inferential knowledge does not define practical knowledge (against Hursthouse, Setiya, Velleman and others).
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) — "because I saw a face at the window" answers a different sense of "Why did you jump?", revealing the jump to be unintentional.
- [Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md) — Hornsby: the reason-vs-cause framing of the Anscombe/Davidson dispute misses a deeper disagreement about agents' causal powers.
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) note 8, [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — both warn against labelling Anscombe an "anti-causalist" (*contrast* [Richter n.d.](../../sources/secondary/richter-nd-iep-anscombe.md), whose §§10–12 gloss is that intentions/motives are not causes, and [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md), an avowed anti-causalist reader).
