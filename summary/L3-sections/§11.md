# §11 — Mental causes versus intentions expressed by "I wanted …"

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › [G04: §§9–11](../L2-sections/G04-s09-11-mental-causality.md) › **§11** · [← §10](§10.md) · [§12 →](§12.md)

*Pages: from p. 17 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: mental causes are also to be distinguished from the intentions with which one acts, even though these may be expressed in the form "I wanted …"; mental causality is not important in itself, but the distinctions are.*

## In one sentence
Even when an agent's further intention is voiced as "I wanted …", it is not a mental cause; and Anscombe adds that mental causality matters to her only as a foil for these distinctions.

## Summary
Because answers giving intentions with which one acts are often phrased "I wanted …", it is tempting to treat the wanting as an inner event that caused the action — a mental cause in the sense of §10. Anscombe denies this: the intention with which one acts is distinct from a mental cause, whatever the grammatical form of its expression (§11, contents line). Speaks reads the section as also implying that a reason for acting need not be any conscious episode at all; a desire on which one acts may never have "occurred" as an item in consciousness, so it cannot be the sort of reportable producing event that a mental cause is ([Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md); the apple-fetching illustration is his). †Anscombe's own illustrations of genuine mental causes in this stretch include music that excites one to march up and down.

The section closes with a methodological remark: mental causality is not of great importance in itself; what matters are the distinctions it forces — between mental cause and reason, motive, object and intention (§11, contents). The ground is thus cleared for the treatment of motives (§§12–15).

## Main claim(s)
- Intentions with which one acts are not mental causes, even when expressed as "I wanted …" (§11).
- Mental causality is unimportant in itself; the distinctions are important (§11).
- (Speaks's reading) some reasons are not mental causes, since they need never become conscious episodes.

## Examples
- Answers of the form "I wanted …" expressing a further intention (§11).
- †Music exciting one to walk/march up and down as a mental cause.

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Construing "I wanted …" as reporting an inner causal antecedent (rejected) (§11).

## In the secondary literature
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — (i) some mental causes are not reasons; (ii) some reasons are not mental causes (§11: a reason may never become conscious). Hence these sections do not assert that reasons are not causes, only that some are not *mental* causes.
- [Thompson 1998](../../sources/secondary/thompson-1998-rep-anscombe.md) — §§9–11 as the final filter excluding mental causality.
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — Anscombe is "not much interested" in whether reasons exclude causes; "mental causation" is a special usage.
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — against Davidson's reading of her as anti-causalist (Ch. 2.3).
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — reads the mental-cause material as ground-clearing within a whole-book design; whether mental states *implement* intention is a question he takes Anscombe to render philosophically idle.
