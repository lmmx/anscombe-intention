# §12 — Motive and intention; a motive is not a cause

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › [G05: §§12–15](../L2-sections/G05-s12-15-motives.md) › **§12** · [← §11](§11.md) · [§13 →](§13.md)

*Pages: from p. 18 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: philosophers sharply distinguish motives from intentions and describe motives as causes; in popular usage the two are less distinct, and "motive" is the wider notion; a motive is not a cause at all.*

## In one sentence
Against a philosophical picture that separates motives sharply from intentions and treats them as causes of action, Anscombe follows ordinary usage in which "motive" is a wider notion that includes the intention with which one acts, and denies outright that a motive is a cause.

## Summary
Philosophers have commonly drawn a sharp line between a man's *intention* (what he aims at) and his *motive* (what moves him), and have described motives as causes of action (§12, contents line). Schwenkler identifies Gilbert Ryle's *Concept of Mind* — where motives are treated dispositionally — as the implicit target ([Schwenkler c2020](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-c2020-316-interview.md)). Anscombe observes that in popular usage the distinction is not so sharp: we readily say that someone's motive was to get money, i.e. we use "motive" for the intention with which he acted; and "motive" covers more than such intentions (§12). So intentions with which one acts are one species of motive.

The decisive claim is negative: a motive is not a cause at all (§12, contents). Giving a motive does not report what *made* one act, in the sense of mental causality (§§10–11). Rather it helps us understand the action: "His love of truth caused him to …" means rather "He did this in that he loved the truth" — it interprets his action (§12; see Notes). The formula that to give a motive is to say something like "See the action in this light" comes in §13. Speaks finds the notion of "interpreting" an action left "pretty much open", and notes that if knowing motives is knowing reasons, the section seems to say reasons are not causes — at least in the restricted sense of "mental cause" ([Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md)). Holton dates the explicit entry of intention-with-which into the "Why?" inquiry to this section ([Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md)).

## Main claim(s)
- Philosophical accounts wrongly make motives sharply distinct from intentions and treat them as causes (§12).
- In ordinary usage "motive" is wider than, and includes, "intention with which" (§12).
- A motive is not a cause (§12).
- Giving a motive interprets the action rather than citing what produced it (§12; developed in §13).

## Examples
- "His motive was …" used to state a further intention (e.g. gain) (§12, per the contents line's claim that popular usage does not sharply separate them).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The philosophers' view that motives are causes (rejected) (§12).
- The sharp philosophical separation of motive from intention (rejected as unfaithful to usage) (§12).

## In the secondary literature
- [Schwenkler c2020](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-c2020-316-interview.md), [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — the motive discussion (§§12–15) is "helpfully read as a response to" Ryle; the guide (Ch. 2.3) compares Anscombe with Ryle and argues against Davidson's reading of her as anti-causalist.
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — "interpret" left open; if motives are reasons, the section seems to deny reasons are causes, unclear whether only as *mental* causes; concedes §12 leans further than his §§9–11 reading. (The source evaluation flags tension between Speaks's "no anti-causal claim" reading and §12's contents line "a motive is not a cause at all".)
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — §12 is where intention's relevance to "Why?" is first raised, so intention-with-which is implicit well before §22 (against Wiseman's start-point).
- [Richter n.d.](../../sources/secondary/richter-nd-iep-anscombe.md) — (implicitly §§10–12) intentions/motives are not causes: the intention is "a part or an aspect of the act"; his climber argument is not Anscombe's (it is best known from Davidson).
- [Teichmann 2008](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2008-philosophy-of-anscombe.md) — (per review) "a motive is a reason, not a cause" among the grammatical distinctions treated.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — §12 among passages disclaiming moralising; motives "peeled off" as rivals.
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) note 8 — Anscombe was as critical of modern conceptions of causation as of causal action theory; *contrast* readings (Richter, Frey) that make her straightforwardly anti-causalist.
