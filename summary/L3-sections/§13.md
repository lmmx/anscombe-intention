# §13 — Backward-looking motives and motive-in-general

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › [G05: §§12–15](../L2-sections/G05-s12-15-motives.md) › **§13** · [← §12](§12.md) · [§14 →](§14.md)

*Pages: from p. 20 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: among motives that are not intentions for the future are backward-looking motives such as revenge ("I killed him because he killed my brother") and motive-in-general ("I did it out of friendship"), which may also be called an "interpretative" motive.*

## In one sentence
Anscombe distinguishes, within the class of motives, those that look forward (further intentions) from those that look back to something past (revenge, gratitude) and from "motive-in-general" (friendship, curiosity), which interprets an action rather than naming an aim.

## Summary
Having widened "motive" to include intention with which (§12), Anscombe marks off motives that are *not* intentions for the future (§13, contents line). The first kind is the **backward-looking motive**: the answer to "Why?" cites something in the past as the ground of the action, as in revenge — "I killed him because he killed my brother" (§13). Gratitude is the commentators' standard companion example ([Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md), on Müller). The second kind is **motive-in-general**, as in "I did it out of friendship" — answers such as "out of friendship", "out of curiosity", "from admiration" do not name a past event or a future aim but characterise the spirit or light in which the deed is to be understood; hence Anscombe also calls them "interpretative" motives (§13). Both kinds give answers to "Why?" that grant it application, so they count among the reasons for acting that mark intentional action (cf. §16's list: past history; interpretation; something future). §14 then asks what distinguishes a backward-looking motive from a mere mental cause, since both point to something prior.

## Main claim(s)
- Some motives are not intentions for the future (§13).
- Backward-looking motives cite something past as ground (revenge) (§13).
- Motive-in-general (friendship, etc.) is "interpretative": it places the act in a light (§13).

## Examples
- "I killed him because he killed my brother" (revenge) (§13).
- "I did it out of friendship" (motive-in-general) (§13).
- Gratitude as a backward-looking motive (via Müller).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- That in a backward-looking motive the past event is just a mental cause and not a reason. §14 opens with exactly this question (corroborated, see Notes).

## In the secondary literature
- [Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md) — Müller: Anscombe distinguishes forward- from backward-looking reasons (§§13–14) but refuses to count acting on the latter as practical reasoning; Müller calls this a mistake and argues backward-looking responders also "calculate". *Divergence*: Piedrahita replies that *Intention* never aimed to cover all practical rationality via the syllogism — backward-looking reasons delimit intentional action without being intentions.
- [Wiland & Driver 2022](../../sources/secondary/wiland-driver-2022-sep-anscombe.md) — the §16 list includes interpretive ("Out of curiosity") and past-ground ("They attacked us first") answers; call the list a "hodgepodge" from which Anscombe shifts method.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — backward-looking motives (§13) are later classed under desirability characterisations.
- [Millgram & Bowman 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-bowman-2026-sep-practical-reason-structure-of-actions.md) — cite "sec. 13" for a quoted deferral ("Consider this … let us not consider it here"), not verified against the text.
