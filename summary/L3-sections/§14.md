# §14 — What separates backward-looking motives from mental causes: good and harm

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › [G05: §§12–15](../L2-sections/G05-s12-15-motives.md) › **§14** · [← §13](§13.md) · [§15 →](§15.md)

*Pages: from p. 21 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: what distinguishes backward-looking motives from mental causes? The notions of good and harm are involved in them.*

## In one sentence
A backward-looking motive differs from a mental cause because it presents the past event as a good or harm to which the action is a fitting response — something a mere "it made me do it" does not.

## Summary
Both a backward-looking motive ("because he killed my brother") and a mental cause ("the noise made me jump") cite something prior to the action. So what distinguishes them (§14)? Anscombe's answer is that backward-looking motives involve the notions of good and harm (§14, contents line): revenge is doing harm to someone *as a return for* harm he did; gratitude is doing good *in return for* good received. The agent's conception of the past event as harm or benefit, and of the act as a response of the appropriate kind, is internal to the motive. Mental causes are not sensitive in this way to whether the agent thought of good or harm ([Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md), citing p. 22): a startle is not a response to anything conceived as good or bad. This makes the backward-looking motive a reason, and its answer a ground of action (cf. §16's "past history whose relevance depends on the agent's taking it as good or bad", per Speaks's paraphrase of §16). Speaks thinks the first paragraph of §14 implies that some reasons can also be mental causes ([Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md)).

## Main claim(s)
- Backward-looking motives must be distinguished from mental causes, though both cite the past (§14).
- The distinguishing mark is the involvement of the notions of good and harm (§14).

## Examples
- Revenge (harm for harm) and, by parallel, gratitude (good for good) (§§13–14).
- Mental causes (being made to jump) as the contrast (§§10, 14; p. 22 per Holton).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Treating backward-looking motives as mental causes (rejected) (§14).
