# §18 — The range of answers to "Why?" defines the class of intentional actions

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part II: The Question "Why?"](../L1-parts.md#part-ii-the-question-why) › [G06: §§16–18](../L2-sections/G06-s16-18-the-range-of-answers.md) › **§18** · [← §17](§17.md) · [§19 →](§19.md)

*Pages: from p. 26 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: that "For no particular reason" is a possible answer does not show that it always makes sense; "not making sense" means that we cannot understand the man, not that some form of words is excluded from the language; the "Why?" in question is identified as one that expects an answer within the range described, and that range defines the class of intentional actions.*

## In one sentence
"For no particular reason" is a possible answer but not always an intelligible one — unintelligibility being a failure of understanding the person, not a rule of language — and the special "Why?" is finally identified as the question that looks for an answer within the range catalogued in §§5–17, which range marks out intentional action.

## Summary
§17 allowed that "For no particular reason" accepts the question. §18 adds that it does not follow that this answer makes sense in every case (§18). Anscombe's illustration is a man who hunts out all the green books in his house and carefully spreads them on the roof: if asked why, and he says "for no particular reason", we cannot understand him; his words would be intelligible only as joking or mystification (§18; corroborated, see Notes). "Not making sense" here does not mean that a form of words is excluded by the rules of the language; it means that we cannot understand the man (§18, contents line). So the pointless answer is acceptable for some actions (idle, casual doings) and not for others, where we need a point to see what he is up to at all.

Anscombe then states the upshot of Part II: the relevant "Why?" is the one that expects an answer in the range she has described (further intention, interpretation, backward-looking ground, or "no particular reason", and not evidence or cause), and it is this range of answers that defines the class of intentional actions (§18). The definition is thus not psychological — not a matter of what goes on in the agent — but a matter of which question, with which range of answers, applies ([Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) cites the end of §18 as evidence of Anscombe's anti-psychologism). Small takes Anscombe's own description of her aim here as outlining "the area of intentional actions" ([Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md)). §19 asks what, then, calling an action "intentional" adds.

## Main claim(s)
- That "For no particular reason" is sometimes a possible answer does not make it always intelligible (§18).
- Unintelligibility = our inability to understand the agent, not exclusion of words from the language (§18).
- The special "Why?" is the one expecting an answer in the described range; the range defines intentional action (§18).

## Examples
- Spreading all the green books in one's house on the roof "for no particular reason" (§18; corroborated, see Notes).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- That if "no particular reason" is sometimes allowed it must always be allowed (rejected) (§18).
- That "not making sense" is a matter of linguistic rules (rejected) (§18).

## In the secondary literature
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) — §§17–18 endorse, with qualifications, intentional action for no reason; Anscombe's aim is to "outline the area of intentional actions" (§18).
