# §21 — No proof of a single final end, but chains of reasons must exist

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part III: The Unity of Action](../L1-parts.md#part-iii-the-unity-of-action) › [G07: §§19–21](../L2-sections/G07-s19-21-no-extra-feature-and-chains-of-reasons.md) › **§21** · [← §20](§20.md) · [§22 →](§22.md)

*Pages: from p. 33 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: a criticism of the Aristotelian proof that there is a final end for a man's actions; nevertheless, if there is to be intentional action at all, some chains of reasons for acting must occur.*

## In one sentence
Anscombe rejects Aristotle's argument for one final end of all human action, while drawing from §20 a weaker, defensible claim: intentional action requires that at least some actions be done for further ends, forming chains of reasons.

## Summary
§20 concluded that intentional action presupposes the practice of giving further intentions. §21 tests this against a famous historical thesis. Aristotle argued that there must be a final end for the sake of which everything else is done (contents line). The argument Anscombe criticises is the opening of the *Nicomachean Ethics* (I.2, 1094a18–26), which infers from the impossibility of an infinite regress of ends that there is some one end desired for its own sake. Her criticism is that the inference is fallacious: from "all chains must stop somewhere" it does not follow that "there is somewhere where all chains must stop" (corroborated, see Notes). [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) adds that §21 still vindicates a weaker form of the classical thought that human beings must always act with some end in view: without some chains of reasons there would be no concept of intentional action.

Even so, she concedes something to the classical view (contents line): not every action can be "for no particular reason", so if there are intentional actions at all, there must be some chains of reasons — action A done for B, B for C. [Speaks](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) puts this as a weakened version of the claim that human beings always act for an end: without some acting with ends in view we would lack the concept of intentional action. The section thus prepares §22's examination of intention with which and the series of descriptions of §§23–26.

## Main claim(s)
- The Aristotelian proof of a single final end for a man's actions fails (§21, contents line).
- Some chains of reasons for acting must occur wherever there is intentional action (§21, contents line; cf. §20).

## Examples
- No concrete examples are recorded in the saved sources. The Aristotelian argument discussed is *NE* I.2, 1094a18–26 (per Rossi; see Notes).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The Aristotelian thesis that all action is ordered to one final end (rejected as unproven).
- The opposite extreme, that all action might be for no reason (excluded by §20).
