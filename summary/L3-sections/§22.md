# §22 — Intention with which, when it looks to the future

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part III: The Unity of Action](../L1-parts.md#part-iii-the-unity-of-action) › [G08: §§22–27](../L2-sections/G08-s22-27-intention-with-which-and-the-a-d-order.md) › **§22** · [← §21](§21.md) · [§23 →](§23.md)

*Pages: from p. 34 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: the intention with which someone acts, where it mentions something future. For it to be true that the agent does P so that Q, he must regard acknowledging "But if P, Q won't happen" as incompatible with having that intention in acting.*

## In one sentence
Anscombe opens the "closer examination" of the third head, intention with which, and gives a test for when a future state is the intention with which something is done: the agent cannot both have that intention and accept that doing P will not lead to Q.

## Summary
With §§20–21 having shown that intentional action requires further intentions, §22 turns to examining them (Holton reports §22 announcing this "closer examination"). Answers to "Why?" that express an intention with which one acts either give a wider description of what one is now doing or mention a future state of affairs the activity is meant to lead to ([Speaks](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md); [Thompson 1998](../../sources/secondary/thompson-1998-rep-anscombe.md): the chain of "Why?" answers may be "pressed into the future").

The section's central claim concerns the future-mentioning case (contents line). To say that someone does P in order that Q (or so that Q) is to say something about his stance: he must treat the acknowledgement "But if P, Q won't happen" as incompatible with having that intention. The point is not that he must be certain that P will bring about Q, nor that the connection must be real; what matters is the agent's own grasp of P as a way to Q. [Millgram](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) reports the example of a bumpkin who thinks an authority can cancel an eclipse: the agent's "working sense" of what brings about what is sufficient, and no causal theory of the means–end link is needed. Millgram also notes Anscombe's remark here that a counter-prediction ("Q won't happen") is "in some way" to contradict the intention — a theme taken up in §31 and §52.

The test gives the intention with which a logical, rather than introspective, criterion: it is shown by what the agent can and cannot coherently accept, not by an inner episode (cf. §§19, 27).

## Main claim(s)
- Intention with which may be expressed by a wider description of the present action or by mention of something future (§22; Speaks, Thompson).
- If A does P in order that Q, A must count "if P, then not Q" as incompatible with that intention (§22, contents line).
- What links P to Q is the agent's own view of the means, not a correct causal connection (per Millgram's reading of the eclipse example).

## Examples
- The eclipse case: someone acting so that an authority will cancel an eclipse ([Millgram](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md)).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- That intention with which is fixed by an inner state or feeling (implicitly excluded; cf. §19, §27).
- † That only a well-founded means–end belief can underwrite an intention with which (the eclipse example tells against this).
