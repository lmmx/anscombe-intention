# §25 — "I didn't care about that": knowledge, indifference and the limits of avowal

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part III: The Unity of Action](../L1-parts.md#part-iii-the-unity-of-action) › [G08: §§22–27](../L2-sections/G08-s22-27-intention-with-which-and-the-a-d-order.md) › **§25** · [← §24](§24.md) · [§26 →](§26.md)

*Pages: from p. 41 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: suppose the man knows the water will poison the inhabitants but says "I didn't care about that; I was only doing my job of pumping". This answer does not fall within the range of answers to "Why?" that define intentional action. Can a person settle what his intentions are just by what he says they are? Why are a man's intentions of interest apart from what he actually did?*

## In one sentence
The pumper who knows of the poison but says he only cared about his pay gives an answer that neither refuses "Why?" nor supplies a reason in the defining range, and Anscombe uses the case to ask how far an agent's own say-so fixes his intention, and why intentions matter apart from deeds.

## Summary
§24 raised puzzles about the identity of the pumping and the poisoning. §25 raises a different difficulty, about *knowledge without intention* (contents line). Suppose the pumper knows the water is poisoned, and when asked why he is replenishing the house with poisoned water replies that he did not care about that: he wanted his pay and just went on with his usual job ([Holton](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md), who attributes the passage to §24; see below).

This answer is anomalous with respect to the classification of Part II (contents line). It is not "I didn't know" (§6) — he knew — nor knowledge by observation only (§8); so "Why?" is not refused application. Yet the answer gives no reason for poisoning: it is not a further intention, a backward-looking motive or an interpretation (§16), and it is not quite "for no particular reason" (§17). Anscombe's case thus marks a place where what the agent does knowingly and the intention with which he acts can come apart. In §17 Anscombe announces that "we shall see (§25)" ordinary cases where "Why?" is not shown to be inapplicable "and yet is not granted application" (see Notes). The contents line likewise says the answer is not within the range of answers that defines intentional action. What exact verdict Anscombe gives on the poisoning, which the man does knowingly, should be checked against the text.

The case raises a methodological question (contents line): can someone determine his intentions simply by declaring them? Holton reports Anscombe's answer as negative in effect: testing whether the man really "didn't care" does not push the question back into his mind, because there is a "control of the truthfulness of the answer" — what he would and would not do if the poisoning were not going to come about, and what else he does. Finally Anscombe asks why a man's intentions should interest us apart from what he actually did (contents line) — a question that bears on the use of the concept in ethics and law.

[Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md) notes that in this section Anscombe moves without comment from present-progressive questions ("Why are you moving your arm?") to perfective past ones ("Why did you replenish the water-supply with poisoned water?").

## Main claim(s)
- An agent may knowingly bring about something (the poisoning) while giving an answer to "Why?" that is outside the range defining intentional action (§25, contents line).
- One cannot fix one's intention merely by saying what it is; the truthfulness of such avowals is subject to control (§25; Holton).
- There is a question to be answered about the interest of intentions as distinct from deeds (§25, contents line).

## Examples
- The pumper who "didn't care", wanting only his pay ([Holton](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md)).
- Holton also mentions the curious clicking of the pump as a knowingly produced effect the man is indifferent to (placement within §§24–25 not fixed).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- That an agent's sincere disavowal settles what his intention was (rejected in effect).
- That anything known to be brought about is thereby what one intends (the case puts pressure on this).

## In the secondary literature
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — the second of three "break points" in the A–D chain (not knowing; knowing but not caring; uncertainty of outcome); "control of the truthfulness"; praises the passage as prescient on genuine indifference. **Mis-citation:** gives it as §24.
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — Ch. 3.4 "Intention and foresight" (§§24–25, 27): how foreseen consequences relate to an action differently from what is brought about intentionally.
- [Schwenkler c2020 (3:16)](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-c2020-316-interview.md) — **self-correction:** for Anscombe the foresight/intention line is what falls within practical knowledge; the alternative formulation via the means–end order elicited by "Why?" fails for side-effects ("Why are you polluting the river?" — "To increase profits"), and his guide gets this "flatly wrong".
- [Teichmann (ed.) 2022](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2022-oxford-handbook-anscombe.md) — Michon: Anscombe allows broad and narrow scopes of intention, which can ground moral differences relevant to double effect (§§24–27).
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — the "only doing my job" evasion as part of the pumping model's limits.
- [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md) — shift to past-perfective "Why?" as evidence that practical knowledge extends to what has been done.
- [Wiseman 2025](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2025-philosophy-talk.md) — Truman case: the deaths were the means, not a by-product, so lack of warm feeling toward the outcome is irrelevant (Wiseman's application, bearing on §25).
- [Teichmann 2008](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2008-philosophy-of-anscombe.md) — ethics chapter on intention vs. consequences relevant to §§24–25.

## Notes and uncertainties
- **Section placement:** the contents line puts the "didn't care" case in §25; Holton cites §24. §§24–25 both start on p. 41.
- **Verification pass:** the earlier † claim that Anscombe treats the poisoning as done "in a sense intentionally" while denying it is his intention was not corroborated and has been replaced. The replacement rests on the §17 forward reference to §25 in the course-hosted copy of the 1963 text of §§1–18 (http://fitelson.org/proseminar/anscombe.pdf; identifiable as the 1963 text by the variants Sandis records, e.g. "imperative", "most of the earliest historical traditions", "To clarify") and on the contents line. The precise verdict on whether the poisoning is "intentional" for the indifferent pumper is disputed among readers (cf. Schwenkler's self-correction; Michon's broad/narrow scopes) and should be checked in the text.
- † The line "roughly speaking, a man intends to do what he does" is attributed to Anscombe in the [Wiseman 2025](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2025-philosophy-talk.md) broadcast: host Josh Landy quotes it and Wiseman stresses "roughly speaking". The transcript gives no section. The writer placed it in §25, and that placement is unverified, so it has been moved here from the Summary. It is not in §4, whose related remark is that the question whether he intends to do what he does usually does not arise.
