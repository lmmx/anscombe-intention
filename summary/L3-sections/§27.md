# §27 — Is there a place for an interior act of intention?

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part III: The Unity of Action](../L1-parts.md#part-iii-the-unity-of-action) › [G08: §§22–27](../L2-sections/G08-s22-27-intention-with-which-and-the-a-d-order.md) › **§27** · [← §26](§26.md) · [§28 →](§28.md)

*Pages: from p. 47 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: is there ever room for an interior act of intention which really settles what is going on under the heading of such-and-such a kind of action?*

## In one sentence
Anscombe asks whether an inner act — e.g. saying to oneself "I intend …" — could be what really determines which kind of action is being done, and answers that such an act does not, in general, fix the description of what one is doing.

## Summary
Having located intention in the order of descriptions of what is done (§26), Anscombe considers a residual temptation: that, at least sometimes, what an agent is doing is settled by an interior act of intending (contents line). Such an episode — inwardly saying "I hereby intend…" — could occur without the intention, for instance as a joke ([Speaks](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md)), and the intention can be present without any such episode. So the inner performance cannot be what makes the action fall under its intentional description. The discussion of the pumper in §25 (one cannot settle one's intention by saying what it is) is here generalised: what one is doing is not fixed by an act of the mind behind the action.

The contents line is phrased as a question ("is there *ever* place…"). The saved and consulted sources do not report whether Anscombe allows any restricted role for an interior act here, so this summary records only the general negative answer. [Narboux 2020](../../sources/secondary/narboux-2020-anscombes-account-of-voluntary-action.md) treats §27 as answering the difficulty raised in §25, where it seemed that "only you can know" one's intention, intention being thought of as a special internal movement (citing §27, p. 48, with §25). Narboux locates Anscombe's critique of "directing" one's intention by a little speech to oneself in §25 of *Intention* (with "War and Murder"), not in §27; [Wiland & Driver](../../sources/secondary/wiland-driver-2022-sep-anscombe.md) likewise report the rejection of "direction of intention" from "War and Murder".

§27 closes Part III and leads into Part IV, where the temptation to "interiorise" returns as a problem about knowledge (§29: acts of will; §30: initiating events).

## Main claim(s)
- An interior act of intention does not in general determine what kind of action is going on (§27, contents line).
- Such acts can occur without the intention and the intention without them (Speaks's report).

## Examples
- Inwardly saying "I hereby intend…" as a joke ([Speaks](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md)).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The "two-factor" picture: intentional action = bodily movement + inner act of intending (Speaks's label for the target of §§27–31).

## In the secondary literature

- [Wiseman 2025](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2025-philosophy-talk.md) — Anscombe drops the idea that every intentional doing has an inner performance as its cause, while allowing that one can inwardly say "I'm going to…" or have unacted intentions.
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — groups §27 with §§24–25 under "Intention and foresight" (Ch. 3.4), suggesting a link to the indifferent pumper (headings only).
