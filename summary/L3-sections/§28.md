# §28 — Knowing what one is doing without looking

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part IV: Knowledge Without Observation](../L1-parts.md#part-iv-knowledge-without-observation) › [G09: §§28–30](../L2-sections/G09-s28-30-knowledge-of-ones-own-action.md) › **§28** · [← §27](§27.md) · [§29 →](§29.md)

*Pages: from p. 49 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: a further enquiry into knowledge without observation — the knowledge a person has of his own intentional actions; I can say what I am doing without looking to see.*

## In one sentence
Anscombe returns to §8's "knowledge without observation" and extends it from bodily movements to intentional actions under their wider descriptions, raising the difficulty that what one is doing (e.g. replenishing a water-supply) often reaches far beyond what one could know without looking.

## Summary
Part II introduced knowledge without observation for bodily position and movement (§8) and made it one of the marks of intentional action. §28 makes "the knowledge that a man has of his intentional actions" the explicit topic (pp. 50–51, as cited in the editors' introduction to [Haddock & Wiseman 2022](../../sources/secondary/haddock-wiseman-2022-anscombean-mind.md)). The datum: I can say what I am doing without looking to see (contents line).

Anscombe's comparison with knowledge of the position of one's limbs (§8) is only partly helpful: as [Setiya 2008](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2008-practical-knowledge.md) notes (pp. 49–50), she also distinguishes such knowledge, which is "speculative" or receptive, from knowledge of what one is doing. The difficulty, as [Schwenkler](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) (per Pellegrini) puts it, concerns descriptions of action in terms of what is happening at a distance — spatial or temporal — from the agent and his immediate movements: painting a wall yellow, replenishing a house's water-supply ([Özaltun](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md) places the painting example in §§28–29). Whether the paint goes on, or the water arrives, seems something one could only learn by observation.

Anscombe's response at this stage (p. 50): the topic of an intention may be something known, or merely believed — on the basis of observation, hearsay, superstition, or nothing at all ([Small](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md)). If one knows or believes that doing ABC brings about Z, one can do Z in doing ABC; and if the belief is correct, doing Z is intentional and one does not know by observation that one is doing Z ([Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md); Small). So she concedes that background knowledge of the circumstances may be observational, without conceding that the knowledge of the action is (Pellegrini, reporting Schwenkler). [Setiya SEP](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) reads p. 50 as excluding inference as well as observation, while noting that such knowledge often rests partly on observation and is claimed only to go beyond what observation and inference support.

The section sets up the puzzle of §29: if what is known without observation is the worldly happening, and the same happening can be known by observation, are there two objects of knowledge or one?

## Main claim(s)
- An agent can say what he is doing, under its intentional descriptions, without observing (§28, contents line).
- Doing Z intentionally by doing ABC requires only knowledge or correct belief that ABC brings about Z; when the belief is correct, Z is done intentionally and not known by observation (§28, p. 50, per Small and Setiya).
- Knowledge of one's action is not of the same kind as knowledge of limb position (Setiya 2008's reading of pp. 49–50).

## Examples
- Saying what I am doing without looking (contents line).
- Painting a wall yellow ([Özaltun](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md), §§28–29).
- Doing Z by doing ABC (schematic, p. 50).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- That only one's bodily movements, or something nearer still, can be known without observation, the rest being observed or inferred (raised as the difficulty).
- That knowledge of action is like knowledge of limb position (comparison qualified).

## In the secondary literature
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) — identifies a tension: §28 (p. 50) suggests true belief about means suffices for intentional action, whereas §48 (p. 89) says intentional action presupposes "knowing one's way about"; resolves it in favour of §48, which comes after practical knowledge and practical reasoning are introduced.
- [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md) — p. 50 is his anchor passage; reads Anscombe as making practical knowledge of doing A a condition of doing A intentionally.
- [Setiya 2008](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2008-practical-knowledge.md) — finds the limb-position comparison unhelpful; the key epistemic claim is that such knowledge is non-inferential.
- [Setiya SEP 2022](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) — "knowledge without observation" excludes inference; partial reliance on observation conceded.
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — Ch. 4.1 "Raising difficulties" (§28); connects to Wittgenstein's *Blue Book* (knowing oneself "as subject") and "The First Person" (OUP abstract).
- [Campbell 2024](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2024-practical-knowledge-structural-challenge.md) — §§28–30 as Anscombe's explicit statement that knowledge "in intention" is of what one is *actually* doing (existential).
- [Ford–Hornsby–Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md) — Haddock (practical knowledge as a "simple consequence of the actuality of its objects", unlike limb-position knowledge), Thompson (progressive), Setiya, Rödl treat §§28–32 (via reviews).
- [Haddock & Wiseman 2022](../../sources/secondary/haddock-wiseman-2022-anscombean-mind.md) — McDowell (practical knowledge must be aided by receptive knowledge; Anscombe not committed to knowledge of all intentional action) vs. Özaltun (she is committed, and rightly).
- [Teichmann (ed.) 2022](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2022-oxford-handbook-anscombe.md) — H. Teichman traces knowledge without observation from Wittgenstein (limb position) to intention (§§8, 28).
- [Schwenkler c2020 (3:16)](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-c2020-316-interview.md) — in the normal case practical thinking is practical knowledge, but intentional action without knowledge is possible (cites §28/§48 region).
- [Wiland & Driver 2022](../../sources/secondary/wiland-driver-2022-sep-anscombe.md) — knowledge of what one is doing without observing oneself (their example: knowing you are reading the entry).
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md); [Campbell 2022](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2022-practical-knowledge-practical-truth.md) — non-observation is not the defining mark of practical knowledge, since some non-observational knowledge (mental causes, limb position) is not practical.
