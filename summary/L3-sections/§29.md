# §29 — Two objects of knowledge? "I do what happens"

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part IV: Knowledge Without Observation](../L1-parts.md#part-iv-knowledge-without-observation) › [G09: §§28–30](../L2-sections/G09-s28-30-knowledge-of-ones-own-action.md) › **§29** · [← §28](§28.md) · [§30 →](§30.md)

*Pages: from p. 51 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: must there not be two objects of knowledge — what I am "doing" (my intention) and what actually takes place, which only observation can give? Philosophical views of will and intention that arise from this problem.*

## In one sentence
Faced with the thought that two ways of knowing require two things known — an inner "doing" known without observation and an outer happening known by observation — Anscombe rejects the retreat inwards and insists that what I do is what happens.

## Summary
§28 left a puzzle: what one is doing (replenishing the water-supply) seems to be knowable by observation, and is sometimes known only that way, yet the agent knows it without observation. The natural inference is that there must be two objects of knowledge: what I am "doing" in the sense of my intention or will, known without observation, and what actually takes place, known only by observation (contents line). [Holton](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) and [Speaks](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) report Anscombe's formulation: if there are two ways of knowing, there must be two different things known. [Hubbs](../../sources/secondary/hubbs-2016-intentions-and-commands.md) frames the middle of the book around this question of two knowledges of one thing.

Anscombe surveys the philosophical views of will that this problem generates (contents line):
- **Acts of will contingently related to the world.** The will as something non-physical, its relation to what happens contingent — a view [Speaks](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) associates with the early Wittgenstein.
- **The "mad" account / "false avenue of escape".** That whatever I think I am doing is what I really "do", or that knowledge of what happens is inferred from knowledge of one's will ([Setiya 2008](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2008-practical-knowledge.md) reports "a mad account", p. 52; [Setiya SEP](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) reports "false avenue of escape", p. 52). [Frey](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) reads the "mad account" as the epistemically modest alternative that restricts the object of knowledge.

Against these she sets the formula "I do what happens" (p. 52). The agent does not stand to his body as an observer asking what it is bringing about and recognising the opening of a window ([Campbell 2024](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2024-practical-knowledge-structural-challenge.md), p. 51). Observation may be an aid, as the eyes are in writing; but someone writing with eyes shut still knows what he is writing ([Holton](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md); [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md), p. 53). The problem is thus sharpened rather than solved: Anscombe refuses to make the object of non-observational knowledge something inner, but has not yet said how one happening can be known in two ways. The answer is deferred to §§31–32 and, ultimately, to practical knowledge (§§45, 48).

## Main claim(s)
- The inference from two ways of knowing to two objects known is the root of mistaken theories of the will (§29, contents line; Holton).
- What the agent knows without observation is what happens, not an inner act of will ("I do what happens", p. 52).
- Making whatever I think I do into what I "really" do is a mad/false way out (p. 52).

## Examples
- An agent asking what his body is bringing about and recognising the opening of a window — the absurd observer's stance ([Campbell 2024](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2024-practical-knowledge-structural-challenge.md)).
- Writing with eyes shut; eyes as a mere aid ([Holton](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md); [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md), which places it at p. 53, §§29–30).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Two objects: inner "doing" vs. outer happening.
- Non-physical acts of will contingently related to events (cf. early Wittgenstein, per Speaks).
- Inference from knowledge of one's will (Grice-style, per Setiya 2008).
- Whatever I think I do is what I do.

## In the secondary literature
- [Hubbs 2016](../../sources/secondary/hubbs-2016-intentions-and-commands.md) — the "two-knowledges/one-object" puzzle frames §§29–48; obstacle is modern philosophy's contemplative conception of knowledge (§32); resolution via §48. Following Thompson, in the good case agent and observer know one and the same fact.
- [Özaltun 2016](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md) — "I do what happens" and non-observational knowledge of what happens are "two faces of the same coin"; §§33–44 are needed before §45 can answer §29.
- [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md) — "I do what happens" and the protest against pushing what is known "back and back" count against restricting practical knowledge to the inner or the progressive.
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — object of practical knowledge is the observable material event; argues against the "argument from practical fallibility" (attributed to Paul).
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) — §§29–30 argue "strikingly" that the object is not one's intention/trying nor one's bodily movement.
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — Ch. 4.2 "False avenues of escape" (§§29–30): knowing what I will; knowing how I move; doing what I think (OUP headings).
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — the "two-factor" picture is attractive partly because non-observational knowledge seems not to be of external fact; adds a fourth option Anscombe does not consider (brain states).
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — raises Ian Waterman (loss of proprioception) and Shoemaker's self-blindness as pressure on §8's criterion; suggests the answer lies in practical knowledge (§32 ff.).

## Notes and uncertainties
- The attribution of the contingent-will view to the early Wittgenstein (*Tractatus*) is Speaks's; the sources do not record whether Anscombe names him in §29. Whether Anscombe names the *Tractatus* is not asserted here.
