# §33 — Aristotle's practical syllogism: a different form of reasoning, not a proof of "ought"

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part V: Practical Reasoning](../L1-parts.md#part-v-practical-reasoning) › [G11: §§33–35](../L2-sections/G11-s33-35-practical-syllogism.md) › **§33** · [← §32](§32.md) · [§34 →](§34.md)

*Pages: from p. 57 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination); the section runs to p. 62. Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: the "practical knowledge" raised in §32 can be understood only once we understand Aristotle's notion of practical reasoning. The practical syllogism does not demonstrate what I ought to do. It is a kind of reasoning different from the proof syllogism, and modern philosophy has misunderstood it.*

## In one sentence
To understand practical knowledge one must first understand practical reasoning. Aristotle's practical syllogism is not a proof that some action ought to be done. It differs in *form* from theoretical reasoning: its conclusion is an action whose point the premises show.

## Summary
§32 asked whether there is "practical knowledge" in the ancient and medieval sense. §33 opens by saying that this can be understood only through Aristotle's account of practical reasoning (p. 57; Frey, Hubbs, Small, Wiland & Driver all cite this as the hinge). Anscombe then diagnoses a common misreading. On that reading the practical syllogism is ordinary reasoning to the truth of a conclusion such as "I ought to do such-and-such". She uses an example about a beggar (1963 text; Sandis, Frey) and a parody, the "mince pie syllogism" (Elliott, Frey). The parody shows that giving reasoning a practical *subject matter* does not make it practical reasoning.

On the logic, she contrasts the theoretical syllogism, whose premises necessitate its conclusion, with Aristotle's practical examples. Their universal premise, if read as a premise of action, would be "insane" (pp. 61–62; Campbell 2022). For example, it would have the agent eat every bit of dry food he comes across. The dry-food case is Aristotle's (*NE* VII.3). Anscombe adds her own imitation of one of his "classroom" examples, about vitamin X and pigs' tripes (Sandis). Read that way, the practical premises do not entail the action, yet the reasoning is not defective on that account. It is calculation of what to do toward something wanted (§§34–35). She also contrasts an imperative case: someone who professes to accept an order and a factual premise must accept the conclusion, just as someone who believes the premises of a theoretical syllogism must (Sandis, on the 1958 wording). [Schwenkler](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) reads the discussion from §33 on as directed against R. M. Hare ([3:16 interview](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-c2020-316-interview.md): Hare's *Language of Morals*). †That the imperative case is specifically aimed at treating practical inference as a logic of imperatives is the writer's gloss.

The 1963 edition adds the section's most cited material (Sandis). Anscombe says the "cause of the mischief" was partly Aristotle's own division of reasoning by subject matter, demonstrative reasoning being about what cannot be otherwise. She then gives an argument about John driving from Chartres to Paris. It concerns something variable yet is theoretical reasoning. So the difference is a "difference of form" between reasoning that leads to action and reasoning to the truth of a conclusion (p. 60; Setiya 2022). She distinguishes three cases:
- theoretical syllogism;
- the "idle" practical syllogism, a mere classroom example;
- the practical syllogism proper, whose conclusion is an action and whose premises are "on active service" (pp. 59–60; Sandis, Frey, Campbell 2024, Small).

## Main claim(s)
- Practical knowledge (§32) presupposes an understanding of practical reasoning (§33, contents line).
- The practical syllogism is not a demonstration of what one ought to do (contents line).
- Practical and theoretical reasoning differ in form, not in subject matter. The conclusion of practical reasoning proper is an action (1963 text; Sandis, Setiya 2022, Schwenkler via Pellegrini).
- Universal practical premises taken as premises of action are absurd ("insane"), and the premises do not necessitate the action (pp. 61–62; Campbell 2022, Sandis).

## Examples
- The beggar syllogism (1963 opening; Sandis, Frey).
- The "mince pie syllogism" parody (p. 58; Elliott, Frey).
- Aristotle's dry-food syllogism and the "insanity" of a universal premise to eat all dry food (p. 61; Sandis, Campbell 2022).
- Vitamin X and pigs' tripes, Anscombe's imitation of an Aristotelian classroom example (p. 60; Sandis).
- John driving from Chartres to Paris: theoretical reasoning about something variable (1963 addition; Sandis).
- An imperative (order) syllogism (Sandis).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The "ethical syllogism" reading, which concludes "I ought to do this" (Sandis, Frey).
- The view that what marks reasoning as practical is its subject matter, i.e. variable or contingent things. Aristotle is partly to blame for this (1963 text; Sandis).
- The view that Aristotle's denial that practical reasoning is "apodeictic" marks a defect. This is the 1957/58 framing, softened in 1963 (Sandis).

## In the secondary literature
- [Sandis 2015](../../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md) — the key textual source. §33 is the main locus of revision in 1958 and 1963:
  - a dropped footnote on entailment;
  - the "professing to accept" sentence;
  - the "as practical premises" and negative-premises addition;
  - the rewritten opening;
  - "not exactly" changed to "not entirely his fault";
  - the new Chartres–Paris passage and the idle/proper distinction;
  - "classroom example".

  Because of the insertion, page numbers from p. 59 differ between editions.
- [Elliott 2016](../../sources/secondary/elliott-2016-anscombe-practical-truth.md) — "special sort of reasoning" (p. 58), mince pie parody, "difference in form", and an aim of "leading to action" (p. 60). Elliott reads the account as instrumentalist (with Müller and Vogler).
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — Ch. 5.1 "A difference in form" (§33) and 5.2 "Calculation" (§§33–35). The premises are not meant to *prove* the conclusion, and the conclusion is an action (pp. 117, 120, via Pellegrini). Anscombe is compared with R. M. Hare. [Schwenkler 3:16](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-c2020-316-interview.md): the practical-syllogism discussion is centred on criticism of Hare's *Language of Morals*.
- [Setiya 2022](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) — "difference of form" (p. 60). Intention is justified by practical, not theoretical, reasoning, and this is where Anscombe differs most sharply from Velleman's cognitivism.
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — practical knowledge is understood only via practical reasoning (p. 57). Frey notes the beggar and mince pie syllogisms (p. 58) and the premises "on active service" (p. 60).
- [Campbell 2022](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2022-practical-knowledge-practical-truth.md) — practical reasoning is an abstract order, not a prior mental process. Universal major premises are "insane" (pp. 61–62). [Campbell 2024](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2024-practical-knowledge-structural-challenge.md) — idle vs proper practical syllogism. The conclusion is an action whose point the premises show.
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) — means-knowledge figures in genuine, as opposed to idle or classroom, practical reasoning only if practicable; Small uses this to argue for basic know-how. In [Haddock & Wiseman 2022](../../sources/secondary/haddock-wiseman-2022-anscombean-mind.md), Small argues that §33 was right about a "difference of form", against Anscombe's later "Practical Inference", which calls the question a matter of indifference. Newton (same volume) argues that Anscombe's practical syllogisms can be valid without being sound and do not compel action, so she is a "pre-critical" sceptic about Kantian practical knowledge. Contrast Rödl's Kantian reconciliation in [Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md).
- [Hubbs 2016](../../sources/secondary/hubbs-2016-intentions-and-commands.md) — practical reasoning concludes in action, or in an intention if the circumstances are not yet right. Hubbs reads the turn at §33 through the intention–command analogy.
- [Wiland & Driver 2022](../../sources/secondary/wiland-driver-2022-sep-anscombe.md); [Piñeros Glasscock & Tenenbaum 2023](../../sources/secondary/pineros-glasscock-tenenbaum-2023-sep-action.md) — both cite §33 (INT 57) for the dependence of practical knowledge on practical reasoning.
- [Schlosser 2019](../../sources/secondary/schlosser-2019-sep-agency.md) — attributes to Anscombe the view that all intentional action is rationalisable by a sound practical syllogism. This conflicts with §17 and §35 and should be treated as a simplification.

## Notes and uncertainties
