# §34 — Practical reasoning need not mimic the proof syllogism; it starts from something wanted

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part V: Practical Reasoning](../L1-parts.md#part-v-practical-reasoning) › [G11: §§33–35](../L2-sections/G11-s33-35-practical-syllogism.md) › **§34** · [← §33](§33.md) · [§35 →](§35.md)

*Pages: from p. 62 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination); p. 61 in the 1957/58 printings. Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: the label "practical syllogism" should not be kept for reasonings that look like proof syllogisms. What practical reasoning starts from is something wanted, and its first premise mentions such a thing.*

## In one sentence
Once the practical syllogism is no longer modelled on proof, nothing limits "practical reasoning" to arguments with a universal major premise. What defines it is that it starts from something wanted and works out how to get it.

## Summary
§33 argued that the practical syllogism is not a demonstration and differs from theoretical reasoning in form. §34 draws a consequence for its *shape*. Aristotle's examples look like proof syllogisms, with a universal premise ("dry food suits any man") brought down to a particular by a minor premise. But §33 showed that such universal premises, taken as premises of action, are absurd. So there is no reason to restrict the term "practical reasoning" to arguments with that look (contents line). In 1963 Anscombe turned this point from a flat assertion into a rhetorical question (Sandis).

What replaces the proof-like shape is a criterion about the *starting point*. Practical reasoning begins from something the agent wants, and its first premise mentions what is wanted (contents line). The later steps work out what can be done toward getting it, until something is reached that the agent can do. This is the "calculation" that Schwenkler groups across §§33–35. †Reasoning of this kind can have a chain of particular means-premises with no universal premise at all. Its validity as practical reasoning lies in each step's being a way of getting what is wanted, not in an entailment from premises to the action.

§34 thus prepares two things:
- §35's clarification that "I want" is not itself a premise, even though the first premise must mention something wanted;
- §36's inquiry into what "wanting" must be for this account to work.

## Main claim(s)
- Practical syllogisms are not limited to those that look like proof syllogisms (§34, contents line).
- The starting point of practical reasoning is something wanted, and its first premise mentions it (§34, contents line).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The assumption, inherited from reading Aristotle's examples as quasi-demonstrations, that the universal-premise form is essential to practical reasoning (contents line; Sandis on the softened wording).

## In the secondary literature
- [Sandis 2015](../../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md) — the 1963 edition recasts the section's opening categorical claim as a rhetorical question, a softening of tone. The section starts on p. 61 in 1957/58 and p. 62 in 1963.
- [Campbell 2022](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2022-practical-knowledge-practical-truth.md) — cites pp. 61–62 (§§33–34) for the claim that universally quantified major premises are generally "insane".
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — Ch. 5.2 "Calculation" (§§33–35) presents the Aristotelian account of practical reasoning as calculating means to an end (headings and abstract only).
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — cites §34, with §23, for the "break" in the means–end nesting past which further hopes, such as the poisoner's utopia, are not part of the action. The section-level basis for this citation is not given in the saved notes.
- [Müller in Ford, Hornsby & Stoutland 2011](../../sources/secondary/ford-hornsby-stoutland-2011-essays.md) — objects that modelling practical reasoning on a syllogism from something wanted (§§33–41) wrongly excludes backward-looking reasons (§§13–14). Piedrahita, in the same file, replies that *Intention* never meant the syllogism to cover all practical rationality.
