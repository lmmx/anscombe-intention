# §36 — What "wanting" means here: trying to get, and the limits on what can be wanted

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part V: Practical Reasoning](../L1-parts.md#part-v-practical-reasoning) › [G12: §§36–40](../L2-sections/G12-s36-40-wanting-and-desirability.md) › **§36** · [← §35](§35.md) · [§37 →](§37.md)

*Pages: from p. 67 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: in the sense of "wanting" that matters here, the X in "A wants X" cannot be just any describable object or state of affairs. Volition and sense-knowledge cannot be described independently of each other. She raises two puzzles: wanting a wife, and wanting something the agent does not suppose to exist yet.*

## In one sentence
The wanting that sets practical reasoning going is the kind whose "primitive sign" is trying to get. Because of this, what can be wanted in this sense is restricted. It cannot be described apart from what the agent knows or perceives, which raises puzzles about wanting things that are not yet there to be had.

## Summary
§§34–35 made "something wanted" the starting point of practical reasoning. §36 asks which sense of "wanting" is meant. Anscombe identifies it by its primitive manifestation: "the primitive sign of wanting is trying to get" (p. 68; Setiya 2022). The feeling of desire and idle wishing are set aside as not the relevant notion.† Wanting in this sense is shown in movement toward a thing, and so belongs to animals as well as to language-users.†

Two consequences follow, per the contents line.

1. **What can be wanted is limited.** In "A wants X", X cannot range over every describable object or state of affairs. †One can wish for anything, including the impossible or the past. But one can *want* in this sense only what one could try to get, so the object must be represented as something attainable by acting.
2. **Volition and knowledge are interdependent.** Volition and sense-knowledge cannot be described independently. What an agent is trying to get has to be specified through what he perceives or knows of it, and what he perceives is described partly in terms of what he goes after.† Kunz's thesis (abstract) likewise reports that, for Anscombe, the relevant "strong" wanting requires knowledge of the existence of the thing and movement toward it.

The section ends with two problems this raises (contents line):
- **Wanting a wife.** Someone can want a wife without there being any particular woman whom he wants. †What he tries to get is specified only generally.
- **Wanting what does not yet exist.** An agent may want something he does not suppose to exist yet, e.g. a thing to be made. That seems to strain the link between wanting and knowledge of an existing object.

†Anscombe's handling suggests that what is wanted is given under a description, as an intentional object, rather than as an existing particular. This parallels the account of action "under a description" (§§6, 23–26).

## Main claim(s)
- The relevant wanting is the kind whose primitive sign is trying to get (p. 68; Setiya 2022).
- In this sense not every describable object or state can be wanted (§36, contents line).
- Volition and sense-knowledge cannot be described independently of each other (§36, contents line).

## Examples
- Wanting a wife (contents line).
- Wanting something the agent does not suppose to exist yet (contents line).
- †Animal trying-to-get as the primitive case. This is the writer's recollection; check the text.

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Identifying wanting with a feeling (a "pang") or with idle wishing.†
- The puzzles of the wife and of the not-yet-existing object, which threaten the requirement that wanting be tied to knowledge of its object (contents line).

## In the secondary literature
- [Setiya 2022](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) — it is now consensus that intending entails wanting in the motivational sense whose "primitive sign … is trying to get" (p. 68).
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — 5.3 "The role of 'wanting'" (§§35–36). The abstract describes the chapter as treating the role of desire in practical reasoning (headings only).
- [Campbell 2024](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2024-practical-knowledge-structural-challenge.md) — cites §36, with §28, for Anscombe on the role of inference and perception in practical reasoning.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — lists §36 among the sections his structural reading covers. No separate claim about it is recorded.
- Kunz (2013, see Notes) — reads Anscombe as distinguishing weak from strong wanting, the latter requiring knowledge of the thing's existence and movement toward it. Kunz judges the view "neither thoroughly developed nor adequately supported" (abstract only).
