# §37 — "What for?" until a desirability characterisation: the saucer of mud

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part V: Practical Reasoning](../L1-parts.md#part-v-practical-reasoning) › [G12: §§36–40](../L2-sections/G12-s36-40-wanting-and-desirability.md) › **§37** · [← §36](§36.md) · [§38 →](§38.md)

*Pages: from p. 70 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: whenever someone wants something, it can always be asked what he wants it for, or in what respect it is desirable, until he gives a desirability characterisation.*

## In one sentence
To want something, in the sense relevant to action, is to want it as somehow desirable. A bare claim to want something with nothing to say about what it is for, or what is good about it, is unintelligible. The man who just wants a saucer of mud shows this.

## Summary
§36 tied wanting to trying to get. §37 adds a further, intelligibility condition. Faced with someone who wants something, one can always ask "What for?" or "In what way is it desirable?". The questioning goes on until a *desirability characterisation* is reached: a description under which the thing is seen as desirable (contents line). The vivid case is a man who says he wants a saucer of mud: he is likely to be asked what for (p. 70; Elliott).

Anscombe anticipates a philosophical reply: philosophers have taught that anything whatever can be an object of desire, so the objects need no desirability characterisation, and it "merely so happens" that he wants them. She calls this "fair nonsense" (p. 71; Elliott; quoted by [Platts 1985](../../sources/secondary/platts-1985-object-of-desire.md)). The point is not that eccentric desires are impossible. It is that a want becomes intelligible as a want only once a desirability characterisation has been "reached and made intelligible" (p. 70; quoted by Platts).

[Gosling's 1969](../../sources/secondary/gosling-1969-desirability-characterisations.md) exposition distinguishes two strengths in the text's "stopping" claim:
- a weaker claim that once a desirability characterisation is given, no further "What for?" is *required* (p. 71, para. 2);
- a stronger claim that no further such question is *in order* (pp. 66, 69, 75).

§38 takes up this point.

## Main claim(s)
- Wanting (in the relevant sense) can always be met with "What for?" or "In what respect desirable?" until a desirability characterisation is reached (§37, contents line).
- A want with no desirability characterisation, e.g. a saucer of mud wanted for nothing, is unintelligible. The claim that anything can be an object of desire is "fair nonsense" (pp. 70–71; Elliott, Platts).

## Examples
- The man who wants a saucer of mud (p. 70; Elliott; Platts).
- *Not Anscombe's:* Richter's "getting all one's green books onto the roof" is his own illustration of the same point.

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The empiricist thesis that any object whatever can be desired, so that a desire needs no characterisation of its object as desirable (pp. 70–71; Elliott, Platts).

## In the secondary literature
- [Elliott 2016](../../sources/secondary/elliott-2016-anscombe-practical-truth.md) — saucer of mud (p. 70), "fair nonsense" (p. 71). Wanting requires seeing the thing "under the aspect of some good".
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — §§37–41 form 5.4 "The guise of the good". Desirability is a "formal restriction" on wanting (p. 140, via Pellegrini).
- [Millgram & Bowman 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-bowman-2026-sep-practical-reason-structure-of-actions.md) — the outermost description in the "matryoshka" structure of an action needs a point, a desirability characterisation, to be intelligible as action at all. They say Anscombe left this inquiry incomplete.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — desirability characterisations stop the "what for?" regress in one way (§37) but allow other questions ("thrilling how?"). His own thesis is that knowing what one is doing is the desirability characterisation of intentional action as such.
- [Richter n.d.](../../sources/secondary/richter-nd-iep-anscombe.md) — intelligibility relative to a limited range of goods (cf. §§37–40). His green-books example is not Anscombe's.
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — the first premise mentions something wanted (§§35–37). Frey takes the knowledge requirement and the guise-of-the-good requirement to be inseparable.
- [Gosling 1969](../../sources/secondary/gosling-1969-desirability-characterisations.md) — the most detailed early critique. Desirability characterisations are claimed to be necessary but not sufficient for wanting for a reason. He separates three interests a "What for?" question can express: the agent's actual reasoning, how far the action has been thought out, and the form of its attraction. He concludes that a clear stopping thesis holds only for the last.
- [Platts 1985](../../sources/secondary/platts-1985-object-of-desire.md) — links the desirability characterisation to Aquinas's "imagined good" (via Davidson). He argues that one can see something as "somehow desirable" before finding an adequate characterisation, so that this is not "fair nonsense". He treats the saucer of mud as a case of opaque content.
