# §38 — Where "What for?" stops; the Nazi example; logic indifferent to moral approval

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part V: Practical Reasoning](../L1-parts.md#part-v-practical-reasoning) › [G12: §§36–40](../L2-sections/G12-s36-40-wanting-and-desirability.md) › **§38** · [← §37](§37.md) · [§39 →](§39.md)

*Pages: from p. 72 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: once a desirability characterisation has been reached, the question "What for?" cannot be continued with any sense. The example given is "It befits a Nazi to spend his last hour exterminating Jews". This does not mean that practical reasoning is safe from criticism provided it commits no fallacy.*

## In one sentence
A desirability characterisation closes the "What for?" series, and the logic of practical reasoning is indifferent to whether we approve of its starting point. A Nazi's reasoning from what "befits a Nazi" has the same form as virtuous reasoning. That does not make such reasoning immune to criticism.

## Summary
§37 said "What for?" may be pressed until a desirability characterisation is reached. §38 adds that, once one is reached, the question cannot significantly be pressed further (contents line). [Gosling 1969](../../sources/secondary/gosling-1969-desirability-characterisations.md) calls this the "stopping property" of desirability characterisations.

Anscombe then chooses a deliberately repellent example. A first premise of the form "It befits a Nazi to spend his last hour exterminating Jews" (contents line) gives a desirability characterisation, namely what befits a Nazi. The example is used to display the logical structure of practical reasoning from such a characterisation ([Elliott](../../sources/secondary/elliott-2016-anscombe-practical-truth.md): she chooses examples like "what befits a Nazi" so that the logic is not obscured by the reader's moral approbation). The point of the choice is methodological. Moral approbation is irrelevant to the logical features of practical reasoning (Elliott, p. 72). Frey notes that the vicious example is chosen precisely to show that the practical syllogism is not a moral proof (p. 72). Millgram counts §§38–39 among the places where Anscombe disclaims moralising.

The section closes by blocking a misreading (contents line). Granting that a reasoning from a vicious desirability characterisation is formally in order, it does not follow that practical reasoning cannot be assailed so long as it is not fallacious. The sources consulted do not report how Anscombe says it may be assailed. §§39–40 take this further. §39 says no particular desirability characterisation is compulsory and that the question belongs to ethics. §40 says the good conceived must really be one of the forms of good.

## Main claim(s)
- After a desirability characterisation, "What for?" has no significant continuation (§38, contents line).
- The logical form of practical reasoning is independent of moral approval of its premises (Elliott on p. 72; Frey).
- Formal validity does not protect practical reasoning from criticism of its premises (§38, contents line).

## Examples
- "It befits a Nazi to spend his last hour exterminating Jews" (contents line; Elliott; Frey).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The thought that, if logic is indifferent to moral content, practical reasoning can be criticised only for invalidity (contents line).

## In the secondary literature
- [Elliott 2016](../../sources/secondary/elliott-2016-anscombe-practical-truth.md) — moral approbation is irrelevant to the logical features of practical reasoning, hence the deliberately non-approved examples such as what befits a Nazi (p. 72). Vogler's *Reasonably Vicious* (reported by Elliott) draws from this the conclusion that an instrumentalist cannot convict the clever vicious agent of an error in practical reasoning. Elliott resists this via Anscombe's later "practical truth".
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — the Nazi example (p. 72) shows the practical syllogism is not a moral proof.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — §§38–39 disclaim moralising. Millgram suspects ethics, including double effect, is nonetheless in the background of the book.
- [Millgram & Bowman 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-bowman-2026-sep-practical-reason-structure-of-actions.md) — cite §§37–38 for the requirement of a desirability characterisation of the outermost description. They note that Anscombe leaves incomplete the question whether a "point" differs from a reason.
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — part of 5.4 "The guise of the good" (§§37–41). The premises "show the goodness of the action one decides on" (p. 145, via Pellegrini).
- [Richter n.d.](../../sources/secondary/richter-nd-iep-anscombe.md) — treats §§37–38 implicitly. His illustrations are his own, not Anscombe's.
- [Gosling 1969](../../sources/secondary/gosling-1969-desirability-characterisations.md) — argues against the strong stopping claim. Health can be an intermediate characterisation for a sybarite who wants what is healthy *because* it is pleasant, so "because it is healthy" does not always close the series.
