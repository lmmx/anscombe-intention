# §39 — No desirability characterisation is compulsory: *bonum est multiplex*

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part V: Practical Reasoning](../L1-parts.md#part-v-practical-reasoning) › [G12: §§36–40](../L2-sections/G12-s36-40-wanting-and-desirability.md) › **§39** · [← §38](§38.md) · [§40 →](§40.md)

*Pages: from p. 74 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: the requirement that wanting have a desirability characterisation does not show that any characterisation is compelling, i.e. one that must be wanted. *Bonum est multiplex*: the good is manifold.*

## In one sentence
Every want must be under some aspect of good, but no particular aspect of good, whether health, pleasure or "what befits a man", is one that everyone must want. The good is many-sided, and whether some characterisation *ought* to move everyone is a question for ethics, not for the philosophy of action.

## Summary
§§37–38 established that wanting requires a desirability characterisation and that a characterisation stops the "What for?" series. §39 blocks an inference from this. It does not follow that any one desirability characterisation is "compulsive" in relation to wanting (contents line), i.e. that anyone who grasps that something has it must want it.

Anscombe tests the point against characterisations that seem strongest. For health and pleasure, "What's the good of it?" is not a sensible question (Elliott). Even so, someone can decline to be moved by them. She considers what a man ought to do, i.e. what befits a man, and asks whether one can coherently say that one has at this moment lost all interest in that (p. 74 n. 1; Elliott). She also discusses Milton's Satan, "Evil be thou my good", whose stance becomes intelligible only if the evil is wanted under some aspect of good, for instance "intact liberty" (p. 75; Elliott). This is the sense in which wanting is of things seen "under the aspect of some good" (p. 75; Elliott; Bond cites p. 74). Millgram notes her question "what is the good of its being bad?".

Hence *bonum est multiplex*: the desirability characterisations are many and do not reduce to one (contents line). Gosling (1969) calls this point iv: no one characterisation is necessary, and "X is a good thing to have" gives only the general form of such characterisations (p. 75). Whether one particular good, such as what befits a man, has a special claim is a question for ethics. Anscombe leaves open that someone who says "Evil be thou my good" may be "committing errors of thought" (p. 76; Elliott). In *Intention* she deliberately sets ethics aside here (Campbell 2022, citing §§39 and 41).

## Main claim(s)
- The requirement of *some* desirability characterisation does not make any *particular* one compulsory for wanting (§39, contents line; Elliott p. 76).
- The good is manifold (*bonum est multiplex*; contents line).
- Whether a particular good is binding on everyone belongs to ethics, which *Intention* does not undertake (Elliott; Campbell 2022).

## Examples
- Health and pleasure as characterisations past which "What's the good of it?" makes no sense (Elliott).
- "At this moment I lose all interest in what befits a man" (p. 74 n. 1; Elliott).
- "Evil be thou my good" (Milton's Satan), made intelligible via e.g. "intact liberty" (p. 75; Elliott).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The inference from "every want needs a desirability characterisation" to "some characterisation compels every want" (contents line).
- The possibility that a perverse avowal like Satan's is unintelligible, and the reply that it is intelligible under some aspect of good (Elliott).
