# §40 — Wanting is to good as judging is to true

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part V: Practical Reasoning](../L1-parts.md#part-v-practical-reasoning) › [G12: §§36–40](../L2-sections/G12-s36-40-wanting-and-desirability.md) › **§40** · [← §39](§39.md) · [§41 →](§41.md)

*Pages: from p. 76 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: the relation of "wanting" to "good" is compared with the relation of "judging" to "true".*

## In one sentence
Good is the formal object of wanting as truth is the formal object of judgement. That does not mean everything wanted is good, any more than everything judged is true. But it does mean that the good under which something is wanted must really be one of the forms of good.

## Summary
§39 left the good plural and refused to make any one desirability characterisation compulsory. §40 states the general structure through an analogy (contents line). Judging aims at truth: a judgement can be false, but what makes something a judgement is its answerability to truth. In the same way, wanting is answerable to good: what is wanted is wanted as good, though it may not be (Frey, p. 76; Teichmann via a reviewer, on "true" and "false" applied to wanting).

Anscombe draws a constraint from this. The good the agent conceives the thing to have "must really be one of the many forms of good" (p. 77; Elliott). †The analogy is not used to make wanting a kind of judging. It shows that a desirability characterisation is not an arbitrary label: it must pick out a genuine form of good, even when the thing does not in fact have it. In this way §40 connects the formal requirement of §§37–39 with the "fair nonsense" of the saucer of mud. One cannot call just anything desirable, though one can be wrong about whether something is.

Pleasure, a key desirability characterisation, raises a further question. Millgram cites §40 for "what is the pleasure of it?", i.e. the request for the respect in which something pleases. †Anscombe signals that the concept of pleasure is difficult and is not explained by treating pleasure as a feeling or impression.

## Main claim(s)
- Wanting stands to good as judging stands to true (§40, contents line; Frey p. 76).
- The good conceived must really be one of the many forms of good (p. 77; Elliott).

## Examples
- Pleasure as a desirability characterisation, with the follow-up "what is the pleasure of it?" (Millgram).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- †Reading the analogy as a reduction of wanting to evaluative belief, or as implying that the wanted is always good. The disanalogy is marked in the same way as for judging, where the judged is not always true.

## In the secondary literature
- [Elliott 2016](../../sources/secondary/elliott-2016-anscombe-practical-truth.md) — "must really be one of the many forms of good" (p. 77). Elliott reads Anscombe's later "practical truth" as adding an ethical dimension: vicious agents act under the description "doing well", which is false of their acts.
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — wanting/good ~ judgement/truth (p. 76). In the later "Practical Inference", the goodness of the end plays the role in practical reasoning that the truth of premises plays in theoretical reasoning.
- [Teichmann 2008](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2008-philosophy-of-anscombe.md) — endorses Anscombe's insistence that "true" and "false" can apply to wanting: wanting something as good when it is not involves a false judgement. This is reported second-hand via a reviewer and should be treated with caution.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — "what is the pleasure of it?" (§40). A desirability characterisation can still invite questions of another kind.
- [Campbell 2022](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2022-practical-knowledge-practical-truth.md) — on her thin vs ethical "practical truth", wanting may be in order at the action-theoretic level without being right in the ethical sense. This is relevant background, though §40 is not cited directly.

## Notes and uncertainties
- † = writer's recollection of the text, not corroborated by the contents line or a saved source; check against the book. It applies to the gloss on the analogy's limits and to the remarks on pleasure.
- Gosling 1969 (see [§37](§37.md) Notes) reads Anscombe's "good" as the formal object of wanting *for a reason* rather than of wanting as such. That fits §40's analogy but is his interpretation.
