# §43 — "The stove is burning" vs "he is paying his gas bill": the complexity of doing

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part V: Practical Reasoning](../L1-parts.md#part-v-practical-reasoning) › [G13: §§41–43](../L2-sections/G13-s41-43-the-order-of-practical-reasoning.md) › **§43** · [← §42](§42.md) · [§44 →](§44.md)

*Pages: from p. 80 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination); a short section, and §44 also begins on p. 80. Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: a contrast between "the stove is burning" and "the man is paying his gas bill" brings out the enormous apparent complexity of "doing" in the second case.*

## In one sentence
Human doings, unlike physical happenings, carry within their descriptions a huge apparent complexity. Paying a gas bill presupposes institutions, background facts and an order of means and ends. Yet the agent says straightforwardly, and knows, that this is what he is doing.

## Summary
§42 identified the order displayed by practical reasoning with the order in intentional action itself. §43 makes vivid what that identification implies, using a contrast (contents line):
- "The stove is burning" reports a physical process, describable without reference to anyone's purposes.
- "The man is paying his gas bill" reports a human doing. Its truth involves an enormous apparent complexity.

Aucouturier quotes the passage (see Notes). The man is described as paying his gas bill when all he is doing is handing two bits of paper to a girl. An enquirer might then say that the description of a human action is "enormously complicated" if one were to say what is really involved in it — and yet a child can give such a report. Aucouturier also reports Anscombe's borrowing of Wittgenstein's phrase "enormously complicated tacit conventions" (*Tractatus* 4.002) and her aim to reveal "the order that there is in this chaos" (§43). Millgram's full text paraphrases the same illustration (handing two pieces of paper to a clerk), and he takes §43 to observe that human activity is as intellectually unmanageable in its complexity as the natural world.

The contents line calls the complexity "apparent". The section points ahead to §§46–47. There Anscombe argues that "intentional" characterises a *form* of description of events, and that many descriptions of what humans do (e.g. paying, hiring, marrying) apply only in virtue of that form. Millgram groups §§43 and 46 together as the treatment of the complexity of human doings.

## Main claim(s)
- Descriptions of human doings like "paying the gas bill" have an enormous apparent complexity that physical descriptions like "the stove is burning" lack (§43, contents line).

## Examples
- The stove burning vs the man paying his gas bill (contents line).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The enquirer's thought that describing a human action is something enormously complicated, if one says what is really involved in it (§43, as quoted by Aucouturier).
