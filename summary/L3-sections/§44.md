# §44 — Conditional calculation followed by action: when is it practical reasoning?

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part VI: Practical Knowledge](../L1-parts.md#part-vi-practical-knowledge) › [G14: §§44–45](../L2-sections/G14-s44-45-practical-knowledge-and-failure.md) › **§44** · [← §43](§43.md) · [§45 →](§45.md)

*Pages: from p. 80 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: a person reasons in the pattern "if I do this, this will result; if that, that", and then acts — Anscombe sorts the cases in which this counts as practical reasoning from those in which it does not.*

## In one sentence
Closing the discussion of practical reasoning (§§33–43), §44 asks when a survey of options of the form "if I do X, Y; if Z, W", followed by action, is genuinely *practical* reasoning, and in doing so prepares the turn to "practical knowledge" in §45.

## Summary
The section takes a pattern of thought that looks like a natural candidate for practical reasoning: the agent lays out alternatives and their results ("if I do this, this will happen; if that, that") and then does one of the things considered (contents line, §44). Anscombe's question is whether the mere sequence *calculation → action* is enough to make the calculation practical. The contents line signals a division: some such cases are practical reasoning and some are not.

*Editorial reconstruction (not corroborated by any source for §44 itself):* the criterion that the surrounding sections supply is the one established in §33 and §§35–42: reasoning is practical when it is reasoning *towards* doing something wanted, so that its conclusion is an action (or decision) whose point the premises show, and when the considerations actually figure in the order of means and ends that the "Why?" question elicits (§42). A conditional calculation that simply records what would follow from various acts, after which the agent acts for some unrelated reason or arbitrarily, has only the form of theoretical knowledge of consequences; it is not thereby practical.

A further point, drawn on by commentators, is that a calculation of options need not single out one course of action — Millgram's full text quotes §44 calling options the "most common situation" for anyone pursuing an objective, and Özaltun cites the bottom of §44 for practical reason's not determining the choice uniquely: there may be several ways to the same end, and practical reasoning does not compel a unique choice as proof compels assent (see Notes and the commentators below). This links §44 to §45's builder, who must settle everything "in a right order" (§45) but is not dictated a single plan by his end.

§44 is short and transitional. Its function in the book's architecture is to round off the account of practical reasoning before Anscombe returns, in §45, to the question left open in §§29–32: what kind of knowledge the agent has of what he is doing (§33 had said that "practical knowledge" can be understood only after practical reasoning is).

## Main claim(s)
- Action following a calculation of conditional outcomes is not by that fact the conclusion of practical reasoning (§44, contents line).
- Whether it is depends on the case (contents line); the sources consulted do not report Anscombe's criterion.
- Options are the most common situation in pursuing an objective, and practical reasoning need not settle the choice uniquely (§44, per Millgram and Özaltun).

## Examples
- The schematic "if I do this, this will happen; if that, that" (contents line). The section's concrete illustrations are not reproduced in any source consulted.

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The tempting view that any reasoning about the consequences of one's options, followed by action, is practical reasoning (contents line).

## In the secondary literature
- [Özaltun 2016](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md) — cites §44 (n. 11) for the point that practical reason need not determine choice uniquely; reads §§33–44 as the necessary preparation for §45's answer to the §29 puzzle.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — groups §§44–45 under "options" and "right order": there are many incompatible ways of building activity into action toward one end, so practical reasons do not compel as facts compel belief (his "logical freedom" of action).
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — treats §§44–45 together with §48 under "The cause of what it understands" (Ch. 6.3), i.e. as part of the practical-knowledge discussion rather than the end of Part V (headings only; body not read).
- [Hubbs 2016](../../sources/secondary/hubbs-2016-intentions-and-commands.md) — reads §§33–44 as the detour through practical reasoning that is then brought back to knowledge in §45.
- *Editorial note:* the part boundary used in this repo (§44 opens Part VI) follows Schwenkler's chapter division; §44's content (per the contents line) still belongs to the practical-reasoning discussion, so it is best read as a hinge.
