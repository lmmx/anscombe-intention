# §45 — The unseen builder: practical knowledge and the problem of failure

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part VI: Practical Knowledge](../L1-parts.md#part-vi-practical-knowledge) › [G14: §§44–45](../L2-sections/G14-s44-45-practical-knowledge-and-failure.md) › **§45** · [← §44](§44.md) · [§46 →](§46.md)

*Pages: from p. 82 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: practical knowledge introduced as the knowledge of what is done possessed by someone who directs a project without seeing it; the difficulty — how can this be knowledge if his orders are not carried out?*

## In one sentence
Anscombe models "practical knowledge" on a man who directs a building purely by giving orders and yet can say what the house is like, and then confronts the objection that, since orders can be disobeyed and intentions can fail, such "knowledge" seems not to depend on what actually happens.

## Summary
**The builder (para. 1).** Imagine someone directing a project — the erection of a building — which he neither sees nor receives reports on, purely by issuing orders; his (evidently superhuman) imagination does the work perception would normally do. He differs from someone merely conceiving speculatively how the thing might be done: the director settles everything "in a right order", and if all goes well his knowledge of what is done is *practical knowledge* (§45; [Özaltun](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md); [Sandis](../../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md)). The case is built so that observation is excluded by stipulation; §48 later calls it "a very improbable one".

**What he knows (paras. 2–3).** Asked what this "knowledge of what is done" is, Anscombe answers that first and foremost he can say what the house is like (§45). The natural objection is that he knows only something conditional — what the house is like *if* his orders have been obeyed. Anscombe's parallel case is the agent's own writing: with eyes shut I write "I am a fool" on a blackboard, and I can say what I am writing without looking; this is not elliptical for a conditional (§45; cf. §29 on writing with eyes closed).

**The difficulty (para. 4).** But orders can be disobeyed and intentions fail to get executed. If the chalk had made no mark, my "knowledge" of what I wrote would, it seems, "have been the same" — which would make it, as Anscombe puts it, "a funny sort of knowledge", one apparently independent of what happens (§45). Her response invokes "Theophrastus' principle": in such a case the mistake is in the performance, not in the judgment (§45; first used in §2, again in §32). Whether this response concedes non-factive knowledge or rejects the premiss that the knowledge "would have been the same" is the central interpretive dispute about the section (see below).

**Coda (para. 5).** Anscombe considers the temptation to locate the object of willing in an "idea" (she mentions William James) and contrasts the special case where saying makes it so — giving something by saying "It's yours!", possible only because property is conventional — to show both the pull of the thought that intention determines its object by itself and its limits (§45; per [Özaltun](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md)).

## Main claim(s)
- Practical knowledge is knowledge of *what is done* — in the paradigm, of the house built or the words written — not of an inner state (§45; cf. §29 "I do what happens").
- The director's knowledge does not derive from observation; its non-observational character is built into the example (§45).
- When intention fails, the error is to be located in the performance, not in the judgment (§45, echoing §§2, 32).
- The section *raises* a problem — how this can be knowledge given the possibility of failure — that §48 takes up again (contents lines §§45, 48).

## Examples
- The director of a building project working by orders alone, without sight or reports (§45).
- Writing "I am a fool" on a blackboard with eyes shut; the chalk that fails to mark (§45).
- Giving by saying "It's yours!" (§45, per Özaltun).
- William James on the "idea" as object of the will (§45, per Özaltun).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- The director knows only a conditional ("if my orders have been obeyed") (§45).
- Since orders can be disobeyed and intentions fail, his knowledge would have been the same whatever happened, so it is not knowledge of what happens (§45).
- The "idea" view of the object of willing (§45).
- The speculative designer who merely conceives how a thing might be done, as opposed to the practical director (§45).

## In the secondary literature
The section is the locus classicus of the dispute over whether practical knowledge is factive and what its object is.
- [Özaltun 2016](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md) — paragraph-by-paragraph reading; **strong factive** view. Distinguishes Anscombe's voice from a Wittgensteinian interlocutor: "my knowledge would have been the same" is the interlocutor's unwarranted premiss and "a funny sort of knowledge" is ridicule of it. When performance fails there is no knowledge; Theophrastus (§§2, 32, 45) marks a second *source of mistake*, not knowledge independent of the facts. The builder example functions as a definition; the object is the public house. Against Haddock 2011 (who reads the "same knowledge" line as Anscombe's own) and against "modest" readings (Roessler, Velleman, Grice, Paul, O'Shaughnessy, Thompson, Moran).
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — also resists a non-factive reading, by a different route: Theophrastus locates *blame* for a falsehood (traced to §2), not knowledge of what is false; "would have been the same" is a modal claim about one state (his parody: "my wife would have been the same if I had not married her"); since the truthmaker of practical knowledge is causally downstream, the very same state could have been false belief. Combines this with his "open knowledge" reading of §31.
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) — by contrast takes §45 to *seem* to say one can have agential knowledge of writing "I am a fool" when one is not, and calls this "a mis-step" (citing Moran 2004, Haddock 2011, McDowell 2013).
- [Setiya 2022 SEP](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) — Anscombe "may seem to suggest" knowledge of what is not the case (p. 82), but it is unclear whether she accepts or merely entertains it; her epistemology of practical knowledge is "elusive".
- [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md) — uses §45's opening ("knowledge of what is done": a house built, words written) as evidence that practical knowledge is not confined to the progressive but reaches completed deeds.
- [Sandis 2015](../../sources/secondary/sandis-2015-klesis-anscombes-intentions.md) — reads "merely conceiving speculatively" as idle practical reasoning (cf. the 1963 idle/proper distinction in §33) and restricts "knowledge independent of what happens" to practical knowledge proper, linking it to "Practical Truth" (1993).
- [Hubbs 2016](../../sources/secondary/hubbs-2016-intentions-and-commands.md) — the builder knows practically because he has set execution-conditions; notes that §45 pairs disobedience with failure of intention while §32 contrasts them, and resolves this by perspective (commander vs recipient). Following Thompson and Haddock: the agent knows that execution-conditions are set and the process begun (imperfective reading).
- [Wiland & Driver 2022](../../sources/secondary/wiland-driver-2022-sep-anscombe.md) — the director grasps the whole means–end order, not atomic acts. Their gloss "one who practically knows doesn't misperform" is stronger than the text.
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — lists "mistake in performance, not judgement" (p. 82) as a mark of practical knowledge; failure is a failure of self-constitution, not of correspondence.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — "right order" as evidence of the logical freedom of action (with §44).
- [Haddock & Wiseman 2022](../../sources/secondary/haddock-wiseman-2022-anscombean-mind.md) — editors' summaries: McDowell (practical knowledge needs receptive aid; Anscombe not committed to knowledge of all intentional action) vs Özaltun's chapter (she is committed, and right).
- [Richter (IEP)](../../sources/secondary/richter-nd-iep-anscombe.md) — uses the blackboard case (not cited by §) for knowledge despite failure.
- **Source on Theophrastus:** Holton traces the dictum to the *Magna Moralia* (1189b), attributed by Anscombe to Theophrastus; [Piñeros Glasscock & Tenenbaum](../../sources/secondary/pineros-glasscock-tenenbaum-2023-sep-action.md) speak of Anscombe "borrowing" from the *Magna Moralia*, Sandis calls it "Theophrastus' point".
- **Mis-citation flag:** [Campbell 2024](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2024-practical-knowledge-structural-challenge.md) cites "the exercise of knowledge how" as §45; on Setiya's and Small's readings that passage is in §48 (p. 88).
