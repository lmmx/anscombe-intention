# §46 — Describing what is done already uses the concept of human action

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part VI: Practical Knowledge](../L1-parts.md#part-vi-practical-knowledge) › [G15: §§46–48](../L2-sections/G15-s46-48-form-of-description-and-practical-knowledge.md) › **§46** · [← §45](§45.md) · [§47 →](§47.md)

*Pages: from p. 83 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: a description such as "building a house" or "writing on the blackboard" already employs the concept of human action, a concept defined by means of the question "Why?".*

## In one sentence
The objects of practical knowledge in §45 — a house being built, a sentence being written — are picked out by descriptions that themselves presuppose the concept of human action, and that concept is the one marked out by the special question "Why?" (§5).

## Summary
§45 left a worry: practical knowledge was said to be knowledge of "what is done", yet what is done seemed to be a matter of fact that could come apart from the agent's knowledge. §46 begins to dissolve this by looking at the *descriptions* under which "what is done" is known. To say that a house is being built, or that someone is writing a sentence on a blackboard, is not to describe a physical process that could be fully specified without reference to agency and then to add something; these descriptions already employ the concept of human action (contents line, §46).

That concept, Anscombe reminds us, was defined by means of our question "Why?" in the sense isolated in §§5–18: intentional actions are those to which that question is given application (§5). So the descriptions of happenings that figure as the objects of practical knowledge are descriptions whose sense depends on the practice of asking and answering "Why?" (§46). † The section appears to tie this to the "enormous complexity" of a doing like paying the gas bill noted in §43: the complexity lies not in a hidden mass of physical events but in the kind of description involved.

§46 is thus a bridge: it generalises from the two examples of §45 to the thesis of §47, that "intentional" relates to a *form* of description of events, and prepares §48's claim that many descriptions of human doings are "formally descriptions of executed intentions".

## Main claim(s)
- Descriptions such as "building a house", "writing on the blackboard" employ the concept of human action (§46).
- That concept is defined via the special sense of "Why?" (§46, recalling §5).

## Examples
- Building a house; writing on a blackboard (§46, carried over from §45).
- † Paying a gas bill (from §43) as an instance of a doing whose description is complex in kind.

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- † Implicitly, the picture on which "what happens" is first described in agency-neutral terms and the agent's knowledge then has to be matched against it (the picture behind the §45 difficulty).
