# §47 — "Intentional" as a form of description of events; the two lists; animals

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part VI: Practical Knowledge](../L1-parts.md#part-vi-practical-knowledge) › [G15: §§46–48](../L2-sections/G15-s46-48-form-of-description-and-practical-knowledge.md) › **§47** · [← §46](§46.md) · [§48 →](§48.md)

*Pages: from p. 84 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: the term "intentional" has to do with a form of description of events; intention in animals.*

## In one sentence
Being intentional is not "an extra property" of certain events that a philosopher must try to isolate, but a matter of events' being described in a certain *form* — the form displayed by the inquiry into "Why?" — and a large stock of our ordinary descriptions of what people (and animals) do have this form built in.

## Summary
§47 states the conclusion towards which the "Why?" inquiry has been working: "intentional" has reference to "a form of description of events" (§47, p. 84). What is essential to this form is what our investigation of the question "Why?" has displayed (§§5–26): descriptions of what someone does that give "Why?" application, that can be extended by "in order to" and "because" (in the reason-giving sense), and that stand in the A–D order of means and ends (§§23, 26). Anscombe contrasts this with the view that intention is an extra property of intentional actions, distinct from the event and needing separate philosophical description (§47; cf. §19's denial of an "extra feature").

To show how deeply the form pervades our descriptions, she gives two lists (p. 85). The first contains descriptions that can apply *only* to intentional (or voluntary) doings — e.g. telephoning, calling, groping, crouching, greeting, signalling, paying, selling, buying, hiring, dismissing, sending for, marrying, contracting. The second contains descriptions that apply sometimes to intentional and sometimes to non-intentional happenings — e.g. intruding, offending, coming to possess, kicking, abandoning, dropping, holding, picking up, switching on or off, placing, arranging (lists as reported by [Holton](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md)). Many of the latter are descriptions that go beyond the vocabulary of physics and that we would not have if we did not, for instance, make switches; Anscombe calls certain of them "vital descriptions" (per Holton). The upshot: were there no such form of description, we would lack a huge range of concepts for describing events; intention is not superimposed on an independently describable stream of movement (§47).

The section ends with intention in animals (contents line). Because what makes a description intentional is its form and the applicability of "Why?"-style explanation, we can rightly ascribe intentions to animals on the basis of what they do, even though they cannot *express* intention and so lack the distinction between doing and expressing intention that language makes possible (§47; cf. [Marcus, via Haddock & Wiseman](../../sources/secondary/haddock-wiseman-2022-anscombean-mind.md); [Piñeros Glasscock & Tenenbaum](../../sources/secondary/pineros-glasscock-tenenbaum-2023-sep-action.md)).

## Main claim(s)
- "Intentional" relates to a *form of description of events*, not to an extra property of events (§47).
- The form is the one displayed by the "Why?" inquiry and the A–D order (§47 with §§5, 23, 26).
- Many ordinary act-concepts are intention-dependent: some essentially, some only on occasion (§47).
- Animals have intentions, though they cannot express them (§47).

## Examples
- First list (only intentional/voluntary): telephoning, greeting, paying, hiring, marrying, sending for, etc. (§47).
- Second list (sometimes intentional, sometimes not): offending, kicking, dropping, switching on, placing, etc. (§47).
- Animal intention without expression of intention (contents line; Marcus). Anscombe's well-known example of a cat stalking a bird, whose movements are "hardly to be called an expression of intention", is in §2 (p. 5), not §47 (quoted by Moran & Stone; see Notes). Aucouturier refers to "§47 on the 'stalking cat'"; whether §47 returns to that example is unverified.

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- Intention as an "extra property" of certain events which philosophy must describe (§47).
- Commentators' puzzle rather than a reported move of the text: if intentional action is tied to descriptions the agent can give, it seems restricted to language-users, yet Anscombe says animals have intentions (Marcus, in [Haddock & Wiseman 2022](../../sources/secondary/haddock-wiseman-2022-anscombean-mind.md)). Moran & Stone note that §47 explains intention "in terms of language" (p. 86).

## Cross-references within *Intention*
- §§2–3 — expression of intention for the future (which animals, lacking language, cannot give).
- §5 — definition via "Why?".
- §19 — no "extra feature" makes an action intentional.
- §§23, 26 — A–D order: the structure of the form.
- §46 — descriptions of what is done employ the concept of human action.
- §48 — descriptions that are "formally descriptions of executed intentions" (first list) and the account of practical knowledge.

## In the secondary literature
- [Schwenkler c.2020 (3:16)](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-c2020-316-interview.md) — §47's phrase is the closest Anscombe comes to saying what intention *is*; the concept is brought to bear whenever we describe what a person or animal does so as to give "Why?" application. Opposes the modern default (intention a mental state causing action in the right way).
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — reports both lists; their purpose is to stop treating "intentional" as an extra property of a natural class of events; parallels Anscombe's later claim ("Causality and Determination", 1971) that the general word "cause" presupposes many special causal verbs.
- [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md) — the two columns underwrite condition (a) of §48; descriptions answering "Why?" are formally of executed intentions, which "follows from" §47. Also cites "sliding on ice" as practical knowledge that is not causal.
- [Setiya 2022 SEP](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) — §47 "apparently" holds that some act-types (paying, hiring, marrying) are essentially intentional, which raises a circularity worry for mental-state accounts.
- [Wiland & Driver 2022](../../sources/secondary/wiland-driver-2022-sep-anscombe.md) — the order of intention is built into how we represent our lives "from the very start"; treat §47 as the book's conclusion.
- [Wiseman 2025](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2025-philosophy-talk.md) — informal discussion of intention-laden descriptions (telephoning, marrying, kicking) in a "grammar of the concept" reading.
- [Frey 2016](../../sources/secondary/frey-2016-action-practical-knowledge-good.md) — knowing what one does is grasping the action's practical form (her intended §47 reference is marked "{need citation}" in the draft).
- [Campbell 2022](../../sources/secondary/campbell-2022-practical-knowledge-practical-truth.md) — relevant via the formal marks of §48. **Mis-citation flag:** she cites "I, 5" for animal intention; the contents line places intention in animals at §47.
- **Animals:** Marcus (in [Haddock & Wiseman 2022](../../sources/secondary/haddock-wiseman-2022-anscombean-mind.md)) — puzzle of language-dependence; animals act intentionally with practical knowledge; rationality transforms what practical knowledge is. [Piñeros Glasscock & Tenenbaum](../../sources/secondary/pineros-glasscock-tenenbaum-2023-sep-action.md) — animals act intentionally but not in the language-dependent way allowing the action/expression distinction (not cited by §).
- Bierson & Schwenkler; Mac Cumhaill (in [Haddock & Wiseman 2022](../../sources/secondary/haddock-wiseman-2022-anscombean-mind.md)) — intention-dependent descriptions (§§46–48); perceptual reports and "Why?" (§47).
