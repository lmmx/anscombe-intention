# §49 — The voluntary, and how it differs from the intentional

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part VII: Voluntary Action and Intention for the Future](../L1-parts.md#part-vii-voluntary-action-and-intention-for-the-future) › [G16: §49](../L2-sections/G16-s49-voluntary-action.md) › **§49** · [← §48](§48.md) · [§50 →](§50.md)

*Pages: from p. 89 (1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: an account of "voluntary" action.*

## In one sentence
Having given an account of intentional action, Anscombe can now say what "voluntary" means — a notion she had to set aside in §7 because it could not be introduced without presupposing the very account in question — and she treats the voluntary as a wider class whose boundaries are fixed by reference to the intentional.

## Summary
In §7 the involuntary was noted as one ground for refusing the question "Why?", but Anscombe declined to use it, since the notion of the (in)voluntary could not be introduced at that stage without treating as solved the problem being investigated (contents line, §7). §49 discharges that debt: with the account of intentional action and practical knowledge in place (§§5–48), "voluntary" can be explained (contents line, §49).

Anscombe sets out the distinction between the voluntary and the intentional under four numbered headings, quoted in full by Narboux 2020 and in part by Aucouturier and McGinn (see Notes):
1. **Mere physical movements** to whose description "Why?" is applicable count as voluntary rather than intentional when (a) the agent's answer is of the "I was fiddling" kind (a casual movement, or even not knowing why), or (b) he does not consider the movements, though he could say what they are if he did (p. 89). Narboux links (a) to §17's idle actions and (b) to the end of §30 on acquired skill.
2. **Known side-effects.** A result of one's intentional action that one knew beforehand would accompany it, and could have prevented by giving the action up, is voluntary but not intentional: one rejects "Why?" for it (p. 89). From another point of view it can be called involuntary, when one much regrets it but feels "compelled" to go on with the action (p. 89). Narboux links this heading to the pumper who does not care about the poisoning (§25).
3. **Voluntary passivity.** What is not one's own doing at all can be voluntary when it happens to one's delight and one consents rather than protesting or resisting: e.g. being pushed out in a punt, or being pushed so as to slide down a bank into a party of people when one "didn't mind" (pp. 89–90).
4. **Every intentional action is also voluntary**, though, as at (2), from another point of view an intentional action one regrets "having" to do can be called involuntary; Anscombe notes that "reluctant" is the commoner word (p. 90).

On Narboux's reading, (1)–(2) show that not everything voluntary is intentional and (4) that everything intentional is voluntary, so "voluntary" applies more widely than "intentional", and (3) widens it further.

The section is brief and has received far less attention than §§45–48, but it has been read as pointing to something larger: that the voluntary — what an agent can be held to account for — has been the underlying concern of the inquiry (see Narboux below).

## Main claim(s)
- With intentional action explained, "voluntary" can now be accounted for (§49; cf. §7).
- The voluntary is wider than the intentional: some voluntary movements and known side-effects are not intentional, and every intentional action is voluntary (§49 (1), (2), (4); Narboux; McGinn).
- Things that are not one's own doing can be voluntary when one consents to them (§49 (3)).

## Examples
- A previously known concomitant result of one's intentional action, which one could have prevented by giving it up (§49 (2); cf. §25).
- Fiddling, a casual movement, movements one does not consider (§49 (1)).
- Being pushed out in a punt; being pushed sliding down a bank into a party of people (§49 (3)).
- Doing something one regrets "having" to do — "reluctant" (§49 (4)).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- No objection is reported by the sources consulted. Narboux warns against three misreadings of §49: that it marks out two kinds of unintentional voluntary action (Hyman); that it gives necessary as well as sufficient conditions for the voluntary; and that it circumscribes a class of voluntary events.

## In the secondary literature
- [Haddock & Wiseman 2022](../../sources/secondary/haddock-wiseman-2022-anscombean-mind.md) — editors' summaries:
  - **Narboux**, "The elusiveness of the voluntary": a new extended commentary on §49; voluntary action depends on intentional action, and the voluntary "has been the subject of Anscombe's inquiry all along"; but *Intention* does not complete the account, which would need the relation of voluntariness to knowledge and an ethical setting for intention vs foresight (arsonist vs surgeon cases).
  - **Aucouturier**: following Aquinas, "human action" (under the command of reason) is wider than intentional action and equivalent to a sense of voluntary action; this explains how carelessness, negligence and omission can be bad unintentional actions. (*Editorial note:* this is Aucouturier's Thomistic thesis, not a report of §49's text.)
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — places §49 at the start of a short "Concluding Discussion" (Ch. 7.1 "Intentional and voluntary"), i.e. as a coda rather than a key (headings and abstract only). *Editorial note:* this is a structural placement rather than an argued rejection of Narboux's view; the contrast is in emphasis.
- [Teichmann 2022](../../sources/secondary/teichmann-2022-oxford-handbook-anscombe.md) — Clot-Goudard: much of Anscombe's action theory answers what knowledge an agent must have to be accountable (Aristotle's voluntary); ignorance can excuse but can be culpable (bears on §49's theme, not cited by §).
- [Speaks 2009](../../sources/secondary/speaks-2009-guide-1-31.md) — on §7: voluntary/involuntary is too close to intentional to serve as a criterion without circularity (background to §49).
- Coverage elsewhere is sparse: [Holton](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md), [Wiseman 2025](../../sources/secondary/wiseman-2025-philosophy-talk.md) and [Small](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) say little or nothing on §49.
