# §52 — "I am not going to" with "I am going to": intention against belief

[Overview](../L0-overview.md) › [Part VII: Voluntary Action and Intention for the Future](../L1-parts.md#part-vii-voluntary-action-and-intention-for-the-future) › [G17: §§50–52](../L2-sections/G17-s50-52-intention-for-the-future-revisited.md) › **§52** · [← §51](§51.md)

*Pages: from p. 91 (to p. 94; 1963 2nd ed. / Harvard 2000 pagination). Anscombe's contents line, paraphrased: "I am not going to —" as an expression of intention and "I am going to —" as an expression of belief; cases in which the two might be said together.*

## In one sentence
The book closes by asking whether one can, without contradiction, express the intention not to do something while believing one will do it — and through the cases of the man on the precipice, the man facing torture and St Peter, it shows both how intention and prediction are related (they concern "the very same thing" that will or will not happen) and why they are not simply contradictories.

## Summary
**The puzzle.** If an expression of intention is a kind of prediction (§2) to which "Why?" applies (§50), then "I am not going to do X" (intention) and "I am going to do X" (belief/estimate) look like contradictory predictions. Can a person sincerely say both? (contents line).

**The cases.** Anscombe considers situations where they seem to occur together (per [Holton](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md)):
- a man hanging from a precipice who is sure he will let go and determined not to — when it happens, "his fingers let go", not he;
- a man certain he will break under torture yet resolved not to;
- St Peter, told he will deny Christ, who might have reasoned that since Christ said it, it is true, and yet said "I will not" — possible because he did not know *how* the prophecy would be fulfilled, so that he could later do intentionally what he had intended not to do without changing his mind.

**What intention and prediction share.** Anscombe insists that intention and prediction are about the same thing. If I say I am going for a walk and someone else knows that this "is not going to happen", it would be absurd to deny that what he knows will not happen is the very thing I said would happen (p. 92, per [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md) and [Small](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md)). The object of the expression of intention is a happening in the world, not a psychological fact about me.

**Why there is nonetheless no flat contradiction.** The first-person combination of resolve with a confident counter-prediction sounds self-contradictory, whereas the third-person counterpart ("he intends not to, but he will") does not — a structure parallel to Moore's paradox (per [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) and [Millgram](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md)). *Editorial note:* in each of the cases above, the counter-prediction concerns what will happen *to* or *through* the agent in ways outside, or not yet within, his practical knowledge (fingers letting go; breaking; the unforeseen manner of Peter's denial). The general contrast from §§2–3 — an expression of intention differs from a prediction not in subject matter but in not being founded on evidence or observation, and in the kind of mistakenness that applies to it — is how [Moran & Stone](../../sources/secondary/moran-stone-2011-expression-of-intention.md) summarise Anscombe's account; no source consulted reports that §52 itself restates it.

## Main claim(s)
- An expression of intention and a contrary expression of belief can in special cases coexist (§52, contents line).
- What another knows will not happen is the very thing I said would happen (§52, p. 92).
- The coexistence is intelligible where the agent lacks knowledge of how the predicted event will come about, or where the event is not his doing (precipice) (§52, per Holton).

## Examples
- The man hanging from a precipice (§52).
- The man facing torture (§52).
- St Peter and the prophesied denial (§52).
- Going for a walk, and the person who knows it will not happen (§52, p. 92).

## Objections and alternatives Anscombe considers
- That "I am not going to" and "I am going to" are straightforward contradictories, so the combination is impossible (§52, contents line).
- That in the expression of intention one is not saying that anything is going to happen. Anscombe rejects this (p. 92): otherwise, having said "I'm just going to get up", one could later dismiss "Why didn't you get up?" as about an irrelevant future happening (quoted by [Moran & Stone](../../sources/secondary/moran-stone-2011-expression-of-intention.md)).

## In the secondary literature
- [Holton 2025](../../sources/secondary/holton-2025-cambridge-lectures.md) — sets out the precipice, torture and St Peter cases; asks what would follow if Peter had known *how*; notes Geach (*Mental Acts*, 1954) also uses Peter and the cock-crow.
- [Setiya 2016](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2016-anscombe-practical-knowledge.md) — the "very same thing" passage (p. 92) is evidence for practical *foreknowledge*: its content is that I will φ, not merely that I am "going to" φ in a failure-tolerant sense (against Thompson); but practical foreknowledge is not required for future intentional action.
- [Small 2020](../../sources/secondary/small-2020-action-practical-knowledge.md) — the walk case shows that what is known is a happening in material reality; others can know the same fact "in belief"; practical reason located in action, not behind it.
- [Millgram 2026](../../sources/secondary/millgram-2026-intention-of-intention.md) — Moore's-paradox analogy: intentions and predictions are not "head-on" contradictories; notes the §22 caveat; reads §§2, 50–52 as the book's frame and links them to indeterminism ("logical freedom" and determinism pass each other by).
- [Schwenkler 2019](../../sources/secondary/schwenkler-2019-guide.md) — Ch. 7.2 draws a parallel with Wittgenstein on Moore's paradox; "factualism": in expressing an intention one says that something will happen (abstract and Pellegrini review).
- [Piñeros Glasscock & Tenenbaum 2023](../../sources/secondary/pineros-glasscock-tenenbaum-2023-sep-action.md) — §§51–52 cited for practical knowledge of future actions, against imperfective-only readings.
- [Setiya 2022 SEP](../../sources/secondary/setiya-2022-sep-intention.md) — §§50–52 (pp. 90–4) as Anscombe's unity of the three heads without positing intention as a mental state.
- [Özaltun 2016](../../sources/secondary/ozaltun-2016-practical-knowledge-section-45.md) — statements of what I did, am doing and will do are all descriptions of what happens (the §2–§45 parallel), compatible with the §52 point.
